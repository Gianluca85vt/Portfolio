Halloween: The Game went wide today, 8 September, on PS5, Xbox Series X|S and
PC. Standard edition €39.99, cross-play on, Deluxe buyers already four days in.
Australia does not get it. The Classification Board refused it outright back in
August, and Refused Classification there means the game cannot be sold, hired,
advertised or legally imported. IllFonic said at the time that despite every
effort it would not be on sale in the country in any form.

The reason is a buff.

## The rule is old and the mechanic walked straight into it

Michael can smoke a joint to gain an advantage. The Classification Board's
position, in its own statement, is that the game contains illicit drug use
linked to an incentive or reward — using the drug confers a gameplay
advantage — and that material of that shape cannot be accommodated inside
R18+ and must be Refused Classification under the Guidelines.

That reading is old. It sits on close to the oldest live tripwire in Australian
classification, and studios have been catching their shins on it for nearly
twenty years. Fallout 3 shipped there
with morphine renamed Med-X. Saints Row IV lost a mission and a weapon.
We Happy Few — a game whose entire premise is a mandatory happiness drug —
was refused in 2018, appealed, and got through only after arguing the drug was
the subject rather than the reward.

The distinction the Board draws is narrow and it is consistent. A drug can be
in your game. Characters can take it. It can be miserable, it can be central,
it can be the whole plot. What it cannot do is make a number go up.

## Why nobody caught it earlier is a pipeline question

Here is the part I keep turning over, because it is the part that costs money.

A drug that grants a buff is not a line of text. Renaming morphine to Med-X
worked for Bethesda because morphine was an item in a list — a string, an icon,
a stat block. You change three fields and the build is compliant. A smoke-for-a-
buff interaction is a different animal: it is an animation set, a state on the
character, a UI element telling you the state is active, audio, probably a
particle, and a hook into whatever system reads "Michael is currently buffed"
when it resolves a chase. Pull it out late and you are not editing a string.
You are removing a node several other systems query.

So the honest options by August were ship without Australia, or unpick a
mechanic across five disciplines weeks from launch and retest the killer's
whole power economy. They took the first. From where I sit that is the
defensible call, and it is also an argument for classification guidelines
sitting in the design document at greenlight rather than arriving with the
lawyers in month thirty.

Australia is not a rounding error, either. It is a mid-sized market that
punches above its size on horror and on asymmetric multiplayer, and a 1v4 game
lives on population.

## Then they cut a second buff

On the morning of 5 September, three days before wide launch and one day after
Deluxe access opened, IllFonic hotfixed out the Two-Person Power-Up. Two
players hiding in a closet together could accept a prompt, mash a button, and
both walk away with a Stronger Together buff. Clips went round showing Dr
Loomis — an adult — running it with a seventeen-year-old character. The fix
landed inside twenty-four hours.

Look at the two removals next to each other. Smoke a joint, get a buff. Hide in
a closet with someone, get a buff. The design leaned on a pattern — a
flavour-flavoured interaction that pays out in stats — and two instances of
that pattern turned out to be unshippable, one to a national regulator and one
to an afternoon on social media. Neither was caught by the other's review.

That is worth saying plainly to anyone building systems with a licence
attached: the pattern is the risk surface, not the individual instance. If your
buff-granting verbs are drawn from adult behaviour because the fiction is adult,
you have a compliance question at the systems level, and it wants asking once
across all of them rather than one at a time after a clip goes viral.

## The scores, and what today changes

The blog looked at this game on Friday, when
[Metacritic had it at 75 and OpenCritic at 56](/blog/halloween-the-game-metacritic-opencritic-split/)
from a pool eight critics deep, and argued that a 1v4 game reviewed against bots
and press lobbies had not assembled itself yet. Three days on, the OpenCritic
top-critic average reads 61 with 22% of critics recommending, which is the small
pool doing exactly what a small pool does. Gamereactor's 6/10 remains the
clearest outlet-side number I can confirm; GameSpace's 80 is the other. I am not
averaging two verdicts and calling it a score.

Steam shows Mostly Positive at 70.9% across 5,111 user reviews as of this
morning. That is a population which did not exist on 3 September, playing the
mode the critics had no way to test, and it is the figure I would watch over
the next fortnight.

And there is a version of this story that is only about the ban, which I think
undersells it. An Australian who wants Halloween cannot buy it, cannot import
it, and has no legal route to a copy — which sits uncomfortably close to
[what a licence actually gets you](/blog/nobody-is-keeping-a-copy-for-you/)
even in the territories where the sale went through.
