I'd take a small town dressed down to the last flowerpot over a big one I can
cross without noticing anything, and Pawbay made that trade on purpose. Whether
it made enough of it is where the reviews part company.

Commando Panda, a small indie team, developed and published it. It came out on
15 May on PlayStation, Xbox, Switch and PC, and reached Steam as *Pawbay: Cat
Chaos* on 21 August. You're a cat. The town is yours to wreck. There's local
split-screen for two, and the price is £17.99 on Xbox.

One outlet I could find scored it: TheXboxHub, three stars out of five, which
is a **6**. Metacritic still shows its Metascore as to be determined, because it
needs four critics and has one. Six more outlets wrote verdicts without a
number, and on a game this small the wording says more than the maths.

## A room and a window

The story takes about as long to describe as it does to play. A kitten wakes up
in a quiet room, runs a short tutorial, and gets out through the window. After
that nothing is waiting for you. Maestros del Mando describes a city with no
final objective, and Virus.hr calls the story a framework that holds the world
together more than an engine that pushes you through it.

That's the correct call for this game. A cat doesn't have a motivation you need
explained, and every cutscene a team this size writes is a week not spent on
the town. The cost comes later. Game Critix, who liked it a lot, still says
some players will want a stronger narrative backbone, and I suspect those
players are the ones who hit the end of the content in an afternoon and found
nothing holding the pieces together.

## Three hours of mischief

The verbs are what you'd hope for. You climb, knock things off shelves,
scratch, steal coin pouches, eat and drink what you shouldn't, and talk to
other cats and people through a wheel of meows. XPN Network calls it "a sweet,
playful sandbox that nails the joy of being a mischievous cat", and reaches
for Untitled Goose Game as the comparison, only softer round the edges.

Two decisions in the structure matter more than the list. Nothing is gated:
you can reach the highest roof in town from the start, with no upgrade standing
between you and it. And the objectives mostly take the form of doing one thing
a set number of times, district after district, which is the repetition
several reviewers name.

TheXboxHub puts completion around three hours and says you can cross the whole
town in a couple of minutes. The same review describes a game that has
promised freedom and then keeps refusing it. You can't climb every box or
canopy, and you can't drop down to that interesting spot below, because the
design has decided it's too far. Its verdict: "if you wish cat sims on the Xbox
would stop blocking you from exploring and, you know, acting like a cat, then
Pawbay might be your saucer of milk. But it comes with its own problems, not
least some control limitations and a tiny game world."

## Built to be knocked over

This is the part I care about, and it's the part the game got most right.

A cat's-eye game changes where the art budget lives. In a third-person game
at human height, the ground is something you glance at while you run. Here the
camera lives thirty centimetres off the cobbles, so the bottom metre of every
building is hero territory. Doorsteps, drainpipes, stall awnings, the crates
outside a shop: all of that is now the foreground, and all of it has to be
modelled, textured and placed as if someone will put their nose on it, because
someone will.

Then every one of those props needs a second job. The awning is a platform.
The drainpipe is a ladder. The shelf of jars is a physics toy, and the gap
between two roofs is a jump somebody had to tune by hand. The Steam page
lists markets, rooftops, homes, alleys and tunnels, and in a game like this each
of those is a traversal route first and a piece of set dressing second. That's
why the town is small. A modular medieval kit of timber frames and tiled roofs
is cheap to build wide. Making every surface of it mean something to an
animal is expensive to build at all.

Maestros del Mando thinks the walled city is the most beautiful thing in the
game, large, colourful and full of people. XPN says every corner reacts to you
in some small way, with doors creaking and townspeople gasping or scolding. Put
those beside TheXboxHub's tiny town and you're reading the same map twice. It's
small, and it's dense, and the density is what the budget bought.

## Where it looks cheap

The art itself splits the reviews, and the split is useful.

Os Garotos de Liverpool praise a charismatic visual style. Jogando Casualmente,
reviewing on Switch, says the graphics look cheap and lack personality, that
the animation is particularly poor and the townspeople's reactions feel
artificial. Both can be true at once, and I think they're describing different
layers of the same frame.

The town can carry charm on colour and shape alone. People can't. A crowd that
reacts is animation work, state by state — startled, chasing, scolding, giving
up — and every state is a clip somebody has to key or capture and blend. A small
team funds the hero first, and in Pawbay the hero is the cat. So the townspeople
get fewer states, the transitions show, and a reviewer watching on a handheld
reads it as cheap. That's what the money bought, and where it didn't reach.

The technical notes line up by platform. XPN reports 4K on Series X|S, quick
loads and smooth running. Seekingtech's first hour on PS5 Pro found foliage
flickering on trees and bushes and awkward pauses in the music. Foliage that
shimmers is very often alpha-tested leaf cards fighting a temporal upscaler; I
can't confirm that's the cause from here, only that it's the usual suspect and
that a patch can fix it.

## Worth an afternoon

Virus.hr's line is the fairest summary I've read: "a charming, chaotic sandbox
with a lot of personality, but also with enough imperfections that we cannot
call it a top-notch achievement." The one number on record is a 6, and it
leaves out the reviews that liked it more.

From my side of the work, the decision I respect is the one the reviewers
complain about. Commando Panda built a town the size it could afford to make
meaningful at cat height, and stopped. A bigger map at the same price would
have been emptier, and nobody would have remembered a single corner of it.

A small world that is densely built beats a large empty one every time. Pawbay
just needed a few more streets of it.
