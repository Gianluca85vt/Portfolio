Rebel Wolves shipped its first game and the critics cannot agree on it. Twelve
scored verdicts average **8**, and they run from a 6 to a 9.5. OpenCritic has
the aggregate at 84 with 89 percent recommending.

Compare that to Onimusha, reviewed the same week: also strong, but with a
two-point spread instead of three and a half. When a game splits critics this
far, the split is usually the most interesting thing about it.

## The clock

</figure>

Coen has thirty in-game days to stop a coronation and save his family. Days and
nights break into eight segments each, and the two halves play differently:
night lets you climb walls and reach places that are otherwise shut to you,
while daylight hands you witchcraft instead. Spend a segment on one quest and
you have not spent it on another.

Reviewers who loved it say the clock finally solves the thing that hollows out
every open world, which is that nothing you choose matters when you can
eventually do all of it. Reviewers who marked it down say you cannot tell which
quests were the important ones until the time is gone.

Same mechanism. One of them enjoyed being made to choose.

## What that costs the people building it

Here is the part nobody outside the job talks about.

A thirty-day limit means the game has to hold more than any single player will
ever see. That is the point. A choice is only a choice if the road you did not
take is actually there, which means somebody modelled it, textured it, lit it,
dressed it and filled it with people, knowing most players would walk past the
door and never open it.

Then double the whole thing. Every space works at night, when the player is
climbing its walls, and again in daylight, when they are not. Two lighting
states. Two sets of readable silhouettes. Two passes of "can you tell where you
are meant to go from here". An environment artist does not get to ship the one
that flatters the set dressing and quietly drop the other.

So I would put money on that being where the technical complaints come from.
The reviews mention uneven combat, awkward blood and health systems, visual and
technical bugs — the rough edges landing on the parts furthest from the core
loop, which is exactly the shape you would predict for a first game from a new
studio built around a structure that multiplies the surface area of everything
in it. Nobody had done this before. It shows, in both directions.

## The number

**8**, from twelve scores between 6 and 9.5.

The mean is 8.04, so the rounding is barely doing any work — this genuinely
sits at an eight. But an average is a poor summary of a game that produced a 6
and a 9.5 in the same week. The distribution says more than the midpoint: most
of the field lands at 8 or above, with a tail of reviewers who found the
friction outweighed the idea.

If the thirty-day clock sounds like a structure you want to be held to, the
high scores are probably closer to your experience. If you would rather see
everything, believe the 6.
