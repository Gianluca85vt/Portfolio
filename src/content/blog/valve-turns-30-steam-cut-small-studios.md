Valve was founded on **24 August 1996**, by Gabe Newell and Mike Harrington,
two Microsoft engineers who left to make games. That's the anniversary being
marked today, and most of the coverage is doing what anniversary coverage
does: Half-Life in 1998, Steam in 2003, Counter-Strike, Portal, thirty years
of "changed PC gaming forever." All true, and I'm not going to relitigate it.

What I want to look at instead is the one number in that history that
actually determines whether a small studio can afford to exist, and how
little it's actually changed in three decades.

## The number everyone remembers is the wrong one

When Valve announced changes to Steam's revenue split in 2018, it made
headlines as "Valve cuts its take." That's technically correct and mostly
irrelevant to anyone reading this. The actual structure is tiered per
title, not per studio: Valve keeps 30% on a game's first $10 million in
lifetime gross, 25% between $10 million and $50 million, and 20% above
that.

Read that again as a small studio or a solo dev, and the reform disappears.
The tiers only start moving once a single game has grossed **ten million
dollars** — not revenue, not budget, gross sales, on Steam specifically,
before refunds and regional pricing eat into it. The overwhelming majority
of games on Steam, including almost everything an environment or technical
artist working solo or in a two-to-five-person team is likely to ship,
never gets near that line. For that project, the split in 2026 is the exact
same 70/30 it was the week Steam opened to third-party publishers in 2005.
Twenty-one years, unchanged, for the tier that actually applies to most of
the platform's catalogue.

## What that cut buys, compared to the alternatives

I'm not writing this to argue 30% is unreasonable in isolation — plenty of
retail and app-store models sit in the same range, and Valve isn't
uniquely greedy here. What's worth knowing is what the alternatives
actually offer, because "just publish somewhere cheaper" gets said a lot
and is rarely followed with numbers.

**itch.io** lets you set your own cut, anywhere from 0% to 100%, with no
platform minimum enforced — the site defaults to suggesting 10% and plenty
of developers pay that or a bit more to keep the platform funded, but you
can legally take everything. **Epic Games Store** runs 88/12 above roughly
$1 million a year in a title's revenue, and takes nothing at all below
that threshold. Both are objectively cheaper than Steam for a small team's
first project.

Neither has Steam's wishlist system, its discovery algorithm, its
existing install base, or the fact that most PC players already have a
Steam library and are far less likely to create a second account for a
platform they've never used. That's the actual trade a small studio is
making when it stays on Steam at 30%: not "Valve is the only option," but
"the traffic is worth more than the eleven-to-eighteen points I'd save
elsewhere" — a bet that gets harder to justify the smaller your marketing
budget already is.

## Why this is worth flagging today specifically

None of this is news, exactly — the tier structure has been public since
2018 and itch.io's model has been public since it launched. What the
anniversary is a good excuse for is saying it plainly, because "Valve
lowered its fees" is the kind of headline that circulates every few years
without the threshold attached, and I've had more than one conversation
with artists assuming it applies to them the moment they self-publish a
small asset pack or a first game on Steam. It doesn't, until the project
is already a commercial outlier.

If you're weighing where to put a small project, weigh the actual numbers
for the size you're actually shipping at, not the headline about the size
Valve renegotiated for. For most of us, that's still 70 cents on the
dollar, same as it's been since Steam stopped being a mandatory patcher
for Half-Life 2 and became a storefront.
