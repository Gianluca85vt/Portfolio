Draw a line on a character's cheekbone. Hold the frame and it reads as ink — a
confident hand-drawn mark on top of a render, exactly the thing everyone has
been chasing since 2018. Now play the shot. The head turns, and the line stays
where you put it, sliding off the face like a smudge on the lens.

That is the whole problem. Everything else in stylised linework is bookkeeping.

A hand-drawn stroke has no idea what it was drawn on. It knows a position in
space and a time, and a face that moves underneath it might as well be weather.
Get one line to hold and you have solved a shot. Get every line on every
character to hold, across a feature, drawn by people rather than generated by a
shader, and you have solved a film.

Sony Pictures Imageworks solved it twice, expensively, and the second solution
was a department.

## What it cost the first time

*Into the Spider-Verse* had a dedicated inking team — people whose job was to
put marks on frames that a renderer had already finished with. Artists who
worked on it have described that as the first they had heard of on a CG animated
feature. Staffing a crew to do it means the studio had already concluded the
look could not be shaded into existence. Somebody had to draw it.

By *Across the Spider-Verse* in 2023 that conclusion had turned into tooling.
The main one is **Kismet**, a linework system built inside Houdini under FX
supervisor Pav Grochola, and the descriptions of it are all about handing
control back: different kinds of line for different worlds, lines that redraw
themselves continuously rather than being locked to one frame, the whole thing
procedural but driven by artists rather than by a solver. Gwen's Earth-65,
Mumbattan and the Vulture each needed their own line behaviour, and one tool had
to make all three without a rewrite.

Around it sat a scattering of smaller things. A curve system in Nuke for
hand-drawn strokes. Hatching tools that ran interactively, in Nuke, at a
responsiveness a compositor would tolerate. Miguel's face got curves drawn with
an early version of the Nuke tool, then rigged to the model and rendered through
Katana — drawn once, bound to a rig, and after that it was the rig's problem
rather than the inker's.

Blender is in there too, which is the part that tends to surprise people.
Imageworks artists took the inklines workflow to the Blender Conference in 2023
and talked through how Blender ended up in the pipeline at all, and how curves
get generated in 3D space so that linework and the 3D render agree with each
other.

## The bit Blender was missing

Grease Pencil could always draw in 3D. What it could not do, for most of its
life, was be *operated on*. A stroke was a stroke. You could parent it, key it,
push it through a modifier or two, and past that you were writing Python.

Blender 4.3, at the end of 2024, changed the category the data belongs to.
Grease Pencil became geometry that Geometry Nodes can read — points with
positions and radii and attributes, evaluated fresh on every frame like any
other node tree output. One line in a release note, and it separates a drawing
that sits in front of a character from a drawing that belongs to one.

Because once the stroke is geometry, sticking it is a graph rather than a
feature request. Sample the nearest point on the deforming surface. Keep what
you found — a face, a coordinate, an offset along the normal. Re-evaluate that
lookup per frame instead of the position you drew at. The line stops living in
world space and starts living on the mesh, and when the head turns the line goes
with it because it was never really at that point in space to begin with.

Derek Flood published a walkthrough of exactly this last week, using animation by
Tiffany Huang in the painterly register of Gwen's world. One person, no
proprietary anything.

## Why binding a mark is never free

Here is where I would slow down before promising anyone a Spider-Verse look by
Friday. The moment you attach a 2D mark to a 3D surface, you inherit every
problem that surface has.

Surfaces stretch. A line bound across a cheek and a jaw will get dragged when
the jaw opens, and ink that stretches stops reading as ink — the weight goes
wrong, and line weight is most of what makes a mark look drawn. Surfaces also
turn away. A stroke on the far side of a head is still perfectly valid geometry;
it just should not be visible, and nothing about sampling a mesh tells you that.
Occlusion has to be handled on purpose or the character wears their linework
like a net.

And every one of these has a threshold where the fix starts fighting the look.
Too little correction and lines swim. Too much and they lock down so hard they
read as a shader, at which point you have spent a week of node work arriving at
an outline modifier.

This is the same trade that shows up any time a drawing has to survive a camera.
Projecting [a 2D concept onto a ZBrush
sculpt](/blog/punk-knight-zbrush-2d-illustrated-look/) gives you something that
looks hand-drawn from one angle and comes apart the instant you orbit it, which
is fine when the deliverable is a turntable-free hero image and fatal when it
moves. Tools like [PaintBridge projecting paint back onto Blender
geometry](/blog/paintbridge-krita-blender-projection-painting/) live in the same
territory — the projection is the easy half, and everything that happens after
the camera moves is the work.

## What a tutorial does not hand you

Imageworks had a linework department, a tool with a name and a supervisor, and
several years across two films to find out where it broke. A node graph you
built on a Sunday gives you the mechanism. It does not give you the taste calls
that mechanism was built to serve: which lines are drawn once and inherited,
which are redrawn every frame on purpose because the flicker is the charm, how
much a line is allowed to stretch before an artist would have redrawn it by
hand.

Those calls are why the films look the way they do, and they are not in any node
tree. What is genuinely new is that the price of asking the question has
collapsed. Getting an ink line to hold on a moving face used to require a studio
willing to fund the attempt. Now it requires an evening and a mesh with decent
topology.
