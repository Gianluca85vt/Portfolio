CAPTAIN TSUBASA 2: WORLD FIGHTERS is out today, 28 August 2026, on PS5, Xbox
Series X|S, PC and Switch, at €59.99 on Steam. Tamsoft developed it, Bandai
Namco published it, and the verdicts landed a day or two ahead of launch.

Five of them, converted to a ten-point scale and averaged, come out at 7.82,
which rounds to **8**.

- PlayStation LifeStyle — 9
- The Games Machine — 8
- GamersRD — 7.6 (76/100)
- TwistedVoxel — 7.5
- BaziCenter — 7

The aggregators counted more and disagree slightly with each other: OpenCritic
had 78 from twenty critics this morning, Metacritic 76 from fourteen. Both sit
inside the same band, so the picture is stable even if the decimal places are
not. What moves between the 9 and the 7 is worth pulling apart, because the
reviewers are arguing about a rendering decision without quite saying so.

## The problem nobody else in football games has

A football sim wants to be continuous. Twenty-two bodies, one camera, ninety
minutes. The art of it is never cutting away. Broadcast presentation exists to
hide the seams that are already there.

Captain Tsubasa is adapted from a manga that works the opposite way. A single
shot on goal takes pages. The pitch stretches to whatever length the panel
needs, the ball leaves a trail you could see from orbit, and the goalkeeper gets
an interior monologue on the way down. The source material's entire visual
language is the interruption.

So Tamsoft builds a football game that is, underneath, a fighting game. Contests
resolve into authored cinematic sequences. The tactical camera is abandoned
mid-play, a bespoke set piece runs, and then the simulation has to be picked
back up — ball position, player positions, momentum, score state — as though
nothing had happened.

That handoff is the expensive part. Every one of those transitions is a place
where the game either feels like one continuous thing or feels like a slideshow
with football in the gaps.

## Which is exactly what the scores are arguing about

Read the five write-ups next to each other and the split is clean. The 9 is
delighted by the spectacle. The 7.5 is titled, more or less, all flash and
little flow. Nobody disputes that the super-shots look tremendous. They disagree
about what the game costs you between them.

Pacing is what breaks first here, and it is the single hardest thing about this
format. You have built the most exciting three seconds in sports games. Now you
have to make the ninety minutes around them survive being repeatedly stopped.

## The parry is the interesting bit of engineering

Tamsoft's answer this time is to make the interruption itself playable. The
sequel adds a parry and something called the Chain System, and reviewers keep
describing them in the language of fighting games rather than sports games —
reads, counters, contests that resolve over several exchanges.

Turn the cutscene into a contest and it stops being a payout. That is a
genuinely smart fix. If the player has input during the set piece, the set piece
is no longer dead time, and the transition back to the pitch reads as a
consequence rather than a resume.

Whether it works well enough is where the review spread comes from. The people
still marking it down are, I think, describing sequences they have now watched
forty times, which is the thing canned animation always eventually becomes,
however good the animation is.

## The art side

Bandai Namco's own figures for the game are 22 national teams, more than 110
playable characters and over 150 new moves. Take that seriously as a production
number. Every one of those characters has to hold a 1980s manga line under a
modern renderer, at whatever distance the tactical camera sits, and then hold up
again in close-up during a super-shot where the camera is a metre from their
face.

Cel shading is unforgiving that way. A physically based character can be a bit
wrong and read as lighting. A flat-shaded one is either on-model or it is not,
and the audience for this game grew up with the model. Anyone who has fought a
toon shader through a camera range knows the two ends of it want different
things: readable silhouettes far out, and enough interior detail up close that
the face does not go to poster paint.

150 new moves is also 150 pieces of bespoke animation and effects work that most
players will see in a handful of matches, which tells you where the budget went.

## Worth noting for anyone about to buy

The recurring criticism outside the pacing is that the game makes few
concessions to newcomers, and that the multiplayer offering is thinner than the
single-player campaign. The campaign covers the World Youth arc, which is a
reasonable entry point if you have never read a page of the manga and a
straightforward nostalgia purchase if, like most of Italy, you grew up watching
it under a different name.

An 8 is about right for something this specific. It is very good at the thing it
is, and the thing it is remains strange.
