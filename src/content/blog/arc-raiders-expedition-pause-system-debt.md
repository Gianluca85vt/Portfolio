Embark Studios confirmed this week that **Arc Raiders'** fifth Expedition,
opening 8 September and running through 29 September, will be the last one
players see for a while. After that, the seasonal wipe system that has
defined the game's live-service rhythm since launch goes on hold until
**early 2027**, while the studio rebuilds it from the ground up.

That much was reported everywhere. The part worth sitting with is what
Embark's design director said about *why*.

## What Expeditions are, briefly

  <figcaption>Embark Studios, via the official Arc Raiders Steam page</figcaption>
</figure>

An Expedition is Arc Raiders' opt-in prestige system: players who join one
reset their Raider's level and clear their stash, in exchange for permanent
account-wide upgrades, extra skill points and expanded stash space. Progress
made toward an in-progress Expedition converts to vouchers if you don't
finish in time, so a wipe doesn't erase everything — but it is still,
functionally, deleting your character to get stronger ones later.

It has been one of the most argued-about systems in the game since it
shipped. The community's complaints were consistent across three prior
rounds: departure windows too tight to realistically hit, a reward
structure that leaned on stash value rather than anything resembling skill,
and a barrier to entry steep enough that newer players felt locked out of
even trying. Embark answered piecemeal each time — the entry cost dropped
from 5 million coins to 3 million, the last Expedition moved from a
two-week to a three-week window, this final one before the pause adds an
opt-in quest reset and drops the "Last Call" completion requirement
entirely.

None of those patches solved it. That's the point.

## The quote that actually explains the pause

Talking to PC Gamer about the rework, Arc Raiders' design director said
the current system exists the way it does because of time pressure, not
design intent:

> "Because of other things that were actively in development, we didn't
> have time to shift the system to what we intend to do next. We want to
> tie more game loops into it, and not just make it about currency value."

That's a studio admitting, on the record, that a core progression system
shipped as the version they had time for, not the version they wanted, and
that they knew the gap existed the whole time they kept shipping content
on top of it.

## Why a live-service team lets that happen

This is a familiar shape to anyone who has worked inside a live-service
production schedule, and it isn't really about Arc Raiders specifically.
Systems and content compete for the same engineers and designers on a
release cadence that doesn't pause for either. When a system launches
half-built because "other things were actively in development," the studio
is making a bet: ship the placeholder now, and find time to properly build
the real version later, once the immediate pressure eases.

The bet usually loses, because later never arrives on its own. Every new
season is itself the next urgent thing, and a system that technically
works — even if the community hates it — doesn't get to jump the queue
against a system that's actually broken. Three Expeditions and three rounds
of surface-level tuning is what that looks like from the outside: real
fixes, none of them the fix, because the team never had the runway to stop
and rebuild the foundation rather than patch what's sitting on top of it.

What makes this case notable is that Embark is choosing to stop rather
than keep patching. Pausing new Expeditions for roughly five months is an
explicit admission that iterative tuning has hit its ceiling, and that the
only way out is time nobody was going to hand them voluntarily — so they're
taking it, at the cost of a live-service cadence they'd otherwise want to
protect. That's a harder call to make than it looks from a press release,
because "we're pausing a feature players engage with" is not a sentence a
live-service roadmap wants to contain, ever.

## What to watch for in early 2027

The design director's own language — "tie more game loops into it, and not
just make it about currency value" — suggests Expeditions return as
something closer to a genuine prestige system tied to how you played,
rather than what you'd hoarded. That's a bigger rebuild than a numbers
pass: new UI to communicate what carries over, new reward logic to test
against exploits the old currency-based version never had to worry about,
and a new onboarding path so newer players don't bounce off it the way
they have three times already.

Five months is not a long runway for that scope of rework in a live game
still shipping regular content around it. Whether Arc Raiders comes back
with the system Embark actually wanted to build, or another version of
what it had time for, is the thing worth checking on when Expeditions
return.
