Hellraiser. Stuntman. Turok. Jurassic Park. John Wick. Space Marine 3.

That is the list Saber Interactive's chief creative officer read out when
somebody asked whether generative AI was in the company's games, and every
single name on it belongs to somebody else.

Tim Willits — who ran design at id Software for two decades before this job —
put it flatly in press interviews reported the first week of September 2026:
"There is absolutely no generative AI in Space Marine 3 or Hellraiser or
Stuntman or Turok or Jurassic Park, or Wick." Six titles, one sentence, no
hedging. For a statement issued into an argument this hot, it is unusually
specific about scope.

It is also, read carefully, a statement about licensing.

## The company saying this is not anti-AI

Saber's chief executive, Matthew Karch, has been one of the louder pro-AI voices
among mid-size publishers. That is the context the quote landed in, and it is
why people did not simply take the assurance and move on.

Earlier this year a writer, Stella Sacco, said publicly that she had been
replaced on a small Saber project — Rideshare Stimulator — by AI writing tools.
Karch responded by insulting her in public, then apologised privately. Saber
first denied that writers had been replaced, and later acknowledged that AI had
been used in that game's development. Willits, by the reporting, is no more
opposed to the technology than Karch is; both have described the smaller
in-house projects as a place to experiment.

So the company is not drawing an ethical line. It is drawing a line around the
titles it does not own.

## What a licensor is actually protecting

Games Workshop settled its position in January 2026, alongside its half-year
results. The internal policy bans AI-generated content and bars AI from its
design processes outright, and the Golden Demon painting competition rules were
updated to ban it in entries too. Chief executive Kevin Rountree's summary was
that some senior managers had been experimenting and "none are excited about
it". The company said it would go on hiring concept artists, writers and
sculptors.

The public framing was about respecting human creators. There is a second reason
sitting underneath it, and it is the one with teeth.

A licensor's entire business is owning artwork it can defend. In the United
States the Copyright Office has held for several years now that material without
human authorship cannot be registered — a generated image has no clean owner and
no clear chain of title. Put one into a Warhammer product and Games Workshop has
a Space Marine it may not be able to stop anybody else from using. For a company
whose miniatures, logos and chapter heraldry are the asset, that is a hole in the
balance sheet rather than a philosophical problem.

Which means the protection working artists currently enjoy on these projects
comes from intellectual property law, routed through a licensor's legal
department. Not from their employer. Not from a union. It holds precisely as far
as the licence does, and Rideshare Stimulator is what the other side of that
boundary looks like.

## Why 40K resists generation anyway, on the art side

Set the contracts aside for a second, because there is a production argument too.

Warhammer's visual language is codified to a degree that very few properties
match. The proportion of a pauldron against a torso, which skull goes on which
piece of trim, the specific way a chapter's heraldry is quartered, how much
filigree a given order gets before it stops being that order — all of it is
written down, and all of it goes through approval passes with the licensor.
Artists working on 40K spend a lot of their time being told a thing is nearly
right.

Nearly right is exactly what a diffusion model is good at. Feed it enough
Warhammer and it will return something that reads as Warhammer at a glance and
falls apart under the approval pass: an Ultramarines badge with the wrong number
of edges, a bolter with a magazine that could not seat, trim that is ornate in
the general direction of Gothic without being any specific order's ornament. The
model has learned the vibe of the IP. The vibe is not the thing being licensed.

You can see the cost of getting this wrong in the direction the accusations
travel. When [a trailer made by a crew of 150 got called AI anyway](/blog/humankind-2-trailer-ai-accusation-150-crew/),
the studio had no cheap way to prove otherwise — suspicion now attaches to
finished work on the strength of how it looks. A licensor watching that happen to
somebody else's game has every commercial reason to make the ban explicit and
put it in writing.

## The pattern is getting easier to read

Rockstar is doing something structurally similar at a much larger scale. The
company [hand-authored more than 600,000 animations for GTA 6 and then said
generative AI had no hand in it](/blog/gta-6-600000-animations-no-generative-ai/) —
a payroll decision first, a marketing line second, and impossible to fake after
the fact. The studios with the most to lose from a provenance question are the
ones ruling generation out loudest.

I would still hold Willits' sentence to what it says. It covers six named
titles. It does not say what tools sat in the pipeline around them, it does not
cover Saber's unlicensed work, and it was given in interviews rather than issued
as a contractual statement by Focus or Games Workshop. That is a good deal firmer
than most assurances of this kind, and it is not the same as an audit.

What it does tell you, if you are an artist choosing where to send a portfolio,
is that the safest rooms right now are the ones with a licensor standing outside
them.
