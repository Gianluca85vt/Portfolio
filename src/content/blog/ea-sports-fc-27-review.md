The verdicts started landing on 17 September, a week ahead of the game going
on sale — Steam's page says 24 September, EA's own materials say the 25th, and
the two have not been reconciled anywhere I can find. Six of those verdicts
carry a number I could convert and check. The mean is **7.5**. The range runs
from 6 to 8.5.

That is a two-and-a-half point spread on a football game, which is not how
this series usually reviews. FC scores cluster. Everybody knows what the thing
is, everybody has played fifteen of them, and the annual verdict tends to be a
half-point wobble around eight. A spread this wide means the reviewers are scoring
different things. What divides them is whether The Grounds counts.

The aggregators have not settled either. OpenCritic sits at 81 with 67 percent
of critics recommending; a German round-up on 19 September had Metacritic at an
early 74 off a handful of reviews. Seven points between two aggregators on the
same week is a sample-size artefact, and both will move.

One verdict I left out of the arithmetic. Radio Times ran a review headlined "a
new low", reported in a German round-up at one star — but I could not open the
outlet's own page to confirm the star count, and a 2.0 dropped into a six-item
set moves the average by most of a point. It exists, it is the angriest thing
written about this game, and its complaint is the £139.99 Ultimate Plus
pre-order edition rather than anything on the pitch.

## Where the year went

The Grounds is an open, persistent social space you walk around in. Minigames,
small-sided matches, other players milling about. For a football game that is a second genre bolted to
the side of the first, and the engineering distance between the two is larger
than the marketing makes it sound.

A stadium is the easiest large environment in games. It is a closed box. The
camera lives on a rail somewhere above the halfway line, so you know the frustum
before you build anything; the lighting is baked or driven by a handful of
authored presets; the crowd is instanced cards with a few thousand variations
and nobody ever gets close enough to care; the playable surface is one material
on one plane, 105 by 68 metres, and the player never leaves it. Twenty-two
characters, one ball, a known camera. You can spend the entire budget on the
things the broadcast camera actually sees.

An open hub throws all of that out. Now you are streaming, because the player
can walk somewhere. You need traversal animation for a body rig built to
sprint, turn and strike a ball, not to amble past a kebab van. Time of day has
to move or the space feels like a photograph. And the netcode has to hold an
indeterminate number of players in one shared area rather than twenty-two in a
deterministic match sim, which is a different problem wearing the same hat.

Do that inside a twelve-month annual cycle and something else gets less. Which
is, more or less, what the reviews say: the football is better in specific,
noticeable ways, and it is better by increments.

## The increments are animation work

The one change nearly every reviewer names is that the ball stops behaving like
it is magnetised. Eurogamer's German edition put it plainly — the ball sticks
less to the feet, and a player's actual attributes matter more. Defending
relies less on AI assistance. Attacking runs and corners want more input.

Loosening ball-to-foot attachment reads on a feature list as a tuning value.
Most of its cost lands on the animation set. The old behaviour — ball snapped to a socket,
contact resolved by fiat — is doing enormous work hiding failure. Once you let
the ball live in physics between touches, the player can reach for it and miss,
arrive a frame late, get a shin instead of a laces. Every one of those is a
state the animation set now has to cover, or it reads as foot sliding and
clipping, which is the thing players screenshot. The reaching, stretching,
off-balance contacts are the expensive part.

That is real craft, and it is invisible in a trailer. It is also exactly the
kind of work that does not move a score, because a reviewer who gets it right
just says the game "feels better".

## The complaint that never changes

Microtransactions in The Grounds, pay-to-win in Ultimate Team, live-service
progression sitting across the modes people actually play. Every review in the
set above raises it somewhere, and the ones at the bottom of the range raise it
first.

We have been here twice in three weeks. EA's other annual sport landed on a
7 when [reviewers went after NHL 27's generative-AI
commentary](/blog/nhl-27-review), a complaint about a shipped feature nobody
asked for. And 2K, running the same annual treadmill, got an 8.5 because
[NBA 2K27 put its year into simulation fidelity](/blog/nba-2k27-review) rather
than a new place to stand around in. Three annualised sports sims, three very
different answers to the same question: what do you build when you cannot
rebuild the sport?

FC 27's answer was a town square. The scores suggest a third of the people
reviewing it would have preferred another year on the grass.
