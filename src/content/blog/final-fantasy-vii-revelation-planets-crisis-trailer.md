Square Enix brought the third and final part of the Final Fantasy VII remake
trilogy to Opening Night Live last night, with director Naoki Hamaguchi
presenting. Spring 2027, on PS5, Xbox Series X, Switch 2 and PC.

The trailer is called The Planet's Crisis and it is doing a lot of work in three
and a half minutes: the Weapons awake, Typhon as a new summon, Vincent Valentine
and Cid Highwind finally playable, a costume system called FITS that hands
characters abilities borrowed from the old job classes — Cloud as a Warrior,
Tifa as a Black Mage.

Everyone is going to write about the Weapons. I want to talk about the
parachute.

## Three new verbs

Buried in the same trailer, next to the enormous monsters, are three additions
to how you get around: a parachute, a grappling hook, and Chocobo mounts across
open terrain.

Rebirth already had Chocobos, so that one is an extension. The other two are
new, and they change the shape of the world in a way that lands almost entirely
on the environment team.

Here is the thing about a parachute. The moment you give a player one, they are
above the level. Not on a scripted flight path with the camera pointed where you
want it — above, drifting, looking down and sideways at their own pace.

## What players see from up there

An open world built for a person on the ground is, structurally, a set. Not
dishonestly — it's just that budget goes where eyes go, and eyes go forward and
slightly down.

So rooftops get simplified, because you're never on one. The far side of a ridge
carries lower-resolution terrain. Buildings that are only ever seen from the
street get their backs turned into flat planes. Vegetation is placed to read
from eye level, which means the cards that make up a tree canopy are angled for
someone underneath them.

Give that world a parachute and every one of those decisions is now on screen.
The rooftops need to exist. The tree canopies need to work from above, where
those angled cards go paper-thin. LOD transitions that were tuned for a horse
now happen while the player is descending toward them, which is the worst
possible case — you're closing distance fast, in a straight line, with nothing
else to look at.

A grappling hook does the same thing at a smaller scale and higher frequency. It
puts players on ledges, on walls, on the tops of things, in places a designer
did not choose for them.

None of this is a criticism. It's the cost of the feature, and it's a cost
Square Enix has clearly decided to pay, which is itself the interesting signal:
you don't add aerial traversal late. That decision was made early enough for the
world to be built around it.

## The Weapons are an easier problem than they look

Everyone reads a colossal monster as a technical showpiece. Sometimes it is. But
scale in a game world is mostly a compositional trick, not a rendering one.

You sell size by denying the player a clean look. You frame the thing against
something whose size they already know, you keep the camera low, you let it
break the horizon line, you never show it fully in one shot. Shadow does more
work than polygon count — a shape that blocks the sky reads as vast whether it
has ten million triangles or one.

The genuinely hard version of that problem is when the enormous thing has to
share screen space with a world the player can walk through, at a distance where
both need to be correct at once. Judging from the trailer, that's what the
Weapons are for. We'll see how much of the encounter is arena and how much is
the actual open world.

## FITS, and the cost of outfits

The costume system is the sleeper item on the list.

An outfit that grants abilities is a design feature. An outfit is also, in
production terms, a full character variant: its own mesh, its own materials, its
own cloth simulation setup, its own weight painting, and its own set of
interactions with every animation the character already has. Multiply by the
party. Multiply again by however many FITS exist.

That is a real line in a budget, and studios do not spend it for a cosmetic.
Which suggests FITS is closer to the centre of the combat system than a costume
menu implies.
