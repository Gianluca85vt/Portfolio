Alessandro Carloni handed Cartoon Brew the sentence that explains this film's
entire art department. The team, he said, didn't want to use CG to just
"simulate" an illustration. That is close to the hardest brief you can give a
CG pipeline, and on the evidence of the reviews they met it while the film
built around them came up short.

Six scored verdicts I could verify average **5.8** on a ten-point scale, which
rounds to 6. The range runs 5 to 7 — JoBlo at the top, 411Mania and Next Best
Picture at the bottom. A narrow spread, and inside it the critics are not
arguing with each other about the animation at all.

## Three films in one pipeline

The look was split three ways, and the production has been open about it. The
Cat's own Seuss World is bold, graphic and minimal. The human world was pitched
to Animation World Network as an elevated version of classic 3D. The Worlds of
the Boxes — where a moving box becomes a doorway — marry a graphic 2D treatment
to real 3D depth.

Read that as a schedule rather than a mood board and it gets expensive fast.
Three looks means three shader libraries, three lighting setups with three sets
of rules about how light is allowed to behave, and a decision for every asset
that crosses a boundary about which version of itself it is wearing. A
character who walks from the human world into a box world is two look-dev
problems with one rig underneath.

Warner Bros. Pictures Animation is a relaunched division and this is its first
full-length feature. Picking a three-system look for film number one is either
confidence or somebody not yet knowing what that costs. I lean towards
confidence, because the thing nobody is criticising is the execution.

## A drawing has no load path

Production designer Aidan Sugano drew the film by hand, and those designs were
translated into 3D models in Maya — characters, environments, props, the lot.
Seuss motifs were kept deliberately: hatch marks, negative space, the arches,
the mechanical hands.

That hatch-mark decision is the one I would want to see the notes on. A hatch
mark is a mark made at a fixed size on a fixed page. The moment it sits on a
surface in a 3D scene, somebody has to decide whether it lives in world space,
where it scales with the object and dissolves into noise at distance, or in
screen space, where it holds its weight and slides across the geometry whenever
the camera moves. Neither is free. Both have a failure mode an audience notices
without being able to name it.

Seuss architecture has the same problem one layer up. He drew buildings that
lean, cantilever and taper to a point with nothing holding them down. There is
no load path anywhere in his work, which is fine on paper and awkward the
instant you add a camera that can orbit. Something leaning at that angle either
has to look like it is about to fall, or the film has to establish early that
gravity here is a suggestion.

This blog looked at the opposite solution a fortnight ago, when Gameloft
Brisbane [built Bluey's cast as flat camera-facing planes on a 3D
skeleton](/blog/bluey-happy-snaps-2d-planes-3d-skeleton/) rather than trying to
model the drawing at all. Two answers to the same question. Keep the drawing
and fake the dimension, or build the dimension and fight to keep the drawing.
Warner took the harder one.

## What the numbers say, and how much they are moving

The aggregates are unkind and unsettled. When I checked on 9 November,
Metacritic showed 46 from 16 critics and Rotten Tomatoes 49 percent; a snapshot
from opening day had Rotten Tomatoes nearer 45 percent across 24 reviews. The
audience number sits higher than the critics' at 59 percent, which is the usual
shape for a family film. ScreenRant's comparison is the one Warner will take:
the new film's Rotten Tomatoes score is roughly five times the 2003 Mike Myers
live-action version's.

Several of the outlets I scored say some version of the same thing. Next Best
Picture calls the animation excellent and the story overstuffed. Roger Ebert's
review describes the visuals as stunning and the film as overstimulating.
Silver Screen Riot rates the style a boon and then says the film does nothing
with it beyond overpopulating the frame. JoBlo, the most positive of the six,
still flags a dragging second act.

A consensus that praises the craft and rejects the script is a specific kind of
bad news for an art department, because the part that took two years is the
part nobody is arguing about and the part that gets blamed is cheaper to fix.

## $20 million, and where the work was done

The film opened in the United States on 6 November 2026 and took about $20
million from 4,003 locations on its first weekend, per Variety's box office
report. It lost the top spot to Godzilla Minus Zero, which pulled $31.6 million
from 3,254 theatres — a foreign-language film outgrossing a Dr. Seuss adaptation
on 750 fewer screens. I [scored Minus Zero at 7 last week on a four-point
spread](/blog/godzilla-minus-zero-review/), and the two films together make a
reasonable argument that 2026 audiences will turn out for a strong look wherever
it comes from.

Reporting around the release put Warner's hopes against Illumination's 2018
Grinch, which cleared $500 million worldwide. The release date had already moved
once — Cartoon Brew had the film dated 6 March 2026 before Warner pushed it to
November 2026, a shift Animation Magazine and Deadline both covered at the time.

The work itself was spread across teams in India, Montreal, London and Los
Angeles, with Autodesk Flow Production Tracking following every character,
environment, prop and version from boards through to final compositing over
roughly two years. Credit for the animation is where my sourcing gets soft:
Wikipedia names DNEG Animation, while AWN's interview coverage credits Leo
Sanchez Studio on character development with Olivier Staphylas leading the
animation effort. Those are not necessarily in conflict — a character design
house and a vendor doing shot animation are different jobs — but I could not
confirm the split from a primary credit list, so treat it as unresolved.

Four territories, two years and three visual systems, to arrive at a 6. The
pipeline did its job.
