Three things came out of Ubisoft on **19 November 2026**. All of them inside about
six hours.

The company confirmed **166 job cuts** at Ubisoft Montreal and Ubisoft Quebec.
It confirmed that two of its five Creative Houses have been merged into one. And
it confirmed, after months of reporting, that it has founded a studio dedicated
to generative AI in games.

Those are three separate announcements. I am not going to pretend one caused
another. Nothing published says so, and the arithmetic of a 166-person cut does
not map onto a new studio of unknown size. What I will say is that on a
single Thursday Ubisoft described where it is building new structure and where it
has decided structure is no longer warranted, and the second of those two
messages went to named people at the two studios that hold its biggest
franchises.

## Which studios, and what they were holding

Ubisoft Montreal and Ubisoft Quebec are not satellite offices. Montreal has
carried Assassin's Creed for most of its existence. It is on Codename Hexe now.
Quebec shipped Assassin's Creed Shadows, and according to GameSpot's reporting no
further updates are coming to that game, which leaves an open question about what
that team moves onto. Far Cry sits in the same complex. Between them, these are the teams that built the
tooling every Ubisoft open world runs through.

The company's line, quoted identically by Eurogamer and GamesIndustry.biz, is
that the cuts are needed "to ensure our resources are aligned with current
production priorities and business needs." IGN reported — and GameSpot relayed —
that the affected teams were working on projects that have been "canceled or
refocused." Push Square put the figure at roughly four per cent of Ubisoft's
Canadian workforce.

Four per cent is how it reads on a payroll. A pipeline does not lose people by
percentage.

An Assassin's Creed is a content factory, and the thing that makes a content
factory work is not a document. It is three hundred people who all agree, without
discussing it, on how an asset is named, which LOD budget a façade gets, how the
streaming grid is carved up, and which of the four tools that can place a prop is
the one you actually use for a roof. That consensus took fifteen years to form in
Montreal, and it lives in the hands of people who have been there long enough to
have opinions about it. A layoff aimed at "projects that have been canceled or
refocused" selects for exactly the seniority that holds it —
because the senior people are the ones who get moved onto the speculative thing
that then gets cancelled.

The loss shows up two years later. An open world takes longer to populate than
the last one did, and nobody can quite explain why.

## The reorganisation is eight months old and already smaller

Ubisoft announced the Creative House structure in **January 2026**: five
semi-autonomous houses, each overseeing specific franchises, intended to begin
operating from April. The pitch was that a publisher this size runs better as a
federation of focused teams than as one enormous studio network.

Two of the five have now been merged. Eight months in.

I would read that as the more informative of the day's three announcements.
A reorganisation that reduces its own unit count that fast is telling you the
unit count was a plan rather than a finding — and every one of those merges
redraws reporting lines, which for an artist means a new producer, a new set of
review gates, and a quarter spent re-learning whose sign-off a scene needs. The
Vantage Studios subsidiary sitting alongside this is backed by a Tencent
investment that reports put somewhere between €1.16 billion and $1.3 billion;
the figures genuinely do not agree across sources, which is worth knowing before
anyone quotes one of them at you.

Ubisoft also said this week that Assassin's Creed Jade, the mobile entry, is
still in development while its focus remains console and PC, and it credited
strong Assassin's Creed sales partly to Black Flag Resynced and to a bump in
Odyssey sales it attributes to Christopher Nolan's Odyssey. In October it said no
further Assassin's Creed remakes are in development. The portfolio is being
tidied in public.

## The AI studio, said plainly

Video Games Chronicle reported on the same day that Ubisoft has founded a studio
dedicated to generative AI in games, confirming earlier reports. Ubisoft has been
running public generative-AI experiments for a while, so this is a change of
status rather than a change of direction — the work graduates from an experiment
inside the company to a company inside the company.

What a dedicated subsidiary buys you is a budget line and a headcount that does
not compete with a game's own. That is a real commitment. It is the kind a company
makes once it has decided a technology is load-bearing rather than exploratory. We have watched this exact move elsewhere
this month: [Pearl Abyss now says AI runs through its entire pipeline, eight
months after apologising for AI props shipping in Crimson
Desert](/blog/pearl-abyss-ai-pipeline-dokev-2028/). The pattern there was an
apology followed by expansion, and nobody involved treated those two things as
contradictory.

I have no idea what that studio will ship. Anybody telling you it exists to
replace the 166 is guessing. The thing I would watch for is narrower: whether the tools it produces land in the hands of the production
artists who are still there, or whether they stay a research output that gets
shown at investor days. Those are very different outcomes for the same press
release.

## The day it landed on

GTA 6 came out on 19 November 2026. Every games outlet on earth had its front
page committed, its reviewers exhausted, and no spare column for a Thursday
afternoon layoff notice from a French publisher. I do not think that timing was
chosen — Ubisoft's internal calendar is not built around Rockstar's — but the
effect is the same either way, and it is why the number is worth repeating rather
than filing.

It is also a sharp contrast to read in one week. Rockstar's answer to the
problem of filling a modern open world was to put [a dedicated team on NPC
animation until GTA 6 carried over 600,000 of them, roughly eleven times GTA 5's
count](/blog/gta-6-600000-npc-animations/) — a headcount answer to a content
problem, and an expensive one. Ubisoft's open worlds have the same content
problem, at a comparable scale, and this week its answer to the headcount
question went the other way.

Both companies are solving for the cost of hand-made volume. One of them bought
more hands.
