CD PROJEKT RED made Night City. Tens of thousands of buildings, adverts, awnings
and bits of pavement litter, a lot of it placed by hand. So when someone at that
studio calls one object **one of the most expensive art assets ever** made
there, it is worth asking what an object has to be to earn that.

The object is the Chimera, the spider tank V and President Myers fight in the
bowels of a Barghest facility early in Phantom Liberty. Game director Gabe
Amatangelo, creative director Igor Sarzyński and art director Paweł Mielniczuk
went through its development on the studio's own AnsweRED podcast, and the
figure that travelled everywhere on 28 September 2026 was this: roughly two
years, and four rebuilds. The studio's phrase for it was iterating over and
over. The two-year estimate is attributed to Mielniczuk.

The rebuilds were driven by the tank's equipment and weapons changing.

That sentence is short and it is doing more work than it looks like it is doing.

## Why a boss costs more than a district

An environment artist builds to be reused. A kit of wall panels, a set of
railings, a family of vending machines — every piece is made once and dressed a
hundred times, and the cost per appearance falls the whole way through
production. That economy is the only reason an open world is affordable at all.
CD PROJEKT RED pushed it hard enough that the studio's environment art director
says [Night City runs at twice The Witcher 3's texel
density](/blog/night-city-hand-built-texel-density/) — the budget went into
resolution on surfaces the player stands two feet from, because first person
puts you there.

A boss inverts every part of that. The Chimera appears once. It is never
re-dressed, never instanced across a district, never seen again from a different
angle in a later mission. Whatever it costs, it costs for one fight.

Getting a spider tank through modelling is a hard week for a good hard-surface
artist. The two years live in everything hanging off the model. A walker of that size needs a
rig with a leg IK setup that holds up on uneven floor, animation for every
weapon it carries, destruction states for the parts the player is meant to shoot
off, VFX per weapon, audio per weapon, collision that does not let the player
stand somewhere the attacks cannot reach, and navigation that keeps a very large
thing from getting stuck on a very small thing.

Change the weapons and you change all of it.

That is what four rebuilds means: four passes down a chain where each
discipline waits on the one upstream, and where the designers
asking for a different gun are asking, without meaning to, for a different
animation set, a different VFX pass, a different mix, and a fresh round of
encounter balancing to find out whether the new thing is still frightening.

## Telegraphing to a camera with no body in it

Amatangelo described the Chimera as a nexus point — gameplay, narrative, art,
worldbuilding, all meeting on one encounter. The art half of that is harder in
Cyberpunk than it would be in most games with bosses, for a reason that has
nothing to do with fidelity.

Cyberpunk is first person. The player cannot see V. There is no third-person
silhouette to read distance against, no camera pulling back to frame the arena,
no dodge animation the player watches themselves perform. Everything the player
knows about an incoming attack has to arrive through the boss itself: a turret
rotating, a leg planting, a light changing colour, a sound with enough
directional information to mean something in a concrete room.

So the tank's read is the fight's interface. Move a weapon to a different hardpoint and the tell moves with it,
which means the animation that sells the wind-up has to be re-authored, and the
timing that gives the player their window has to be re-tuned against it. A
third-person game absorbs that change more cheaply, because the camera is doing
some of the communicating. Here the art is doing all of it.

## The thing Cyberpunk was never built to do

There is context behind the two years that nobody on the podcast had to say out
loud. Cyberpunk 2077's original bosses were the weakest part of it. Adam
Smasher, Oda, Sasquatch — encounters that asked a sandbox shooter-RPG to behave
like a game with arenas, and got bullet sponges instead. The systems underneath
were built for a city where the player picks their own approach, and a boss is
the one moment where the game takes that choice back.

Phantom Liberty opened with one anyway, and spent its most expensive asset on
the thing you meet on the way in.

Two years of rebuilding starts to look like a studio finding out, slowly, what
its own engine would let a boss be. You cannot
design that on paper. You build the tank, put it in the room, watch it fail to
be frightening, and change the guns.

The expensive order to do it in, though, is exactly this one. Art committed
before the encounter has settled is art that gets rebuilt, and four is a lot of
rebuilds to absorb. Studios pay that bill in different ways — Eclipse Glow Games
[built a full-scale cathedral just to run a hands-on
demo](/blog/tides-of-annihilation-cathedral-nanite-knights/), which is the same
arithmetic in a different direction: an enormous asset, one use, justified by
what it proves rather than by what it amortises.

What makes the Chimera unusual is that CD PROJEKT RED said the number out loud.
Most studios would rather you thought the boss arrived finished.
