I keep my own credit list in a plain text file, and for years that felt like
paranoia.

At sixteen hundred UTC this afternoon, Zerply goes dark. Sixteen years of VFX
profiles, reels and credit histories, switched off on a Monday. The export
window shut on the 21st, because a human being was processing each request by
hand and there were only so many hours in the week. Miss it and you missed it.
After today the domain is a redirect, and the page you spent a decade filling
in is a thing you used to have.

Five days after that export deadline closed, MobyGames announced that
developers can claim their credits — including the ones their studio left off
the roll entirely — verify their identity, and hang a portfolio off the result.

It costs $14.99 a month.

## Four o'clock

The product is good. I have read the announcement twice looking for the catch
in the design and it is not in the design: letting a claimed credit carry
screenshots, a contribution note and a reel is exactly the right shape for
art and technical art, where the job title is the least useful fact about the
job. Separating "prove you are you" from "prove the roll misspelled your name"
is a sane split. Somebody thought about this.

The catch is the meter.

Because look at what the two dates together describe. The free place
where our side of the industry kept its work is gone this afternoon. The paid
place opened the same week. Nobody planned that as a pincer — Zerply ran out
of money, Atari saw a business, and the calendar did the rest — and it lands
as one anyway, which is the part worth sitting with. The record of who made
what has no owner who is paid to keep it. So it goes where records go when
nobody is paid to keep them: to whoever can charge for it.

A hundred and eighty dollars a year, to hold the receipt for work you already
did.

## Gold on Friday

Now put that against the week the same industry was having.

Microsoft's latest round of cuts took just under half of World's Edge, the
studio holding Age of Empires, and cancelled the project the survivors were
working on. Over at The Coalition, the story director on *Gears of War: E-Day*
posted that he was out — days after the game went gold, with release on
6 October. The game is finished. It ships in eight days. The person who shaped
what it says will watch it come out from LinkedIn.

And Microsoft's chief executive, asked about the streamlining, said it was
great to see.

I don't think that man was being cruel. I think he was being accurate about
what he can see from where he sits, which is worse. From that altitude a studio
is a cost line and a shipped game is a delivered asset, and the several hundred
people who turned one into the other are an input that has finished inputting.
It is a resolution problem. The org chart stops rendering below a certain zoom
level, and everything I care about lives down there.

You know what happens next, because we have all watched it happen four times
this year. A few hundred people go looking for work at once, in a market
already full of people who went looking for work in July. And every one of them
opens with the same problem: proving what they did.

## What a line hides

Here is the thing a credit roll cannot tell you.

Last week it came out that the Chimera tank fight in *Phantom Liberty* took two
years. Not two years of a studio's time — two years of reiterating over and
over on one boss encounter, a fight most players clear in ten minutes and
remember as "the big tank one". Two years.

On the credits, that is a name under a heading. Maybe two names. The roll has
no field for "spent twenty-four months making a scripted vehicle read as
threatening at four different camera distances without breaking the collision
mesh", and that sentence is the only thing in the paragraph that would get
somebody hired.

This is the whole reason the MobyGames feature exists, and the reason I am
irritated rather than grateful. The database is offering to sell me a text box
in which to explain what the credit roll should have said. The credit roll
belongs to the studio. The studio wrote it, the studio shortened it, the
studio in several cases this month no longer exists. And the fix for a record
the employer got wrong is now a subscription the employee pays.

## A hundred and eighty dollars

Let me be plain about the arithmetic, because it is where this stops being
abstract.

Fifteen dollars a month is nothing to someone in work. To someone three months
into a layoff, it is a line on a spreadsheet next to the other lines, and it is
competing with Substance and Houdini Indie and the renewal on the domain where
the portfolio lives. It gets cut. Of course it gets cut. It is the one expense
on that list that produces nothing this month.

Which means the people who most need a verified record of their work are the
people most likely to switch it off, and the people who least need it — in
work, salaried, credited properly, not looking — are the ones who will
comfortably keep paying. Nobody designed that. It is what a subscription does
to a population being laid off in waves.

And it is a gate in front of a public good. The history of who built the games
is not a premium feature. It is the only durable thing an artist takes out of
a studio, because the art files stay behind, the tools stay behind, the build
stays behind, and the one asset that walks out with you is the sentence "I made
that." We have now put a card reader on that sentence.

## Paid first

There is a version of this that works, and it shipped this week too.

Transport Fever 3 comes out tomorrow with its best modders' work in the box on
day one, on PS5 and Xbox as well as PC. Urban Games paid those modders up
front. Gave them early builds. Let them argue about the tools while the
exporter could still change. Their names go out with the game.

Look at the size of that decision. Nobody needed a new technology, a new
platform, a database, a verification tier or fifteen dollars a month. Somebody
with authority over a budget decided that the work had a value before it
existed, and paid for it then, rather than deciding afterwards how little of it
to write down. It cost a line in a spreadsheet at a studio with a fraction of
Microsoft's resources.

So the problem was never that contribution is hard to measure. Every studio
knows exactly who did what; the version control history is right there, with
timestamps. The problem is that writing it down properly has no owner, no
budget and no deadline, and unowned work in this industry falls, always, onto
the person with the least power to refuse it.

Keep your own list. Screenshot your own work. Write down what you did,
in your own words, on a machine you control, this week and not the week you
need it — because the site that was holding it for you switches off at four
this afternoon, and nobody sent you a second reminder.

An industry that will not pay anyone to remember who built its things has
already told you, in the only language that costs it money, what it thinks the
builders are worth.
