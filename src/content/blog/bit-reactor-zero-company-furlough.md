Star Wars Zero Company shipped on 27 August and went straight into Steam's top
sellers by revenue. I wrote up the reviews here the same week — [an 8.5 across
six outlets](/blog/star-wars-zero-company-review-tactics-camera/), with the
recurring note being that somebody had finally made a Star Wars tactics game
that understood tactics games.

Most of the people who made it were off payroll while those reviews were going
up.

Game File broke it, and Game Developer, PC Gamer and Aftermath have all since
carried it: developer Bit Reactor furloughed staff in the weeks before launch,
with one source putting the figure at around **80 per cent of the team**. A
studio representative confirmed the furloughs and said the hope is to reinstate
people and restore their pay if the game does well. The decision was the
studio's own — not EA's, and not Disney's.

## Furlough is a specific word

It is not a layoff. Furloughed staff are still employed, still on the org
chart, and not being paid. No severance, because nobody has been terminated.
The arrangement is designed to be reversible, which is genuinely different from
a redundancy round, and it also means the people involved cannot draw a line
under it and go and find other work without walking away from whatever they are
owed.

So the income of the team now depends on the sales performance of a game they
have already finished and cannot touch. Every patch note they read is about
their own work, from the outside.

## Where the money runs out

The gap this fell into is structural, and anyone who has worked at a studio that
builds somebody else's licensed property will recognise it immediately.

A developer like Bit Reactor is funded on milestones. Money arrives when work is
delivered and signed off, and the last milestone is the one where the game goes
gold. After that the deliverables stop, which means the payments stop, and the
revenue share — if the deal even has a meaningful one, which on a licensed
property split between a publisher and a rights holder it often does not — turns
up quarters later. Meanwhile the studio still has a lease, a hardware refresh, a
render farm and forty-odd salaries.

Gold to launch is usually six to twelve weeks of certification, ratings, disc
manufacture and marketing runway. Payroll does not pause for it. A studio
without its next contract signed is paying full freight through the exact window
where it has nothing left to invoice.

Nobody talks about this window because it is boring right up until it isn't.

## The part that gets expensive later

Zachariah Scott, a former senior technical artist at the studio, criticised
leadership publicly for celebrating the game's reception without acknowledging
the people who were furloughed. That role is the reason the story landed
differently for me than another studio-in-trouble headline.

A technical artist is the person holding the parts of a production that were
never written down. The export chain from DCC into engine. The naming
conventions the level scripts silently depend on. The shader library and which
three of its variants are load-bearing. The rig that makes the camera behave.
When my own review of this game had a criticism, it was the camera — and a
camera in a squad tactics game is not a content fix. It is a system with
constraint solving, collision, and a hundred hand-tuned exceptions for the
rooms where the generic solution looks bad.

A skeleton crew can triage crashes and ship a stability patch. Reworking a
camera means going back into the rig, and the rig lives in somebody's head
alongside every reason it is shaped that way. Furlough that person for three
months and the knowledge does not sit in a wiki waiting for them. Some of it
goes to whichever studio calls first.

That is the cost nobody has on a spreadsheet. It gets paid in the patch that
takes eight weeks instead of two.

## What success means now

The studio's stated condition for bringing people back is the game performing.
No threshold has been published — no unit count, no revenue figure, no date by
which the call gets made. Zero Company is currently among Steam's top sellers,
which sounds like it ought to settle the question and does not, because a top
seller position on a launch week says nothing about whether the developer's
share of it clears a payroll that has been running unfunded since gold.

There is also an unresolved dispute between the studio's founders that has been
in the reporting since 2024, which I am not going to characterise from
second-hand summaries beyond noting it exists and is part of why the picture is
messy.

The game is good. The reviews were earned by people who were not being paid when
they came out. Both of those are true on the same Tuesday.
