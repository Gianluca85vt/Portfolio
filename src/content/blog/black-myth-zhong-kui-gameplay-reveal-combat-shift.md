Game Science has a habit now: every 20 August, the anniversary of Black Myth:
Wukong's 2024 launch, the studio posts an update on whatever it's building
next. This year that update was substantial — 15 minutes of gameplay from
**Black Myth: Zhong Kui**, the first extended, in-game look since the sequel
was announced at gamescom's Opening Night Live a year ago. The footage
racked up over a million views within a day, which tells you how much
goodwill one 2024 game bought an entire follow-up nobody has a release date
for.

The build is early — Game Science says so upfront, and it's captured in a
cinematic 21:9 crop rather than a native gameplay aspect ratio, which is
usually a tell that a studio is still controlling exactly how much you see.
Inside that frame: Zhong Kui, the demon-queller and ghost-catching
magistrate of Chinese folklore, wielding a sword instead of Wukong's
staff, in combat that reads noticeably slower and more deliberate than
anything in the first game. No release window has been given.

That's the whole news item. The interesting part is one sentence buried in
most of the coverage: where Wukong was built around acrobatic,
transformation-heavy combat, Zhong Kui is built around **blocking and
parrying**.

## Why that's a bigger ask than it sounds

Wukong's combat system, mechanically, is forgiving to build content for.
Transformations swap the player into an entirely different moveset and
silhouette — different animation set, different hitboxes, done. Evasion-based
combat rewards good read-and-dodge timing, but the animator's job is mostly
about making the dodge windows generous enough to feel fair and the attack
telegraphs big enough to read at speed. It's a system built to look
spectacular in short trailer cuts, because acrobatics photograph well.

A parry-and-block system built around a grounded stance is a different
discipline entirely. The whole feel of it lives in a much narrower band:
the exact number of frames of parry window, how forgiving or punishing a
late block is, how the character's weight reads on impact, and — the part
that actually eats animator time — **hit reactions that have to be authored
per attack type**, not just per enemy. Souls-adjacent parry systems live or
die on whether a block feels like it's stopping real mass. That means
weapon-clash VFX synced frame-accurately to the parry window, camera shake
tuned so it reads without disorienting, and enemy attack animations
re-authored so their telegraphs are readable from a blocking stance instead
of a dodging one — you can't just reuse Wukong's enemy attack library and
swap the player's response to it.

None of that shows up in a highlight reel. It shows up in whether the combat
still feels good on the fortieth enemy type, long after the marketing
footage has stopped being representative.

## The character-design problem underneath it

There's a second, quieter shift. Wukong is a shapeshifting trickster —
the character design brief is "whatever's most striking in this scene,"
because transformation is the whole point. Zhong Kui is the opposite kind
of folklore figure: a magistrate, bound by rank and formality, whose
design has to communicate authority and constraint rather than chaos. That
pushes the costume and prop language toward heavier, more architectural
silhouettes — robes with real structural weight, ceremonial elements that
have to survive fast combat readability without turning into noise. Grounded
combat and a grounded character concept are reinforcing the same design
constraint from two different departments, which is either a sign the
studio has a clear brief, or a sign they've backed themselves into having
to nail both at once.

## What I'd actually watch for next

Not another cinematic-crop trailer — Game Science has shown it's willing to
sit on footage for a year between updates, so there's no reason to expect
uncut gameplay soon. What would tell you whether the parry system works is
the same thing that tells you with any Souls-adjacent combat: enemy variety
under player-controlled camera, not scripted boss choreography. Fifteen
minutes of curated footage can make any timing window look generous. A
public demo — the kind Wukong never really got before launch — is the only
thing that would settle it.

Everything above is current as of the 20 August 2026 reveal; Game Science
has not confirmed a platform list, release window, or engine details beyond
what shipped with Wukong.
