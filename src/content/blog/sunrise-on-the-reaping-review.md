Peter Bradshaw gave *The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping* one star in the
Guardian. Time Out gave it four out of five. Same 158 minutes, same cut, both
filed off the same embargo.

The film opened in the US on 20 November 2026 — the sixth Panem picture and the
second prequel, with Joseph Zada as the young Haymitch Abernathy and Ralph
Fiennes back as Snow. Lionsgate took $21.25m in Thursday previews, which
Deadline puts second in franchise history behind *Catching Fire*'s $25.2m in
2013. Commercially it is fine. Critically it has handed me the widest spread I
have had to average this year.

## The number, and what it is made of

Six verdicts carried a number I could read and check:

- Time Out — 4/5, so 8
- Collider, Victoria Luxford — 8
- Screen Rant — 6
- Film-Book — 6
- The Times, Kevin Maher — 2/5, so 4
- The Guardian, Peter Bradshaw — 1/5, so 2

Mean 5.67, which rounds to **5.5**, and the range runs 2 to 8. Six points of
spread on a ten-point scale means the critics answered different questions.
Bradshaw's objection is that the film has no reason to exist beyond its own fanbase;
Maher's two stars call it an origin story nobody asked for. The eights are weighing
whether this particular story, competently told, is worth two and a half
hours. Those two arguments never
meet.

And six is a small sample pulled in a particular direction, because the outlets
that print numbers are not the outlets that set the temperature. Variety, the
New York Times, Screen Daily and The Hollywood Reporter all filed and none of
them scores out of anything. Screen Daily's headline calls the prequel
underwhelming and short of compelling characters. Brandon Yu in the New York
Times landed on bloated and limited. RogerEbert.com's review is warm, and runs
under a headline about feeding the fans, which is close to Bradshaw's complaint
read in the other direction.

The aggregates have been moving all week, so the snapshot matters. Forbes
recorded 69 percent on Rotten Tomatoes from 62 reviews on 17 November, the day
the embargo lifted; by 20 November the page showed 67 percent from 132.
Metacritic sat at 55 from 32 critics on its critic list and 56 from 35 on the
film's main page. Audience numbers are a different film entirely — the
Popcornmeter was at 95 percent. Read the 5.5 as what the scoring outlets said,
and read the 56 as what the prose reviews said, and the gap between them is the
film's whole reception.

## Why a well-made film reads flat

The crew is strong. Jo Willems shot it, his fourth trip to Panem
after *Catching Fire* and both *Mockingjay*s, and this one originated in IMAX
digital. Uli Hanisch — *Babylon Berlin*, *Dark* — did the production design,
and one review places the tribute training centre inside Berlin's
International Congress Centre, which is exactly the kind of brutalist
borrowing Hanisch is good at. Janek Sirrs supervised the visual effects, split
across a list of houses that circulated with the trailer and that I could not
check against the finished credits.

So you get reviews that call the film the best-looking entry in the series and,
a paragraph later, visually flat. Both halves are accurate. A franchise's
design language depreciates. Panem's was set in 2012 — the brutalist Capitol,
the soot-and-timber District 12, the pastel grotesques of the broadcast circus
— and every film since has had to build inside it. By the sixth, a new set is
being judged against five previous versions of itself, and the audience has
priced in the familiarity before anyone has lit anything.

The escape route is the one [Widow's Bay took when it won Best Set Decoration
for looking undesigned](/blog/widows-bay-set-decoration/): stop decorating,
build rooms that look lived in, let the camera find them. A Quarter Quell
cannot do that. The Capitol is supposed to look designed, by people who want
you to notice the design, which leaves the art department competing against its
own back catalogue with one hand tied.

IMAX is the one new variable, and it cuts both ways. The taller frame gives the
arena vertical space the 2012 crop never had — useful for a valley full of
plants and animals that reviewers cannot tell are real or robotic. It also
resolves every seam in the dressing at a scale the earlier films never had to
survive, across a 158-minute cut that several reviewers describe as slow out of
the gate. A large-format frame does not flatter an unhurried scene. It turns it
into an inventory of the room.

This is the second scored film on the blog this month where the made parts
survive a reception that went after the writing. *Ebenezer* produced a
four-point spread where [everyone praised the ghosts and nobody could agree
about the lead](/blog/ebenezer-review/). Same shape, wider gap. In both cases
the departments that build things came out of it better than the department
that decides what gets built.

Zada is the thing all six verdicts agree on, the one-star included. David
Rooney in The Hollywood Reporter called it a breakout and the beating heart of
the film. A series that has run out of new places to put a camera has at least
found a new face to point it at, and for the people who spent two years
dressing a valley in Berlin, that is a thin return on the work.
