Nine eras. Twenty-five actors, more than a hundred extras, close to a hundred
and fifty crew, and — because somebody had to be on set holding them — a pig,
five chickens and dozens of rats. That is what Amplitude Studios says went into
the ninety seconds of live action it premiered for Humankind 2 at Gamescom's
Opening Night Live, shot at Nu Boyana Film Studios in Sofia with practical sets
and props built for the job.

The internet watched it and said a machine made it.

Not a fringe reaction either. Within hours of the trailer going up the comments
had settled on "AI slop", the phrase doing most of the heavy lifting in
arguments about images this year. Amplitude answered by email — real actors,
practical sets, custom-made props — and then, on 4 September, put out a
behind-the-scenes clip showing the sound stage, the blue screen and the crew
standing around in the middle of it.

## The tells people used were all real

Here is the uncomfortable part for anyone who makes pictures for a living. The
things viewers pointed at were all genuinely in the footage. Every one of them
also turns up in work nobody generated.

Shot length, first. A ninety-second sweep through nine eras gives you maybe four
seconds a shot, most of them less. Nothing is held long enough to inspect a
hand, a face, a piece of set dressing. Generative video clips are short for a
different reason — the models were trained and served in short chunks — but the
grammar that comes out the other end looks identical. Quick cuts, no lingering.
An audience that has learned to distrust footage nobody lets you stare at will
distrust this too.

Then the compositing. Nine historical periods in one shoot means blue screen
behind almost every plate, environments built in post, and a grade laid over the
whole thing to make Bronze Age and orbital mechanics feel like one film. That
grade is doing enormous work. It also flattens everyone into the same light,
softens the edges where actor meets background, and produces exactly the
plasticky evenness that diffusion output has. A comp artist earns their week
hiding those seams. The better they hide them, the more the result resembles an
image that never had seams to begin with.

Crowd extension is the third one. You shoot a hundred extras and you multiply
them, and multiplied crowds have repeating faces and repeating gaits at the back
of frame. Tiled duplicates are one of the oldest tells for generative video.
They are also just what crowd work has always looked like when you zoom in.

BETC handled the creative, and LiveFX did the CGI on the pieces that could never
be practical — a tornado, a nuclear blast, the space sequence. Those are the
shots where the seams live.

## The montage form is the trap

Strip the argument back and what you get is this: the visual language of "sweep
through all of human history in a minute" is the same visual language a
text-to-video model produces when you ask it for anything ambitious. Scale
jumps. Era jumps. A camera doing impossible moves. Everything gorgeous and
nothing sitting still.

Studios have been making that trailer for thirty years. It belonged to them
first. But the models learned on it, they are fluent in it now, and audiences
have started reading the form itself as a signature. Amplitude walked into a
shape that no longer signals what it used to.

I have watched enough reveal reels to know that the ones that survive this
scrutiny tend to share a quality: somewhere in them a shot is held long enough
that you can see something specific and slightly wrong. A prop with a real
scuff. A performer's weight shifting. The chickens, honestly. Nobody prompts a
pig.

## What Amplitude did not say

They confirmed the shoot. They did not say whether generative AI touched
anything in post-production, and nobody has asked the question in a way that got
answered. Those are different claims, and a studio that has just spent a week
defending its live-action production has every reason to leave the second one
alone.

Which matters, because most of the accusation amounted to "something here was
machine-made", and a behind-the-scenes clip of a sound stage does not settle
that question. Compositing tools
artists already have on their desks ship generative fill, generative extend,
generative cleanup. The line between "we filmed this" and "no model touched
this" stopped being the same line about two years ago, and studios have not
worked out how to talk about the gap.

The blog covered a version of this in August, when LinkedIn's
[million-click "seems like AI slop" button turned out to suppress human
renders](/blog/linkedin-ai-slop-button-portfolio-work/) at the same rate as
generated ones. Same failure, different venue. Neither a crowd nor a classifier
can tell a heavily-post-processed real image from a synthetic one, because at
the pixel level, after the grade, there is often not much left to tell.

## The cost is entirely one-sided

An accusation costs one comment. The rebuttal cost Amplitude a press statement,
a set of production stills, a behind-the-scenes edit and a news cycle spent
talking about their process instead of their game.

That asymmetry is the working condition now, and it lands hardest on the people
with the least budget. A studio with SEGA behind it can produce call sheets from
Sofia. A freelancer whose ArtStation post gets the same comment has a layered
file and a timelapse nobody will watch, which is the same evidence in a form
that convinces nobody.

Back in August I wrote about
[reading Opening Night Live trailers for what they actually show you](/blog/gamescom-onl-2026-what-to-watch-for/)
— in-engine versus target render, the tells that tell you something. Those tells
still work. "Looks AI" reads a genre rather than an image, and the genre now
belongs to everybody.
