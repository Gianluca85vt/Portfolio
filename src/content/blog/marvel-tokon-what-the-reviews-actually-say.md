Arc System Works' Marvel tag fighter landed on **6 August**, the reviews have
had a week to pile up, and the scoreboard is doing something I do not see often:
it is telling two completely different stories at the same time.

Critics: **87** on OpenCritic, Mighty tier, 94% recommending it. **85** on
Metacritic across 41 reviews, of which forty are positive and exactly one is
mixed. Not a single negative.

Players on Steam: **Mixed. 48% positive** across more than five thousand
reviews.

That is a forty-point gap on the same game in the same week. I went through the
lot — the majors, the Italian sites, France, Spain, Germany, Japan, Australia,
Malaysia — to work out where it comes from. Short version: almost none of it is
about the game, and the part that *is* about the game is the bit I find most
interesting as an artist.

So what are the players so annoyed about?

## The PC port, almost entirely

This is the whole gap. It is not subtle.

Look at the two user scores side by side. On Metacritic, where the ratings are
overwhelmingly PS5 players, the **user score is 8.0**. On Steam it is **48%
positive**. Same game, same week, same patch level. The only variable is the
platform.

The PC version shipped with frame pacing that fell apart on hardware that has no
business struggling — the bottleneck turned out to be a single CPU core, so
throwing a better GPU at it changed nothing. In a genre where the entire
contract with the player is that the game reads your input on a specific frame,
an unstable 60fps is not a blemish. It is a broken promise.

Then it spread. Because crossplay is on by default, PS5 players started eating
rollback spikes in matches against struggling PC opponents, and the community's
own workaround became *turn crossplay off* — which is a grim thing to have to
recommend for a game whose headline feature is a shared player pool.

And there is a second tier of complaints that has nothing to do with
performance at all:

- A **mandatory PlayStation Network login** on a PC game, which players say
  drags background processes along with it
- **Kernel-level anti-cheat**, which means no Linux and no Steam Deck
- Players in **countries where PSN does not operate** simply cannot play a game
  they are allowed to buy

Arc System Works shipped a first big patch on **10 August** that, by PC Gamer's
account, largely fixed the performance. The Steam score barely moved. That is
how this works now: the review score is a record of the launch, not of the
current build, and you do not get the launch back.

Everyeye were blunt enough to score the PS5 version and tell readers outright
not to buy it on PC. Gamekult did something I respect more — they declined to
publish a score at launch and explained why, rather than grading an online game
on a broken sample. That is the correct call and almost nobody makes it.

Worth saying: it sold anyway. Nearly **24,500 concurrent players** on Steam at
peak and fourth on the platform's sellers chart. Being furious and playing it
constantly are not mutually exclusive.

## Now the part that is actually about the game

Strip out the port and one real criticism survives, and it shows up in reviews
that otherwise disagree about everything.

**You cannot always tell what is happening on screen.**

Gamesurf put it most directly — with a support character on top of your point
character, the screen fills up and you lose track of what you are supposed to be
hitting. ScreenRant found the UI failed to communicate. jeuxvideo.com choked on
the tutorial. Polygon, the one properly sceptical voice in the whole pile, went
further and said the game has not yet worked out what it is.

Here is that criticism in a single screenshot:

<figure>
  <img src="/img/blog/marvel-tokon/screen-02.jpg" loading="lazy" width="1440" height="810" alt="Doctor Doom firing a full-screen super, the arena almost entirely obscured by green energy, particle sparks and impact flashes" />
  <figcaption>Doctor Doom mid-super. Beautiful frame — now find the opponent in it.</figcaption>
</figure>

That is a gorgeous frame. It is also a fight I cannot read. Where is the
opponent, what state are they in, and am I about to get hit?

I want to be fair about how hard this problem is, because it is my end of the
job. Fighting games have the tightest visual brief in the medium. Every effect
has to sell impact — that is the entire feel of the thing — and simultaneously
never obscure the two silhouettes and the space between them, because that is
the information the player is actually reading. Those two goals are in direct
opposition. Turn the spectacle up and legibility goes down. There is no clever
trick that gets you both; there is only where you choose to sit on the line.

Tokon sits further towards spectacle than any tag fighter I can think of, and
then multiplies the problem by putting **four characters per side** instead of
the usual two or three. Every extra body is another silhouette, another palette,
another set of particles competing for the same screen. The 4v4 hook and the
readability complaint are not two separate issues. They are the same decision,
seen from the good side and the bad side.

The tools to fix it are the boring ones, which is usually a good sign: pull the
saturation and the value range down on assists so the point characters stay
dominant, put a stronger rim or a subtle desaturation on the non-active fighter,
darken the stage behind a super instead of adding light on top of it. None of
that requires new tech. All of it requires someone with authority to say the
effect is too loud and mean it. That is an art direction call, and it is
patchable.

## What they got completely right

I do not want the criticism to swamp this, because the presentation is genuinely
special and it is why the scores are where they are.

<figure>
  <img src="/img/blog/marvel-tokon/screen-03.jpg" loading="lazy" width="1440" height="810" alt="A comic-book page transition: Danger sent flying by Captain America inside a full-bleed panel, with Spider-Man reacting in two smaller panels below and printer's marks along the edges" />
  <figcaption>Panels, gutters, hand-lettered sound effects — and printer's registration marks around the bleed. That last detail is the one that gives the whole thing away as deliberate.</figcaption>
</figure>

The comic-panel language is not a filter dropped over the top of a fighting
game. It is used structurally — panels, gutters, sound-effect lettering, the
printer's registration marks running along the edges of the page. Somebody on
that team looked at how a comic actually reads and rebuilt the transitions
around it, rather than settling for halftone dots and calling it a homage. The
Games Machine's line about landing between American comics grit and Japanese
styling is the right read, and holding both of those at once without the result
turning to mush is the hard part.

The stages carry the same care, and this is the bit I keep going back to:

<figure>
  <img src="/img/blog/marvel-tokon/screen-05.jpg" loading="lazy" width="1440" height="810" alt="Ghost Rider fighting outside Fogwell's Gym on a street cordoned off by Damage Control, with armed agents watching from behind the tape" />
  <figcaption>Damage Control have taped off the street and their agents are watching the fight. The gym is Fogwell's. None of it is mechanical, all of it is doing a job.</figcaption>
</figure>

Look at what is in that frame. Damage Control have cordoned off the street.
Their agents are standing behind the tape watching the fight. It is set outside
Fogwell's Gym, which is where Daredevil's father boxed. None of that is
mechanical — you could strip every bit of it out and the fight would play the
same. It is there because environment work at this level is world-building, and
somebody understood that a background is a chance to tell you where you are and
who is watching.

The rest of the consensus, briefly: the twenty launch characters across five
teams of four, plus The Champion as an unlock, are mechanically distinct in ways
that track their comics — Ghost Rider overheats, Captain America's shield
ricochets, Magneto zones with scrap. Kieron Gillen, an actual Marvel writer with
X-Men, Thor and Iron Man runs behind him, wrote all five story arcs. The
one-button specials get a newcomer into a real match in minutes without capping
what an expert can do. The DualSense work drew specific praise, which is rare.

And the flaws the press agrees on, equally briefly: Episode Mode is repetitive
and cutscenes recycle between arcs, so solo players get the least out of this.
Defensive options are thin, which lets a snowballing opponent snowball. Launch
balance is uneven, with Wolverine the name that keeps coming up. And there is no
traditional Arcade mode.

## What I would do

**On PS5, buy it.** The critical consensus is not wrong and the version they
scored is the version you would be getting. It is €69.99, which is what
everything costs now.

**On PC, wait.** Not forever — the August 10 patch reportedly did most of the
work, and Arc System Works are clearly still on it. But let another patch cycle
go by and check the recent Steam reviews rather than the overall score, because
the overall one is carrying the launch week around with it and will for months.

**If you are on Linux or Steam Deck**, there is nothing to wait for. Kernel
anti-cheat is a decision, not a bug.

The thing I will remember about this launch is not the 87. It is that a game
this well made shipped in a state where the smart move for a PS5 owner was to
switch off the feature connecting them to half the player base. The reviews were
right about the game. The players were right about the product. Those are
different things, and this week is a good reminder that a score measures one of
them.
