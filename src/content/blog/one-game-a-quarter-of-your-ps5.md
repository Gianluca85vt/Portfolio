Grand Theft Auto 6 arrives on Thursday at somewhere around two hundred and
twelve gigabytes, and the most revealing thing anybody said about it this week
came from Madden NFL 27, which put out a message asking players please not to
delete it to make room.

A company that was taken private two months ago in a fifty-five-billion-dollar
deal is reduced to asking you, individually, for a slot on your hard drive.

It is funny for about four seconds. Then you notice what it admits. The scarce
resource on a console in 2026 is the eight hundred and forty gigabytes of usable
space inside it, and this week made it very clear that nothing shares that space
out except weight.

## Shelf space

Do the arithmetic, because nobody at the platform holders seems keen to.

A current 1TB PS5 gives you somewhere in the region of eight hundred and forty
gigabytes once the system has taken its cut. The original 825GB machine, which
is still in a great many living rooms, gives you six hundred and sixty-seven. At
roughly 212GB, one game takes a quarter of the first and nearly a third of the
second. Before the day-one patch. Before an online component that will do what
every online component does and grow for a decade.

Worth being straight about the number: 212 comes off store listings and the
trackers that watch them, the widely-repeated figure is 210, and Rockstar has
not put either in its own words. Call it a shade over two hundred and treat the
decimal as noise. The fraction is the whole story.

And the fraction is why EA is out here begging. Madden is fighting for
residency, because a game you uninstall is a game you do not come back to. Every publisher running a live service knows this
number to a decimal place. Which means install size stopped being an engineering
by-product some time ago and became territory — and the only people who can
seize a quarter of a drive are the people who were already winning.

## Cardboard and a torch

The other half of the week makes the same point from underneath.

GTA 6 has no disc. The "physical" edition is a code in a box, and in PEGI
regions that code is printed on the inside of the cover rather than on a
separate card. Shine a strong torch at the closed case from the outside and you
can read it, backwards, through the cardboard. Walk into a shop, light up a
shelf, redeem the lot, go home. Whoever then pays eighty for the box takes home
a box.

Look at what that object actually is. An empty Blu-ray tray. A rectangle of
printed card. A string of letters, sold at full price, defeated by a torch.

The security failure will be fixed in a manufacturing run and is already handled
differently in North America. What interests me is what the object confesses. The disc is gone, so the install is the
copy. The only version of this game that will ever exist for you is the one
sitting in a quarter of your drive, and the thing on the shelf is a receipt for
it.

Shelf space used to be controlled by whoever could afford the end cap. We moved
that arrangement inside the machine, where you cannot see how full it is until
something has to go.

## The drive is working now

Then there is the part that genuinely impressed me, which comes from the
opposite direction.

Gears of War: E-Day keeps its city at full size on an Xbox Series S, a machine
whose games see roughly eight gigabytes of memory. The Coalition did it by
setting aside a few gigabytes of the console's SSD and treating it as a slower
tier of RAM — pushing city data out to flash when access heuristics say it has
gone cold, pulling it back when it is wanted. Write-ups of the Digital Foundry
interview put the net gain near a gigabyte. Tom's Hardware went and measured the
same system on PC and clocked the streaming at up to 3.7 GB/s.

That is lovely work. It is also an authoring problem wearing an engineering hat,
and anyone who has split a level for streaming knows it: deciding what goes cold
*together* is a content decision. Somebody in the art directory chose the
granularity, and if they chose it badly the heuristic thrashes and the frame
budget goes with it.

Hold the two stories next to each other, though. One game is taking a quarter of
the drive as a warehouse. Another is spending the drive as memory. The same
component, asked to do two jobs that are both getting harder, inside a budget
that stopped growing when the console shipped.

## Who pays for the compression

Two hundred gigabytes is textures, audio, and the same asset written down eleven
times. Geometry barely registers; it has been cheap for years. BC7 at 4K across a few thousand materials, because somebody decided the
normals could not take BC5 on hero surfaces. Audio kept at a bitrate the audio
lead won in an argument in month four, when there was still room. And
duplication, which is the quiet one — the same brick wall shipped into eleven
streaming packages, because reading it linearly off the drive beats seeking for
it, and the drive is cheaper than the seek.

Every one of those is a defensible call. Together they are a quarter of your
console.

Here is the part that does not make the trade press. The compression pass arrives
at the end. It always arrives at the end. Somebody looks at the number in the
last six weeks and the request comes down to take forty gigabytes out, and it
never comes out of scope, because nobody removes a district in the last six
weeks. It comes out of surfaces. It comes out as an automated pass that drops
whole directories two tiers and a list of exceptions you have to argue for one
by one, with the argument due Friday.

Every environment artist has watched something they tuned for days get crushed
in a batch job, lost the banding argument, and shipped it anyway. Nobody outside
the team ever knows which surfaces lost. You know. You know every time you walk
past it.

## Ninety dollars a slot

The analysts have published their arithmetic and it is worth reading as policy
rather than as forecasting. Roughly 55 million copies by the end of Take-Two's
fiscal year in March. A blended average selling price near ninety dollars —
eighty for the standard edition, a hundred for the Ultimate, preorders skewing
to the expensive one. Call it five billion dollars.

Set that against a quarter of every drive it lands on and you have the exchange
rate the industry now runs on. The space costs the buyer nothing and costs the
publisher nothing, so it goes to whoever can command it, and the bill is paid by
whatever gets uninstalled to clear room, which is the fifteen-hour game somebody
spent four years on.

Watch the same mechanism work on the calendar this month. Studios are shifting
release dates out of the blast radius. Steam approvals are running near twelve
days and indie launches are slipping behind them. One resource after another —
drive space, the release window, the awards conversation — allocated by weight,
with no rule anywhere that says a smaller thing gets to keep its slot.

The discs went and the shelf stayed. It is inside the machine now, where nobody
can see how full it is until something has to go, and nothing in there rations
it except how big you already are. At least the old one could be audited with a
torch.
