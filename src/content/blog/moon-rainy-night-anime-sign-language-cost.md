Hands are where 2D animation budgets go to die. Every frame is a fresh drawing,
the shapes are small, the silhouettes read as ambiguous blobs at speed, and a
hand that is off by a few degrees stops reading as a hand. So most shows hide
them. Pockets, held poses, a cut to the face while the arm does its work
offscreen.

CompTown has just announced a show that cannot do any of that.

On 7 October the official site for the TV anime of Kuzushiro's **Amayo no
Tsuki** — *The Moon on a Rainy Night* — put out its first promotional video and
named the main cast. Sora Amamiya plays Kanon Oikawa, who is hard of hearing.
Iori Saeki plays Saki Kindaichi, the classmate who approaches her after
noticing how the rest of the school handles her. The premiere window is 2027,
with no month, no episode count and no streaming partner attached. The manga
runs in Kodansha's Comic DAYS; English-language listings disagree on how many
volumes exist, seven in one place and twelve in another, so treat the length as
unsettled.

## Why signing is not a gesture

A wave is a gesture. You can draw it loosely, hold a cel for three frames,
smear the in-between and nobody is harmed. Sign language is a language, and
that changes the drawing brief completely.

Four things carry meaning in a sign: the shape of the hand, the way it is
oriented, where it sits relative to the body, and the path it travels. Get the
handshape wrong and you have produced a different word, or no word. Get the
movement path wrong and you have produced a grammatical error, because in
Japanese Sign Language, as in every signed language, the motion is doing work
that spoken Japanese does with particles and inflection.

Which means the usual economies are unavailable. You cannot hold a cel, because
the movement is the sentence. You cannot smear an in-between, because the
in-between is a phoneme. You cannot cut to a reaction shot to skip the middle
of the motion — the middle of the motion is the dialogue, and cutting away is
the animated equivalent of muting a line and leaving the subtitle up. You
cannot loop a cycle, because nobody says the same thing twice.

A deaf viewer notices all of it immediately, in the way a Japanese speaker
notices a mispronounced word. For everyone else it registers as a vague
discomfort they cannot name, which is arguably worse, because it reads as bad
animation rather than as a mistake.

## The credit that is missing

The December 2025 announcement gave the core team. Tomoe Makino directs, off
*Aharen-san wa Hakarenai*. Shogo Yasukawa handles series composition and
scripts, off *Shokugeki no Soma*. Character design is Ayumi Nishibata, who did
*Otherside Picnic*. Music comes from jizue, a Kyoto band, which is an
interesting choice for a show about someone who does not hear the way the
audience does.

This week's video added the next layer: assistant directors Seung Deok Kim and
Tsutomu Tomoyuki, colour key Haruko Nobori, art director Mai Ichioka,
compositing director of photography Atsushi Satō, editor Masahiro Gotō, sound
director Nobuyuki Abe. CompTown's president Masao Yamamura takes the animation
producer credit.

Neither list names sign-language supervision — 手話監修, the credit a Japanese
production uses for it. That may simply mean it has not been announced; studios
reveal staff in layers and this is only layer two.

But it is the credit worth watching for, because of what it buys. An
animator who does not sign cannot invent a correct sign, any more than a
letterer who does not read Arabic can invent correct Arabic. The supervisor is
not a consultant brought in to check the work afterwards. They are upstream of
the drawings, deciding what the reference footage contains, and the reference
has to exist before anyone keys a scene.

The reference point here is *A Silent Voice*, Kyoto Animation's 2016 film,
which is why it keeps coming up whenever this material does. Naoko Yamada has
described getting direction on every aspect of the signing — the meaning, the
reasoning behind it, and how it looked — running from pre-production through
post. That is a structural arrangement, not a courtesy credit, and it is the
reason the JSL in that film is held up as accurate rather than decorative.

## Two ways out, both expensive

There is a route that guarantees fidelity, and the blog has already looked at
it: the live-action *Look Back* [handed a sequence to a rotoscope
director](/blog/look-back-live-action-rotoscope-sequence/) rather than trusting
key animation to carry it. Rotoscope solves hands by refusing to invent them.
Shoot a signer, trace the result, and the handshapes are correct by
construction. It also looks like rotoscope, which is a strong stylistic
commitment to make on a school romance, and it is slow.

The other route is 3D hands, posed on a rig and rendered to match the 2D. On
paper it is the obvious answer — build one hand, reuse it forever. In practice
the saving tends to evaporate, which is the whole finding of what Studio Orange
does: [every trick that makes CG read as
anime](/blog/studio-orange-3d-anime-2d-look/) costs back the asset reuse that
3D was supposed to buy. Hands are the worst case for that, because the
corrections anime demands — the slight slide of a shape, the line weight, the
redrawn frame when the silhouette goes muddy — land hardest exactly where the
detail is finest.

So the likely answer is the unglamorous one. Good reference, a supervisor, and
a lot of ordinary key animation done carefully, with the signing scenes
budgeted as the expensive sequences they are and the rest of the show paying
for them.

## The mix is a problem too

Naming a sound director early is a hint, and the hint points at something real.
A story told partly from inside the head of a hard-of-hearing character has a
mixing problem, and the cheap solutions are all bad. Muffle everything and you
have a gimmick that gets tiresome in two episodes. Drop to silence and you have
flattened a range of hearing into a binary. Partial hearing is frequency-shaped
rather than volume-shaped, which is a far more interesting thing to build in a
mix and a much harder one to sustain across twelve episodes.

Abe has the first chance to decide how often the show sits in Kanon's hearing
at all, and that decision shapes the animation too, because a scene the
audience hears from inside Kanon needs the signing legible on screen without a
single spoken line propping it up.

A premiere window two years out and a credit list still filling in. The next
staff reveal is the one to read.
