On 12 September the production committee for *Bleach: Thousand-Year Blood War –
The Calamity* put out a notice: episodes 49 and 50 were moving to 19 and 26
October. The reason given was quality, as the story approached its climax — the
committee's wording was about delivering the work in the best possible form.
It was the second schedule change for this cour, which had premiered on 25 July.

Episode 50 went out today. It is the last one. Studio Pierrot started this show
on 5 October 2004, ran it for 366 episodes to March 2012, brought it back in
autumn 2022, and has now finished the Thousand-Year Blood War arc across four
parts and fifty episodes. Twenty-two years, near enough.

I want to talk about the four weeks, because the shape of that delay is a
legible piece of production information and almost nobody reads it as one.

## The end of a show is the expensive part

A fifty-episode run is not fifty equally difficult episodes. In a shonen
adaptation the difficulty curve bends sharply upward at the end, and it bends in
the departments that are hardest to throw bodies at.

Think about what episodes 49 and 50 of this particular arc have to contain.
Final-form fights. Bankai released at full length. Reiatsu doing things to the
air around it. That work is effects animation — drawn, frame by frame, by a
small number of people who are good at it — and then photography, where those
elements get layered, glowed, distorted and graded into the shot. Both are
per-cut costs. Neither divides cleanly across more staff. You cannot put six
animators on one cut of Ichigo's getsuga and get it six times faster; you get
six slightly different getsugas and a supervisor's week spent reconciling them.

Meanwhile the slot is still twenty-two minutes, every week, the same as episode
one.

So the overrun arrives at the end by construction. Episodes are made roughly in
order, the buffer gets spent on whatever went wrong in the middle, and the most
expensive episodes in the production meet the schedule at its thinnest. The
notice in September was not the moment the problem started. It was the moment
there was no slack left to hide it in.

## Twenty-two years is two different pipelines

The 2004 series and the 2026 series are the same franchise made by different
machinery. A mid-2000s TV anime composite was thin by today's standards: digital
paint, a modest stack of layers, effects done largely in-line with the
animation, a grade that mostly left the cels looking like cels. It was quick
because it had to be, at a weekly cadence, for 366 episodes.

What a Thousand-Year Blood War cut does is a different order of work. The
compositing pass is where a lot of the show's identity now lives, which is the
same point as [the credit that decides how a ufotable film will
look](/blog/witch-on-the-holy-night-ufotable-photography/) — photography stopped
being the step that assembles the episode and became the step that authors it.
That buys enormous visual range. It also means the per-cut cost of the hardest
sequences went up by a lot more than the weekly runtime went down, which is to
say not at all.

## Reruns are the cheapest buffer on television

The gap got filled with reruns — episodes 7, 28, 46 and 48, per Anime News
Network, through late September and into October. That is the standard move, and
it is standard because it costs the committee almost nothing. The slot keeps its
habit with the audience, the advertising inventory still sells, and the staff get
four weeks they would otherwise have taken out of their own nights.

This blog has watched the same instrument used on a much bigger scale this
autumn: [Chiikawa's thirteen-week break is a production
buffer](/blog/chiikawa-break-production-buffer/), twenty-five reruns holding a
slot to Christmas. Four weeks for a finale and thirteen weeks for a daily strip
are the same decision at different magnitudes — pay the cost where the audience
can see it, because the alternative cost is permanent.

And a finale is exactly where that trade is most obviously worth taking. An
episode that ships under-composited is the episode people will link to for
twenty years. A four-week wait is forgotten by December.

## What the notice did not say

"To improve quality" names no department, no sequence and no cause. It is the
phrasing a committee reaches for when it would rather not say whether this was
an outsourced cour that came back wrong, a key animator lost mid-production, or
simply forty-eight episodes of accumulated optimism. I do not know which, and
nothing published tells me.

A couple of other things are worth marking as unsettled rather than reported.
Coverage disagrees on where the finale streamed outside Japan — some of it
points at Hulu and Disney+, some at Netflix for territories outside the US. And
one outlet has reported a special "ending movie" tied to the finale, which I am
not treating as confirmed: no announcement from Shueisha, Pierrot or TV Tokyo
has turned up behind it, and that report carries its own internal date
contradictions. If it is real, it will be announced properly.

What is certain is the thing that happened today. A show that started when
flip-phones were current finished its last episode, four weeks later than
planned, because somebody decided the last four weeks of work were worth
waiting for.
