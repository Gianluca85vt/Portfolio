Zerply shuts down tomorrow. Sixteen years of VFX credits and reels, gone, and
[no one site replaces what it did](/blog/zerply-closing-export-your-work/) —
which makes the timing of the other thing happening this week hard to ignore.
MobyGames, the games database Atari has owned since 2022, is opening up its
credit records. Developers can claim their own page, verify who they are, and
build a portfolio hanging off the credits the database already holds.

Including, and this is the part worth sitting with, credits they never
received.

## What the roll leaves out

Game credits are written by the employer, and they are written once. There is
no appeal after ship. Every artist who has been in the industry more than a
couple of years knows somebody who missed a roll: the contractor whose
six-month stint ended in March on a game that shipped in November, the
outsourcing house that got one line reading the vendor's company name while
eleven people inside it modelled half the props, the person who left badly and
discovered what that costs when the credits went to cert.

Technical artists have a specific version of this problem. The role does not
map cleanly onto a department, so it gets swept into whatever bucket the
producer building the roll finds convenient — Additional Art, or Engineering,
or a Special Thanks that reads as a favour rather than a job. I have looked at
enough credit rolls to know the shape of the thing: the further your work sits
from a named discipline, the vaguer your line.

MobyGames already allowed uncredited contributors to be added where their
involvement could be independently verified. What the new tier changes is that
there is now a direct route to it, attached to a verified identity, rather than
a community submission from a stranger. The bar stays higher than for someone
whose name is printed in the roll, which is correct and also means the people
with the thinnest paper trail — the vendor artists, the short contracts — are
still the ones who will struggle.

## Two kinds of proof, kept apart

The design detail I like: the claim flow does not let you rewrite the
structural credit data. A human process at MobyGames confirms you are the
person you say you are. Corrections to the credits themselves — the misspelled
surname, the wrong role — still go through the existing community verification
the database has always used.

That split is doing real work. Identity and accuracy are different questions
with different failure modes, and a system that lets a verified account edit
the record it is verified against is a system that fills up with polite fiction
within a year. Keeping the two apart is unglamorous and it is the reason this
might survive contact with a hundred thousand users.

The portfolio side is where the value sits for anyone reading this. Claimed
credits can carry screenshots, a reel, a description, links, and a note on what
you specifically contributed. That last field is the one that matters. A line
in a roll is a job title and a shipped product; it says nothing about whether
you built the foliage system or dressed forty rooms with someone else's kit.
Employers searching the database can filter on that. Product manager Reece
Denzel told GamesIndustry.biz at Gamescom that thousands of developers have
already claimed pages.

## The price

Moby Professional went into beta in June, with a 1.0 due this autumn. It costs
$14.99 a month, or $11.99 a month billed annually, with a 45-day trial. The
beta was free and was expected to run to the end of summer; I could not confirm
whether the paid tier has switched on yet.

Fifteen dollars a month is not much against a games salary. It is quite a lot
against no salary, which is the situation a meaningful slice of this audience
is in — [Ninja Theory closed this month with 268 more Xbox
jobs](/blog/ninja-theory-closure-xbox-268-cuts/), and that was one of several.
The record of what you built is most valuable to you at exactly the moment you
have least money, and a subscription reverses that. I do not think anyone at
MobyGames set out to design it that way. Databases cost money to run, Atari is
a public company, and the honest version of this is that nobody has worked out
who pays for the industry's memory.

What sits badly is the direction of travel. Zerply dies tomorrow. ArtStation and
Sketchfab [changed hands in August](/blog/kitbash-buys-artstation-and-sketchfab/).
The infrastructure holding the proof of an artist's career keeps being
something a company owns, and the companies keep changing their minds. A
verified credit page is a good thing to have. It is worth knowing that it now
lives behind a card on file, and worth exporting whatever else you have while
the exporting still works.
