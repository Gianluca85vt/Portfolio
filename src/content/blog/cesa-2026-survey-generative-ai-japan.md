CESA handed out a preview edition of its Video Game Industry Report 2026 at
Tokyo Game Show on 17 September, and one line from it went round the world by
the weekend: **85.8%** of Japanese game developers use generative AI at work.
Last year's figure was 51%. That is a thirty-five point jump in twelve months,
which is the kind of number that gets a headline whether or not anyone reads
the second paragraph.

The second paragraph is where the survey design lives, so let me start there.

## Two surveys, two sample sizes

The 85.8% comes from CESA's annual developer questionnaire — the one it runs
under the title *Game Developers' Employment and Career Formation* — which was
open online from May to August 2026 and closed with **1,349 valid responses**.
Inside that total, 63.0% said they use generative AI daily and 22.8% said they
use it occasionally. Add those and you get the headline.

The breakdown of *what* they use it for does not come from those 1,349 people.
It comes from a separate strand answered by **50 CESA member companies**, and
the shape of it is worth writing down in full:

- internal business operations — 32%
- supporting programming — 25%
- proposals and idea generation — 23%
- translation and localisation — 22%
- producing visuals — 16%
- QA and testing — 15%
- marketing and publicity — 14%
- scenario and text — 10%
- music and audio — 9%

Coverage is not unanimous on this point. Some outlets ran the task table as
part of the preview; at least one reported that the preview did not disclose
the most common uses at all. My reading is that both are describing different
strands of the same document — the developer survey gave a percentage and no
task list, the member-company section gave the task list. The full report lands in early
December. Right now the methodology is an inference from press summaries.

Fifty companies is a small base. Percentages off fifty move a full two points
every time one respondent changes their mind, and nobody should treat 16% and
15% as meaningfully different numbers at that size. What survives the error
bars is the ordering, and the ordering is clear enough: the top four uses are
paperwork, code assistance, pitching and translation.

## What "uses generative AI" measures

The headline cannot carry this gap. A survey question that asks
whether you use generative AI at work returns a yes from the programmer who
had Copilot finish a loop, from the producer who had a model tidy a schedule
mail into Japanese business register, and from the studio generating final
character art. Only the third is what people picture when they read 85.8%.

I have watched the same conflation happen at smaller scale in every studio
conversation about this. Somebody runs a model over the meeting notes and the
question in the room becomes whether the art department is next. The two things
share a tool and share nothing else.

The number that a working artist would want is not in this survey. How many of
those fifty companies shipped a generative asset into a build? How many used it
in blockout and threw the output away? How many ran it through an internal
review board first? CESA's managing director has been public about balancing
experimentation against copyright and creator protection, and several of the
named respondents — Capcom, Konami, Sega, Level-5 — have internal policies
requiring human review and forbidding raw output going out unchanged. That
policy layer is invisible in a yes/no question, and it is the entire difference
between a tool and a replacement.

## The benefits list is a schedule argument

The three benefits developers most often expected, in order: improved
operational efficiency and productivity, shorter development cycles, reduced
development and operational costs.

All three are arguments about time rather than quality, which matches what
Japanese studios have been saying out loud.
Akihiro Hino spent part of a Level-5 broadcast apologising for AI use and then,
in the same show, restated the ambition — [turn artwork straight into polygons
and compress five-year projects into two](/blog/level-5-ai-apology-five-to-two-plan).
That is the same sentence as "shorter development cycles", delivered by a studio
head rather than a survey form.

Whether the compression is real is a different question. Blockout and iteration
speed up. Review, integration, art direction sign-off and the long tail of
fixing what the tool got subtly wrong do not, and in my experience the last of
those eats a good share of what the first one saved. A 22% localisation figure
I believe without argument. A claim that the same tooling halves a five-year
project I would want to see land before I priced a schedule against it.

## Where the constraint comes from

The counterweight comes from contracts, and rarely from inside the art team. Saber's Space Marine 3 is shipping with
[no generative AI anywhere in it because the Games Workshop licence forbids
it](/blog/space-marine-3-no-generative-ai-games-workshop), which settles the
question before a single artist has an opinion about it.

That is the mechanism to watch over the next year, more than the adoption
percentage. Adoption is going to keep climbing — a survey that counts "did you
use it once" always climbs. What decides whether generative output reaches a
finished game is licensing, platform policy, and whichever publisher first has
to explain in court where a texture came from.

December will tell us how much of the 2026 report holds up. Until then the
honest summary of 85.8% is that most Japanese developers have opened the tool.
Almost none of what matters to an artist is in that sentence.
