On the night of 3 February 1972, prints of *Pakeezah* arrived at Bombay's Maratha Mandir in a palanquin pulled by four horses, with military bands playing outside and police holding the gates. Meena Kumari sat inside next to her husband, the film's director Kamal Amrohi. She died eight weeks later.

Fifty-two years after that, archivists opened the cans holding the film's original camera negative at Ramnord Lab and found it had almost melted. Unusable. Not degraded in the way an archivist budgets for — gone.

The 4K restoration that premiered at Festival Lumière in Lyon this month, with a UK gala at the BFI London Film Festival on 18 October, was therefore built from none of the material the film was shot on. That is the part worth sitting with, because the word "restoration" implies a return to something, and there was nothing left to return to.

Film Heritage Foundation's team worked from a 35mm interpositive the Foundation had preserved, a CinemaScope release print, and a colour reversal internegative held by the NFDC-National Film Archive of India. Accounts differ a little on the exact list — Il Cinema Ritrovato's programme notes describe an interpositive plus a 35mm intermediate reversal print from NFDC, where Variety and the Foundation's own release name all three — but every version agrees on the shape of it: duplicates, no original.

Each of those is a photographic copy of a copy. An interpositive is struck off the negative, so it sits one generation down. A release print sits further. Every optical dupe stage adds grain, softens the edge of everything in frame, compresses the contrast at both ends, and shifts the colour balance, and none of that is recoverable later. You cannot sharpen information that was never exposed.

So the 4K number describes the scanner and the pipeline, not the picture. It is the same ceiling I wrote about when [HIDIVE scanned Ninja Scroll's 35mm and found the cels themselves were the limit](/blog/ninja-scroll-4k-restoration-cel-scan/) — once you are resolving the physical structure of the source, more pixels record the source's problems in more detail. Here the source is a dupe. A 4K scan of a dupe is an extremely faithful record of a generation of loss.

Which is not an argument against doing it. It is an argument for being specific about what you got.

## Vinegar syndrome is a geometry problem

The condition report on the interpositive lists scratches, mould, halos, emulsion damage and vinegar syndrome. The last one is the interesting one technically, and it is the one that quietly decides how much work the scan is.

Acetate film base hydrolyses. It releases acetic acid, the acid catalyses more hydrolysis, and the reaction accelerates itself inside a closed can — which is why a single bad reel can take its neighbours with it. The chemistry is the famous part. The mechanical consequence is the part that lands on whoever runs the scanner: the base shrinks and warps, so the film no longer sits flat at the gate and the perforation pitch stops being constant.

A frame that is physically a slightly different size and shape from the frame before it cannot be registered with a single global transform. Every frame needs its own stabilisation and its own warp correction, derived per frame, and the correction has to be gentle enough not to start breathing across a cut. That is weeks of work that never appears in the press release, and it is the reason restoration timelines look absurd until you have watched someone do it. Two years, for this one, at L'Immagine Ritrovata in Bologna with Lionheart Cinema.

Mould is a different category again. Dirt sits on top of the image and can be cleaned or painted out from surrounding frames. Mould eats the emulsion, which means the image is not obscured, it is absent, and what fills the hole is a decision somebody made. Across a reel of it, you are no longer cleaning a film. You are repainting parts of one.

## There is nothing left to match the colour to

The internegative had faded. Of course it had.

Eastmancolor stock from that era fades unevenly, because the three dye layers do not die at the same rate — the cyan goes first and fastest, which is why so much sixties and seventies colour film ends up sitting in pink. And the fade is not a uniform offset you can invert. It varies along the roll, it varies with density, so a shadow and a highlight in the same frame have drifted by different amounts in different directions. Pulling a single curve back across that gets you closer and leaves you wrong in a way that is hard to see and impossible to prove.

The normal answer to "what should this actually look like" is a reference. In live-action CG work the reference is a chart shot on set under the same light, which is the whole reason [a render can be lit correctly and still refuse to sit in the plate](/blog/colour-charts-cg-plate-integration/) — without a measured sample, two images are sitting in different colour worlds and no amount of eyeballing closes the gap reliably.

Nobody was shooting reference charts for this on a Bombay set in the sixties. There is no surviving print anyone trusts as a reference, because the prints have faded too. So the restoration reconstructed colour from original film stills — themselves on their own stock, with their own fade — and from what Tajdar and Rukhsaar Amrohi remember their father's film looking like.

Say that out loud and it sounds like the softest possible basis for a grade. In practice it is close to the best evidence available, and the Amrohi family involvement runs deeper than a consultation: Lionheart Cinema, the restoration's production partner, is Bilal Amrohi's. But it should be labelled for what it is. The *Pakeezah* that screens in Lyon and London is an argued reading of how the film looked, made by people with good reasons, and it will become the canonical version simply by being the one that exists in 4K.

## The film was never one look anyway

Here is where it stops being a restoration story and becomes a production story.

*Pakeezah* was shot across sixteen years. Filming began in 1956 in black and white, moved to Eastmancolor, then moved again to CinemaScope, with scenes reshot as the format changed under it. Sources disagree on the exact years for those switches — the commonly repeated 1958 date for colour, and a post-*Kaagaz Ke Phool* 1959 date for the scope conversion, are not firmly sourced, and I would not state either as fact. What is not in dispute is that the material spans multiple stocks, multiple generations of anamorphic optics, and multiple lab eras.

Then Amrohi and Kumari separated in March 1964 and the production stopped. Cinematographer Josef Wirsching, who had shot it from the start, died in 1967. Shooting resumed in 1969 with other leading Indian cinematographers finishing the film while trying to hold Wirsching's lighting style together across the join.

So the question a restoration is supposed to answer — what did this look like when it was new — does not have a single answer even for the 1972 release print. It was always a composite of a decade and a half of different materials, photographed by different people, matched by hand at the time. The restoration is matching a match.

That is also the argument for doing this work on films like this one rather than only on the tidy ones. A production that went this badly leaves a mess in the elements, and the mess is where the history is.

## What the sound gets

The audio was rebuilt as a 5.1 mix from the surviving sound negative and a release print. Ghulam Mohammed's score and the film's songs are, for a great many people, the reason *Pakeezah* is still watched at all, so the sound is not a secondary deliverable here.

A 5.1 mix out of a 1972 optical track means decisions about placement that the original recording simply does not contain. There is a defensible case for it — cinema playback chains are built for it now, and a straight mono feed through a modern room has its own problems. There is also a version of this that pushes reverb and width onto a mix whose intimacy was the point. I have not heard it, and the restoration notes do not say how conservative they were, so that one is genuinely open.

What I would want on the eventual disc is both: the 5.1, and the restored mono, labelled. Restorations that hand you the choice age better than the ones that decide for you.

## One more thing worth saying

The elements that saved this film existed because somebody filed them. An interpositive sitting in a lab, an internegative in a national archive, a print — none of it was glamorous work, and the people who did it were not solving a 2026 problem.

The negative that was supposed to be the permanent record is the thing that melted.
