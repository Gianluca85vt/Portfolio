**Horizon Hunters Gathering**, Guerrilla Games' three-player co-op spin-off
of its own franchise, is being rebuilt from the inside. Bloomberg reported
on 19 August that PlayStation has spent since June stripping the
live-service elements out of the game after closed playtests this year came
back badly, and is turning it into a smaller, more traditional co-op title
with a story mode instead. GamesIndustry.biz, Game Developer and multiple
outlets picked up the same reporting the same day, all citing Bloomberg's
Jason Schreier. Guerrilla has reportedly been told to show real progress by
the end of the year, and some of the team has already been reassigned to
another project that is facing its own review.

The number that makes the retreat make sense: Hunters Gathering's Steam
playtest peaked at **561 concurrent players**, according to Steambase
tracking of the public sessions in February and May. For a live-service
game — a category whose entire business model depends on a big enough
standing population to keep matchmaking and content drops worth funding —
that is not a soft launch number. That is a "turn it off before launch"
number.

Guerrilla announced Hunters Gathering on 5 February, and the reveal was
deliberately not another photoreal Aloy adventure. It's a stylised,
cartoon-leaning art direction on top of tactical three-player co-op hunts
against machines — closer in spirit to a hero-shooter than to Forbidden
West. That art direction choice was not incidental. A cel-shaded,
graphic-novel palette is cheaper to iterate on than the mainline games'
photogrammetry-heavy realism, easier to reskin for cosmetics, and far more
forgiving of the geometry and lighting compromises that come with running
three players' worth of characters, machines and VFX on screen at once
instead of one. It is, in other words, exactly the art direction a studio
picks when it knows the game has to ship new content on a schedule for
years. I've written before about Avalanche staffing up for that same
live-service art discipline on Hogwarts Legacy 2 — modular kits built for
reuse and recombination from day one, rather than a world authored once and
polished.

## The pipeline problem is the same one, upside down

Hogwarts Legacy 2's open question was whether a studio that built its
reputation on "author one gorgeous static world" could retool for
live-ops content cadence. Hunters Gathering's problem is the reverse: an
art pipeline that was already built and staffed for exactly that cadence —
seasonal Hunter cosmetics, rotating hunt zones, matchmaking-friendly
performance budgets — now has to become something closer to a fixed,
authored campaign with a story mode, on whatever timeline gets the studio
through its year-end review.

That is not free. Modular kits designed to be endlessly recombined into
short repeatable missions read as generic and thin when a player is meant
to sit in a space long enough to care about it as a place, which is exactly
what a story mode demands. Environment art built to be cheap to reskin for
a battle pass is not the same brief as environment art built to make a
specific location memorable. None of that means the game can't work — cel
shading is a perfectly good look for a story-driven co-op game too, Guerrilla
clearly has the technical bench, and Decima is proven at authoring detailed
static spaces. But it means a chunk of tooling and asset planning that was
built around one economic model now has to justify itself under a
completely different one, on a deadline that was set by disappointing
playtest numbers rather than by the art team's own schedule.

## What I'd watch for

Whether the stylised art direction survives the pivot is the tell. If
Guerrilla keeps the cartoon look, that's a signal the reboot is mostly
about systems and pacing, and the art pipeline mostly gets repointed at new
goals. If a future showcase quietly walks the art direction back toward
something closer to mainline Horizon, that's a signal the whole live-service
premise — cheap-to-iterate look included — got junked along with the
matchmaking, and Guerrilla decided it was cheaper to start the art bible
over than to repurpose what live-service assets it already had.
