Five scored verdicts on Minecraft Dungeons II were up by the morning of 29
September, and they run from 6 to 9. IGN sits at the bottom, Destructoid at the
top, and GameSpot, GamesRadar+ and Game Informer bunch around seven. The
arithmetic mean of the five is 7.25, which rounds to **7.5**.

Three points is a wide range. What makes it strange is that the five reviews
describe more or less the same game.

## The list of improvements barely changes between them

Combat has more weight. Traversal is less of a slog. The build variety that the
2020 original ran out of somewhere around the twentieth hour now goes further.
Nobody in the five claims it reinvents the formula, and IGN's Travis Northup,
who scored it lowest, still calls it a beautiful and fun dungeon crawler — his
complaint is that it plays it extremely safe.

So the spread measures something narrower than quality. It measures how much
credit each reviewer gives a sequel for doing the same thing better.

That question had a cleaner answer here six weeks ago, when
[Mortal Shell II cleared its original by a full grade](/blog/mortal-shell-2-reviews/)
on a nearly identical strategy — sharpen the systems, replace nothing. The
scores moved as a block. Dungeons II ran the same play and the critics
scattered instead, which suggests the strategy only pays when the original left
a lot of obvious room.

## What a game made of boxes can spend a graphics budget on

Dungeons inherited a hard constraint from the thing it is spun off from.
Everything is built out of rectangles, and the camera sits far enough back that
a player character occupies a small slice of the frame — then you add three
friends and a crowd of mobs on top.

Boxes are cheap. Keeping twenty of them distinguishable on a loud screen is
not, because no asset is allowed a silhouette more complicated than a block.
The work moves into colour, contrast and motion. An enemy wind-up has to land
as a shape change or a flash, since there is no face to grimace and no blade to
catch the light. It is the same legibility problem that makes
[porting Diablo 4 to Switch 2 a real cost to the art team](/blog/diablo-4-switch-2-age-of-hatred-what-porting-costs-art/)
rather than a resolution slider — a busy ARPG screen has to stay parseable
whatever the hardware does.

Which is why "prettier" is a specific claim in a game like this, and several of
the five make it. The gains have to be in how light falls across a cube, how
fog sits in a corridor, how a hit registers. Destructoid's headline points at a
new area called the Sift as the reason for its 9, and a new dimension is the
one place a boxy game can genuinely change what it looks like: new palette, new
light, new fog.

## The number

**7.5**, from five scores between 6 and 9, collected on 29 September 2026. It
is the plain mean of what was published, converted to a ten-point scale and
rounded to the nearest half.

Five is a small sample on release morning and the figure will move this week.
The aggregate numbers circulating — a Metacritic in the mid-seventies, an
OpenCritic in the mid-eighties — sit far enough apart that neither is worth
leaning on until the critic lists fill in, so I have left both out of the
arithmetic.

The range is the part to carry away. A sequel can be competently made, agreed
on by everyone who played it, and still leave two experienced reviewers three
points apart on whether the improvements add up to a reason to buy it.
