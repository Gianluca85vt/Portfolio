Konami let the Townfall verdicts out early. The radar had the embargo pencilled
for the 22nd and the reviews were up before that, which is how these things go
when a publisher likes what it is reading. Across the eleven scored verdicts I
could verify on the morning of 21 September, the average is **8.0**, and the
scores run from 6 to 10.

The aggregators counted far more than I did — OpenCritic is showing 82 from 37
critics, Metacritic 81. Those numbers and mine agree, which is reassuring and
also slightly boring. The part worth twenty minutes is what all these reviews
say in the same breath.

## They all wrote the same sentence about the air

GamesRadar called the atmosphere impeccably crafted. DualShockers went further
and put it among the best in the entire franchise. PC Gamer's verdict was that
Screen Burn understands what makes a Silent Hill game. Collider, stopping short
of the second and third games, still called it the strongest attempt yet to
stand next to them. GameSpot filed it as the series completing its comeback.

Eleven verdicts, and the ones at 7 are not disputing any of that. Cultura
Geek's 6 is the lowest number I found and it is not a review that says the game
looks or sounds wrong.

So St Amelia — a Scottish town, 1996, which is a setting nobody in this series
had used — landed. That is an environment team and an audio team going eleven
for eleven in a week when the scores otherwise spread across four points.

## The four points are about stealth

VGC put it in the headline: outstanding horror, marred by infuriating stealth.
It is the recurring complaint, and it is doing most of the work in the gap
between the 9s and the 7s. Townfall leans on avoidance and puzzles rather than
fighting, and how much of the game is spent hiding is the thing reviewers
weighed differently.

That is a systems argument sitting on top of art that everyone likes, and this
blog watched the same shape play out when Control Resonant's verdicts landed on
Friday and [its environments drew near-unanimous praise while the rest of it
split eight ways](/blog/control-resonant-review/). Two games, three days apart,
both of them with the art department well clear of the argument.

## Fog and sightlines want opposite things

Here is the bit I cannot stop chewing on, and I want to be clear that it is a
reading rather than something a reviewer said.

Fog has done two jobs in this series since 1999. It sets the mood, and it hides
how little of the town the hardware is drawing. That second job is the famous
one. When [Townfall showed its first-person footage in August I wondered what
the fog would be covering this
time](/blog/silent-hill-townfall-fog-first-person-shift/), because a camera at
eye height changes the maths — you lose the third-person crutch where the
player reads threats from outside their own body, and everything the player
knows now has to arrive through a single head-mounted lens.

Stealth needs the player to hold a model of who can see them. Where the guard
is facing, how far the cone reaches, which wall breaks the line. You build that
model by looking, and you trust it because looking told you the truth. Volumetric
fog is the single best tool in the kit for denying exactly that. It is doing its
job beautifully when you cannot tell whether the shape at the end of the street
has noticed you.

Which means the thing earning the unanimous praise and the thing drawing the
word "infuriating" may well be the same system, tuned once, pulling in two
directions. You can tune around it — occlusion that thins near a patrol, audio
that carries what the eye cannot, a tell that reads through haze. That work is
fiddly and it is invisible when it succeeds.

I have no way to know whether Screen Burn spent that time or spent it somewhere
else. But when a horror game gets praised for how little you can see and
criticised for a mechanic that depends on seeing, the two complaints are
probably neighbours.

## The number

**8.0**, the arithmetic mean of eleven published scores between 6 and 10,
converted to a 1-10 scale and rounded to the nearest half point.

Eleven against the 37 the aggregators counted is a thin sample and a sample
taken hours after the gates opened, so read it as an early figure. It happens to
land within a fifth of a point of OpenCritic's, which suggests I have not picked
a weird eleven.

The four-point range is narrower than the six points that Control Resonant
opened up last week, and it is telling you something simpler: most people liked
this, a few found the hiding tedious, and none of them thought the town was the
problem. If you bounced off the stealth in the last horror game you played, that
is the axis to go reading about before Wednesday.
