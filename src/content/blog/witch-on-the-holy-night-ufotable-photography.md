Aniplex ran an information programme for the *Witch on the Holy Night* film on
30 September and came out of it with the three things people were waiting for:
a 90-second final trailer, a theme song, and — at last — a staff list. The
film opens in Japan on **20 November 2026**, with a global theatrical run
following in January 2027.

The theme is supercell's, with Nagi Yanagi singing; outlets covering the
stream noted it is the first new supercell song she has sung in roughly
fifteen years, which loops back to the visual novel's own music. That is the
headline most places ran with.

The line in the staff list that actually tells you what the film will look
like is further down. Director of photography: **Yuichi Terao**.

## Photography, in the anime sense

Outside Japan the credit reads like a mistake. There is no camera on an anime
production. There hasn't been film in the path for twenty-odd years.

The word survives from when there was. The photography stage used to mean an
optical bench: cels stacked over a background, lights above and below, filters
and diffusion gels in between, and a camera shooting the stack one frame at a
time. Fog, glows, flares, the halo around a lamp — all of it was done there,
physically, by the people operating the rig. When the whole industry moved to
digital compositing in the late nineties the department kept its name and
inherited the job.

So the director of photography on a modern anime is the head of compositing.
Every glow, every depth-of-field falloff, every grade, every particle of
atmosphere, every bit of grain goes through that desk. On most productions it
is a finishing stage. On a ufotable production it is closer to the main event.

Terao joined the studio in December 2003 and has been its lead on photography
for the run that made the house look famous: *Fate/Zero*, *Unlimited Blade
Works*, the *Heaven's Feel* films, *Demon Slayer* and its features. If you
have ever watched a ufotable fight and thought the image seemed lit rather
than drawn, that is his department.

## What a visual novel actually hands you

Here is why that credit is more interesting on this film than on another
*Demon Slayer*.

*Mahoutsukai no Yoru* is a Type-Moon visual novel from 2012. A visual novel's
images barely move. Character art sits on a painted background and the drama
is carried by things a compositor does: a colour shift as the sun goes down,
a rack focus onto a face, a slow push across a still, rain layered over the
plate, the whole frame going cold when someone stops being friendly. Mahoyo
was admired for doing a lot of that — people called it the best-looking thing
Type-Moon had made, and they were describing effects work over static art.

Which means the adaptation problem is not the usual one. Normally you take a
manga, with its drawn motion and its panel grammar, and you work out how much
animation you can afford. Here the source is already a compositing-led piece
of visual storytelling. The grammar transfers almost directly. The job is to
take a language built out of filters and camera moves over stills and make it
work at twenty-four frames a second.

Putting the studio's strongest compositing lead on it is the obvious call, and
it is the one they made.

## The part that could go wrong

The glow is also the tell.

ufotable's photography pass is heavy. Diffusion, bloom, atmospheric haze, a
soft falloff on nearly every edge — it is gorgeous on a sword fight in a
burning building, and it tends to treat every surface the same way. Line art
loses its tooth under it. Texture flattens. A frame that should feel like
paper feels like glass.

*Mahoutsukai no Yoru* is mostly two girls and a boy in a Western-style mansion
in a snowbound town, talking. Long stretches of it are interior dialogue.
Dialogue scenes are where a house style gets exposed, because there is no
spectacle sitting on top to distract from it.

There is a version of this film where the photography department does the
subtle work — lamp light that behaves like lamp light, snow outside a window
that reads as cold rather than as a particle layer — and a version where every
shot gets the same silk. The trailer suggests the first. Trailers are cut by
people who know which shots are finished.

Worth putting next to what [a shadow costs when the sheet itself blocks the
light](/blog/eri-honami-yano-ottawa-cel-shadows/), where Honami Yano paints on
transparent cel and the darkness in the frame is a physical event rather than
a layer. Two opposite answers to the same question about where the image
actually gets made.

And the optical bench that the word "photography" is a fossil of has its own
ceiling, which is roughly [what a 4K scan of 35mm cel animation runs
into](/blog/ninja-scroll-4k-restoration-cel-scan/): past a point you are
resolving the film stock and the dust on the glass instead of the drawing.

## The rest of the list

Masashi Takeuchi directs, with Hikaru Kondo as chief director. Moe Tsuzuki on
character design, Masato Nagamori as chief animation director, Koji Eto on art
direction, Hideyuki Fukasawa on music. Takeuchi's most-cited prior credit is
episode direction on *Mugen Train*, which is a reasonable place to learn how
this studio's pipeline fits together.

Japan gets it on 20 November. Everyone else waits until January.
