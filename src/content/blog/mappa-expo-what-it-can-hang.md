MAPPA turned fifteen this year and put the evidence in a museum. *MAPPA EXPO 15th
Anniversary* has been at the Yurakucho Museum in Tokyo since **16 September** and
closes on **7 December**, with further venues to follow, and Anime News Network
walked the second phase of it this week. Twenty-three productions. Original
drawings, cels, storyboards, seven illustrations drawn for the show and sold
nowhere else. A life-size Bomb, lit from inside, in the *Chainsaw Man* room.

I have not been, and I am not going to write as though I had. What I can read is
the inventory, and an inventory is a statement. It tells you what a studio
believes its work is made of.

## Paper is the part that frames

The three headline areas are built on production drawings. The *Chainsaw Man – The
Movie: Reze Arc* room traces Denji and Reze from the first meeting to the fight
with Bomb through the drawings that got them there. The *Jujutsu Kaisen* area
revisits the big fights the same way. *Attack on Titan: The Final Season* puts up
original drawings for key scenes and, by Time Out Tokyo's account, the
hand-painted watercolour of the hill from the ending.

That watercolour is the thing I would walk past twice. A background is one of the
last pieces of an anime pipeline that gets authored *complete* — someone paints
it, and what they painted is very nearly what reaches the screen. A key animation
drawing is not like that. A genga sheet is pencil, timing notes and corrections,
handed downstream to be cleaned up, scanned, digitally painted, re-weighted,
filtered and lit. It is a brilliant object and it is an instruction, not a result.

Which is why an exhibition of production materials from a cel-era film works so
cleanly. When I wrote about the Ghost in the Shell retrospective in August, the
point was that [a layout sheet shows you the decision where a still only shows
the result](/blog/ghost-in-the-shell-exhibition-backgrounds) — and on a 1995
production, the painted board and the cel laid on top of it *were* the frame.
Hang the board, hang the cel, you have hung the film. Thirty years later the
relationship between what hangs on a wall and what appears on a screen has got a
lot longer.

## The part that does not go behind glass

Ask anyone who watches a lot of modern television anime to describe MAPPA and you
will get answers about surface: the heat shimmer and smoke in *Chainsaw Man*, the
grain, the way a light source blooms, fights that keep moving the camera through
real space instead of panning across a held cel. Almost none of that is decided
on paper. It is decided in 撮影 — satsuei, the photography department, which in a
digital studio means compositing — and in layout, where somebody works out where
the camera sits and how it travels before a drawing exists.

I have made this argument on this blog about another studio: ufotable's identity
sits almost entirely in [the compositing credit most people scroll
past](/blog/witch-on-the-holy-night-ufotable-photography), the department that
holds three separate light sources in a night exterior rather than dropping one
flat key over everything. MAPPA is a different house with a different look and the
same structural fact. The drawing is the performance. The composite is the
surface, and the surface is what people mean when they say a show looks
expensive.

You cannot put that in a vitrine. You could put up a layer breakdown — line art,
paint, shadow pass, diffusion, grade, the lot, stepped out — and I would stand in
front of it for twenty minutes. Most visitors would read it as a diagram and keep
walking. I understand entirely why that is not the exhibition anybody builds.

## So the showpieces got built as sets

What this expo does instead is interesting, and I think it is the correct
instinct. The things being photographed and shared are not framed pages. They are
objects: the lit Bomb; a life-size Yuji Itadori; a recreated train interior with
three-dimensional pieces and video around it; and in the Final Season room a
large-scale installation of the Rumbling, which Metropolis describes as visuals
and 3D displays and nippon.com as a model.

Those choices track almost exactly onto the sequences where MAPPA's work is a
camera problem rather than a drawing problem. A fight staged inside a train
carriage is a blocking exercise in a confined volume. The Rumbling is scale,
crowd and perspective. Both are the kind of shot where a drawn production reaches
for a three-dimensional layout — either a punishing amount of perspective genga or
a rough 3D set the drawn layer rides on, and from the footage I would bet
heavily on the second. Rebuilding those as physical spaces is a reasonable
translation. A room communicates a camera move. A stack of paper does not.

And the Final Season area apparently carries displays explaining the production
process, which is the one place in the listings where somebody tried to show the
middle of the pipeline rather than its two ends. If that is accurate, it is the
most unusual thing in the building.

The anniversary movie is worth watching for the same reason the expo is worth a
ticket, and it also demonstrates the problem. It
is a compilation piece cut across fifteen years of the studio's catalogue, and
what holds it together is grading and compositing — a consistent treatment laid
over material drawn by dozens of different teams in different years. That
treatment is the studio's signature. It exists in no single drawing on any wall
in Yurakucho.

The show rotated part of its contents when the second phase opened on **28
October**, which means the version Anime News Network walked is not the version
somebody saw in September, and the touring legs will presumably be a third thing
again. Fifteen years is a lot to fit in one museum. I suspect what gets dropped
each time is never the Bomb.
