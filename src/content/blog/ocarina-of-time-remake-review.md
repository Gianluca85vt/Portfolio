Reviews for The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time went up on 4 November 2026,
the day before the Switch 2 remake shipped. Nine of them, converted to a
ten-point scale, average 9.5. The spread is one point — 9 at the bottom, 10 at
the top, nothing below. Metacritic sat at 96 on launch day across seventy-odd
critics, which made it the best-reviewed game of 2026, and OpenCritic has it at
95 across 69.

A one-point range on nine verdicts is unusual enough to be worth saying out
loud. Phantom Blade Zero managed a four-point spread last month. Critics
disagreeing is normally where a review round-up gets interesting, and here they
do not disagree about anything except how much ambition they were owed.

The comparison everyone reached for is the 1998 original's 99, still the only
99 Metacritic has ever recorded. That number rests on 22 reviews. The remake's
96 rests on roughly four times as many, which is not the same measurement
taken twice, and the gap between them says more about how many outlets existed
in 1998 than about either game.

## The two things I wanted pinned down

Before launch I wrote about [the team that missed 1998](/blog/ocarina-of-time-remake-devs-never-played-it/)
— Aonuma handing the revision decisions to staff with no memory of the N64
release — and ended it with two production questions. What happens to Hyrule
Field, whose emptiness started life as a draw-distance budget and turned into
the game's sense of scale. And how you load a voice performance onto faces that
were, in 1998, a texture on a flat plane.

The reviews answer one and a half.

On the field: Video Games Chronicle says the remake sticks closely to the
original's design, unlike the other big nineties remakes of recent years, and
Forbes reports much of the level design kept intact. Vooks found some dungeon
puzzles and room layouts altered, which is the limit of it. So the field is
still the field. Nobody I read treats its emptiness as a problem, and nobody
says it was re-lit either, so whether anyone made a decision about it or simply
left it alone is still open.

On the faces: they limited the problem. Nintendo Life describes voice acting in
key cutscenes rather than throughout, and rates it among the better first-party
Nintendo performances in a while. That is a scoping answer and a good one. You
do not rig every NPC in Kakariko for lip sync. You pick the two dozen shots
that carry the story, build real facial rigs for those, and leave the rest of
the cast doing what they did before.

## What they did change

Forbes lists a free-form camera and a real jump button. Both of those are
bigger jobs than they sound on a game designed around their absence. The 1998
jump was contextual because the N64 pad had four C buttons and one stick, and
every ledge in the game was authored knowing the player could not miss. Put a
jump button on it and somebody has to walk the whole geometry again checking
what is now reachable.

The camera is the same kind of work. Z-targeting existed partly because there
was no second stick to look around with, and every corridor in every dungeon
was framed for a camera that behaved a particular way. Hand the player free
look and you are re-checking sightlines in rooms that were composed as
single-angle stages.

Combat stayed as it was, per Forbes. The interface went to the Breath of the
Wild and Tears of the Kingdom item wheel, per VGC.

## Caution is what an outsourced spec looks like

Here is the part that interests me, and it connects to the thing that came out
four days after launch. [Bandai Namco made the art](/blog/ocarina-of-time-remake-bandai-namco-art/)
— characters, terrain, animations, cutscenes and UI, by its own account, with
Monolith Soft on game design, programming and graphics.

Think about what you have to write down to commission that. A spec precise
enough that another company can build Hyrule Field and have it come back
looking like Hyrule Field is, by necessity, a description of a game that
already exists. Every element you want reconsidered is an element you cannot
specify, because nobody knows yet what it should be. So the brief describes
1998, closely, and the conservatism the reviews flag is baked into the
procurement before an artist touches it.

This is procurement doing what procurement does. Nintendo wanted a canonical
text rebuilt at modern fidelity by partners, and wanting that rules out the
version where the dungeons get re-thought.

Nintendo Life's verdict — stunning, not quite definitive — is the one I would
have filed. No Master Quest, and a remake of the most canonised 3D game ever
made that declines to argue with it.

The 9.5 is real. Nine critics, nobody under 9, and a game that looks better
than anything else on the hardware. Whether a remake this faithful was the
thing worth spending a hundred artist-years on is a different question, and
the scores were never going to answer it.
