*Cyberpunk: Edgerunners 2* arrives on Netflix on 20 October — tomorrow, midnight
Pacific, so breakfast in Europe — and all ten episodes arrive together. It is a
standalone story with a new cast. Nobody needs the 2022 season first.

The name worth stopping on is the director's. **Kai Ikarashi** is running this,
his first series as director. Before this he worked on the first *Edgerunners*
and on *SSSS.Gridman*, both at Trigger, and the credit everybody points to is
the sixth episode of season one, "Girl on Fire." That is the episode he got
hired off.

## Promoting an episode director is a real bet

Twenty-four minutes of television and four hours of it are different jobs, and
the difference is not stamina.

An episode of an anime series is where a studio concentrates. One director, one
animation director, a storyboard that one person has thought about for months,
and a slot in the schedule the other eleven episodes are quietly subsidising.
The episode that makes people say "this show looks incredible" is very often the
episode that got the budget and the best key animators, and you can usually find
the two episodes either side of it that paid for it.

Running the whole series is the opposite problem. You set a drawing standard
that has to survive being executed by people you did not hand-pick, in weeks you
are not in the room for, on cuts that are not yours. The look stops being
something you achieve and becomes something you specify — model sheets, colour
scripts, rules about what the line does when the camera moves. Plenty of
brilliant episode directors have never made that transition work. Some never get
asked twice.

Trigger clearly thinks he will. I have no inside knowledge of why, but the
structure of the decision tells you something: they picked the person whose one
episode already demonstrated the register they want for ten, rather than hiring a
safer pair of hands and asking for that register as a note.

## The design reset costs more than it looks

Ichigo Kanno is lead character designer, and has said the sequel adopts a
different aesthetic from the first season because it is about something different.
Ikarashi has said the designs come off 90s comics. Showrunner **Bartosz Sztybor**
— back from season one as writer and producer — has pitched the tone as 90s
cinema, naming *Pulp Fiction* and *Léon*.

Behind all of it sits a large, unglamorous bill.

A sequel with the same cast in the same city is the cheap sequel: every model
sheet, every colour key, every background plate of a street you already built is
sitting on a server, and reusing it is most of what a second season is for. New
cast plus a declared aesthetic change throws that away on purpose. New designs,
new colour, new line weight, and a background library that cannot simply be
pulled forward because the new look will not sit on top of the old plates.

They had the cheap option and did not take it. That is the part I find
encouraging, and also the part that makes the schedule worth watching.

## Two ways to build the same city

Night City has now been made twice, by two companies, in two media, and the
methods have almost nothing in common.

CD PROJEKT RED built it as geometry you can walk into. Every building, advert and
piece of litter placed by hand, at [twice the texel density of The Witcher 3](/blog/night-city-hand-built-texel-density/),
because a first-person camera can be brought to within thirty centimetres of any
surface in the game and the texture has to hold.

Trigger does not have that constraint and does not pay that cost. An anime
background has to hold up at one framing, for the length of one cut, at the
resolution it is composited at. Which frees the drawing to be about mood rather
than coverage — and it is why season one's Night City felt more oppressive than
the game's, while containing a fraction of the actual content.

What nobody has said is whether any of CDPR's data crosses over. The studios have
worked together since 2020 and the obvious move would be using game geometry as
layout reference for camera moves through the city. I have seen no confirmation
of that either way, and I would rather say so than assume it.

Worth noting what Trigger is not doing, though: this is not the Studio Orange
route, where [3D models get bent and dropped to a lower frame rate to read as
hand-drawn](/blog/studio-orange-3d-anime-2d-look/). Trigger's answer to making
cyberpunk hardware look convincing has always been to draw it, accept the cost,
and put the money in the cuts that matter.

## Ten at once is a production decision, not a marketing one

The batch drop gets discussed as a bingeing question. From the other side of the
desk it is a schedule question, and the answer is in the studio's favour.

A weekly broadcast series ships episodes while later ones are still being made.
That is how an episode goes out with unfinished cuts, and how a show that opened
strong ends up with a recap episode in week nine. A season delivered complete has
every episode finished before the first one streams, which means an episode in
trouble can be fixed by taking time from one that is ahead.

It does not guarantee anything. It removes the specific failure mode that eats
most television animation, and *Edgerunners* got the same deal in 2022 and came
out looking like it.

Tomorrow we find out whether one episode's worth of talent scales to ten.
