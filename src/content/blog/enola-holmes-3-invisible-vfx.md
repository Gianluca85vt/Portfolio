Vine FX put out its VFX breakdown reel for *Enola Holmes 3* this week,
covering the 150 shots it delivered for the film — one of four vendors on
the project alongside Untold Studios, beloFX and Company 3. The reel leads,
reasonably, with the flashy stuff: sequences where Enola's thought process
gets visualized on screen, letters and symbols lifting off a page and
reassembling in mid-air as she works through a coded message. That's the
part every trade write-up is going to lead with too.

The shot I keep thinking about is quieter. Enola finds a coded message
hidden in the smudge marks left on a mirror — and those smudges were built
from **real fingerprint references**, layered so they stay legible as
Morse code up close while still reading as an ordinary dirty mirror at a
normal viewing distance. That's a much harder authoring problem than it
sounds, and it's one I've run into from a completely different direction.

## Two things at once is the whole job

Any texture or prop detail on a production has to survive two different
readings at once: the glance and the close-up. A dirty window, a scuffed
wall, a fingerprint-smeared mirror — in the wide shot it just needs to sell
"this surface has been touched." That's the easy 90% of the work, and it's
mostly noise: grime dictionaries, smear brushes, a bit of directional
smudging so it reads as motion rather than a stamp.

What Vine FX had to do is the hard 10%: make that same surface hold up
under a hero close-up where the *specific pattern* of the dirt is the
plot. The smudges can't just look like fingerprints in general — they have
to resolve, on inspection, into dot-dash groupings a character can actually
translate on screen. That means the detail has to be authored with a code
underneath it, not scattered procedurally and hoped into legibility. You
can fake general grime with noise. You cannot fake a message.

I've hit a smaller version of this on prop and set-dressing work: a label,
a gauge, a scratched serial number that needs to read as real texture from
ten feet and as actual information from two feet. The instinct that fixes
it is always the same — stop treating the detail as decoration applied on
top of the surface, and start treating the surface as the thing that
carries the information, with the "worn" pass built to preserve legibility
rather than obscure it. Real fingerprint references get you photographic
believability. Placing them so the code still reads is the actual
craft.

## The other half of the reel is deliberately boring

The rest of Vine FX's work on the film is the kind of VFX that's designed
to be invisible: window replacements and bluescreen composites, including
the view outside Sherlock Holmes' office in a Victorian London that no
longer exists to shoot on location. That's set-extension work in the most
literal sense — matching period lighting, period glazing, period
atmosphere, well enough that nobody watching the finished film registers
it as an effect at all.

The set piece that bridges the "invisible" work and the showier work is a
mine explosion. Production shot a practical blast on set, and Vine FX used
**LiDAR scans of the set** to build a digital version they could blow apart
at a scale the practical explosion couldn't safely reach — matching the
live-action plate for lighting and material, then taking the destruction
further than pyrotechnics alone would allow. Scanning the set before you
break it, so the digital double you build afterward matches down to the
specific geometry that was actually standing there, is the same logic as
the fingerprint smudges: the fake only works because it's built from a real
reference of the specific thing, not a generic stand-in for the category of
thing.

*Enola Holmes 3* is directed by Philip Barantini — taking over from Harry
Bradbeer, who directed the first two films — and released on Netflix on 1
July 2026. Vine FX's team was led by VFX supervisor Ben Hicks and CG
supervisor Tim Kilgour, working under production VFX supervisor Mike
Ellis.
