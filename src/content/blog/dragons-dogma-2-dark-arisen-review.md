Capcom's expansion to Dragon's Dogma 2 arrived today, 9 October, after an early
Steam unlock the evening before, and it arrived carrying the weight of the base
game's reception. Dragon's Dogma 2 shipped in 2024 to an 86 on Metacritic and a
6.5 from users — one of the widest critic-player gaps of that year. Dark Arisen
is the answer to it.

Across the eight scored verdicts I could verify today, the average is **8.5**.
The range runs from 7 to 10. OpenCritic has the expansion at 85 from 34
critics with 91 percent recommending; Metacritic sits at 84 from 21, all
positive or mixed, none negative. Both figures as of 9 October, both still
moving.

A three-point spread on an expansion is unusual, and it is not spread evenly
across the thing. Read the low scores and the high ones next to each other and
they agree about the new region, the story, and the combat. They disagree about
one system.

## The slot machine in the armoury

The expansion adds a loot loop. You find items in the new content, pay
Rift Crystals to appraise them, and get Relic gear with randomised passive
effects on top. Push Square, which rates the expansion Excellent, still says
the equipment grind will not be for everyone. RPGamer's 3.5 out of 5 points at
repetition and pacing. Screen Rant's 7 is the floor of the sample.

From an art pipeline, that system is a strange thing to add to this game in
particular. Dragon's Dogma 2 authors its armour as discrete sets: a cuirass has
a modelled buckle, a weight that reads in the walk cycle, a material response
that somebody tuned against the game's physically-based lighting. There are not
many of those, and that scarcity is the point — recognising a piece of armour
on another player's pawn is part of how the Rift works socially.

Randomised affixes undo that. Once the same cuirass exists in forty numerical
variants, the player stops reading it as an object and starts reading it as a
roll. The appraisal screen becomes the real interface and the armour becomes its
icon. Diablo can afford that because its armour is authored to be read at
thirty degrees above the character in a 2-inch silhouette. Dogma's is authored
to be read from behind, in third person, at walking pace.

Capcom knew the complaint was coming, judging by how the expansion was pitched:
VGC reported in June that it was built "based on wide feedback to the main
game". Feedback asked for a reason to keep playing after the dragon. This is the
cheap way to provide one.

## Norgan is doing engineering work

The new region is a northern tundra. Reviewers describe whiteouts, and the
Steam pre-order bonus is called the Norgan Fashion Set, which is where the name
comes from. MP1st filed its review under "Into the Tundra".

Snow and fog in an open world are almost always a budget instrument. A whiteout
is a fog volume with a particle layer, and what it buys you is the right to stop
drawing things. Cut the far plane to eighty metres and the LOD chain behind it
never runs; the vegetation instancer never populates; the pawn crowd that made
Vermund's cities expensive has nothing to render at distance. You get atmosphere
and a frame budget from one decision.

Making it frightening rather than merely cheap is the harder version of the
trick. Reviewers put the new region's best moments there: losing the horizon,
then walking into something that was already standing in it. Whether the whiteout is dynamic
or scripted to specific stretches of the map, nobody has said yet, and it makes
a large difference to what the effect cost to build.

## The Switch 2 version is the technical story

Dark Arisen is also Dragon's Dogma 2's arrival on Switch 2, and Capcom's own
investor announcement frames the expansion and the new platform as one move to
broaden the game's audience. That matters because of what Dragon's Dogma 2 was
in 2024: a game that fell to the low thirties in its capital on a PS5, pinned
there by CPU work rather than by pixels. Every NPC in Vernworth ran a schedule.

The producer told Eurogamer that the Switch 2 build holds a minimum of 30fps
and often runs higher, and that performance was exceeding the team's
expectations. Base frame rate is 30 in both handheld and docked, with frame
generation producing what Capcom calls a 60fps-equivalent feel.

Which frame generation is an open question right now. Vice reported AMD's FSR
frame generation, which would make this the first Switch 2 title to use it;
other coverage describes the pipeline around DLSS, which is what Switch 2's
Nvidia silicon natively offers. Capcom has not published internal render
resolutions for either mode. Until it does, or until somebody frame-counts it
properly, treat the specifics as unsettled.

What is not unsettled is the shape of the trade. Generated frames interpolate
what the renderer already drew; they do not make the simulation tick faster and
they do not shorten the path from your thumb to a parry. Dogma's combat is built
on reading a wind-up and answering inside a window. A 30fps simulation
presenting at 60 gives you a smoother picture of the same 33-millisecond
decision grid.

That is a different bargain from the one CD Projekt struck, where
[Witcher 3 drops to 720p handheld to pay for its new lighting](/blog/witcher-3-remastered-switch-2-720p-lighting-budget/).
Spending resolution on image quality is a trade the player can see and judge.
Spending latency on apparent smoothness is one most players will feel without
being able to name.

Capcom is in the middle of [rebuilding the RE Engine so AI can drive its
tools](/blog/capcom-rex-project-re-engine-ai/), and the unglamorous half of that
project — one data structure instead of a decade of bespoke formats — is the
half that makes a four-year-old open world fit on a handheld at all. Dark Arisen
is what the plumbing buys.
