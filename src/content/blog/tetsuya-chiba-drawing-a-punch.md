A punch in a still drawing has a problem. The moment of contact is the moment
with the least information in it: two bodies touching, no displacement, nothing
yet moved. Film gets round this with blur, a shaking camera, a sound cut. A
page has none of that. A page has ink that does not move and a reader whose eye
travels at a speed you only partly control.

Tetsuya Chiba spent fifty years solving that, and he solved it the way an
animator would.

He died on 22 October, aged 87, in a hospital in Tokyo. The news stayed private
for five days: on the 27th a statement went up in his wife's name as chief
mourner, the publishers began posting tributes, and NHK reported the cause as
myelodysplastic syndrome. Anime News Network carried the death that day and a
second round of industry condolences the day after. Born in Tokyo in January 1939, he spent his childhood in
Manchuria and came back to Japan with his family after the war. He turned
professional in 1956. In 2024 the Japanese government gave him the Order of
Culture, reported at the time as the first award of it to a manga artist.

The work everyone knows is Ashita no Joe, which ran in Kodansha's Weekly Shōnen
Magazine from the first issue of 1968 to the middle of 1973. Ikki Kajiwara
wrote it under the name Asao Takamori. Chiba drew it. Twenty volumes of a boy
learning to box and being dismantled by it.

Open a Chiba fight and count how often the actual connection is the big
drawing. It mostly is not. The big drawing is the frame before — a heel
screwing into the canvas, hips already rotated ahead of the shoulder, a fist
still back and loaded. Or it is the frame after, the head already gone, the arm
through and past its target, the loser's feet leaving the floor in the wrong
order.

That is anticipation and follow-through. Any animator has them drilled in, and
the reason they are drilled in is that they survive being still. A pose that is
loaded tells you about a force that has not happened yet. A pose that is
wrecked tells you about a force that has. Contact itself tells you almost
nothing, which is why it so often gets a small panel or no panel at all.

Chiba also used layout as timing. A panel that runs the full width of the page
is a long beat and you read it slowly, because your eye has further to go. Four
narrow panels stacked together are fast, because they are over before you have
settled. Nobody hands a mangaka a frame rate. He authored the reading speed
into the geometry of the page, which is the same job a dope sheet does for a
scene and considerably harder to revise.

## A body that keeps the damage

The harder thing he did was let the figure deteriorate.

Games and animation both run on the opposite assumption. You build a character
once, you approve a model sheet or a mesh, and then you protect it, because
every shot and every scene has to match. Damage arrives as a decal pass, a
swapped texture, a bandage prop. The underlying proportions are not allowed to
move, and for good reason — the moment they do, nothing in your library lines
up any more.

Chiba moved them. Over the serialisation, Joe's face thickens and the planes of
it change, a fighter's skull rather than a boy's. Weight-cutting hollows him
out. By the end the figure is carrying its own history, and the cost of that is
exactly what you would predict: volume one and volume twenty are not drawings
of the same person, and no model sheet could have reconciled them.

A decision about material made at the drawing desk is a decision about what any
production downstream will be able to do, which is the pattern in
[a mangaka's choice of material deciding which pipelines can follow her](/blog/cosmic-mimimi-ichikawa-after-the-gems/)
— gems render cheaply, soft bodies do not. Chiba picked a sport whose whole
subject is a body being destroyed, and every adaptation since has had to find a
way to animate attrition.

## The line itself

Then there is the brush. Chiba's contour thickens where a limb is bearing load
and thins where it is swinging free, and the variation is not decoration. It is
a stress diagram, drawn freehand at speed, telling you which parts of the body
are currently holding the weight.

This is the thing a 3D pipeline has chased for a decade and still mostly
approximates. An outline shader gives you width from curvature, or from depth,
or from a vertex attribute you painted by hand — and getting a line that
reports *effort* means driving that width from something nobody in the rig
knows about. Worth reading next to what it takes to
[rebuild a hand-inked line as geometry](/blog/spider-verse-inklines-grease-pencil-geometry-nodes/):
the machinery required to fake what a loaded brush does by accident is
genuinely large.

## Readers held a funeral

One measure of how well the drawing worked: when Rikiishi, Joe's rival, died
in the ring in 1970, a real memorial service was held for him in Tokyo, with
the playwright Shūji Terayama involved in staging it. People in mourning
clothes, for a character. Readers do not do that for a design. They do it when
the figure on the page has been taking damage in front of them for two years
and the damage was drawn as though it mattered.

And the last page everybody remembers is a quiet one. Joe slumped in his
corner, spent, the drawing itself going pale — the phrase that attached itself
to it in Japan is that he burnt out pure white. After twenty volumes of
engineering impact, the ending is a man sitting down and the ink draining out
of the panel. Subtraction, held for as long as it takes to turn the page.
