**Black Pony Immersive went public on 19 August**, in an exclusive interview with
GamesIndustry.biz, followed same-day and next-day by Game Developer, GamesBeat,
Engadget, PC Gamer, Kotaku and a dozen others. Harvey Smith — Arkane Austin's
studio director, and the designer behind Dishonored and Prey going back to
Deus Ex — has started a new studio with fellow Arkane veteran Ben Horne. Twenty-six
people, based in Austin with remote staff across the US, backed by a publisher
nobody's named yet. First project: unannounced, single-player, what Smith
himself calls, in the GamesBeat interview, "an action RPG or FPS-RPG hybrid or
an immersive sim, whatever" — he's clearly tired of the genre-label argument
and I don't blame him.

I wrote yesterday about **Warren Spector's retirement** and what immersive sim
level design actually costs to build at modern fidelity. This is the same
conversation from the other side: not a veteran leaving the genre, but two
veterans trying to keep it alive after the studio that made them famous for it
got shut down.

## What actually got closed in May 2024

Microsoft closed Arkane Austin as part of a wider Bethesda restructuring,
laying off 96 people according to the WARN notice filed at the time — reported
by the Austin Business Journal, the Austin American-Statesman and Austin
Chronicle among others. That studio didn't just make Dishonored and Prey. It
built and maintained the *pipeline* that made those games' environments work:
modular kit-bashing systems tuned for reuse across multiple traversal routes,
lighting workflows built around player-triggered state changes rather than
fixed camera beats, and a level-design-to-environment-art handoff where the
blockout and the final dressing pass were expected to survive several rounds
of systemic redesign without a full rebuild. Try handing that to a new hire as a document. It is tribal knowledge that lives
in a team, and when
the team is laid off, the knowledge scatters with it.

## "Well-realized worlds" is a production sentence, not a marketing one

Black Pony's mission statement, quoted by GamesIndustry.biz, promises "narrative-rich
games set in well-realized worlds, where players create their own memorable
experiences with expressive, emergent game systems." Read that as a producer
would and it's fine, if generic. Read it as an environment artist and it's a
commitment to the exact cost structure I wrote about yesterday: every space
has to hold up lit, dressed and collision-tested along routes the player
invents rather than the ones a script anticipates. That's expensive at any
headcount. At 26 people, without an existing shared toolset to fall back on,
it's the whole risk of the studio.

What's genuinely interesting is that Smith is framing the smallness as an
advantage rather than a constraint. He told GamesIndustry.biz the studio
doesn't have to "seek much approval from anyone else," which makes it "very
nimble" — and nimble, for an environment team rebuilding a pipeline from
zero, matters more than headcount does. A 26-person studio can't afford
Arkane's old approach of hand-authoring three redundant paths through every
room at ship-quality fidelity. What it *can* do, if the founders are honest
about the budget they actually have, is the cheaper version I flagged
yesterday: reactive lighting states, moveable set dressing, systems that
acknowledge player behavior without needing a fully simulated alternate route
for every choice. Prey's Talos I already leaned on exactly that kind of
economy — GLOO cannon traversal reused existing geometry instead of building
new rooms for it. If Black Pony's environment team inherits that instinct
along with the designers who built it, that's the actual asset they're
carrying out of Arkane's closure, more than any single artist's name.

## The team behind the name

The named alumni so far, per Gematsu and Console Creatures: Ricardo Bare
(Deus Ex, Prey), Monte Martinez (Ion Storm, WolfEye), pulled together with
staff from Insomniac, Epic, Avalanche and Volition. That's a studio built
around people who've each separately solved pieces of the systemic-world
problem at different companies — which is either the fastest way to
reconstruct Arkane's old pipeline from institutional memory, or a recipe for
several incompatible opinions about how a kit-bashing system should work. We
won't know which until there's something to look at. Nothing about the
project has shipped a screenshot yet, and the publisher backing it hasn't
been named. What's confirmed is the harder, less quotable part: 96 people
lost a studio that knew how to build these spaces, and a fraction of that
knowledge is trying to reassemble itself at a fraction of the size.
