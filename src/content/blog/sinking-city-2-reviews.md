**The Sinking City 2** launched on **18 August** for PC, PS5 and Xbox Series
X|S, and the reviews landed the same day. Metacritic has it sitting at 79 on
PS5, 84 on Xbox, 75 on PC. OpenCritic's aggregate is 81, "Strong", with 77% of
critics recommending it across 48 reviews.

Averaging the seven outlets I could find a published number for, on a 1-10
scale, gives **8** — working at the foot of this piece. That is a real jump
from the original Sinking City, which launched in 2019 to reviews in the
low-to-mid 60s and a well-documented mess of a publishing dispute with Nacon
on top of it. Frogwares, a Ukrainian studio that kept shipping through a full
invasion of its home country, has apparently earned the sequel the first game
didn't get.

The pitch for the original Sinking City was investigation: an open,
half-flooded version of a fictional New England city, Sherlock Holmes-style
deduction mechanics, combat as an occasional interruption. It was ambitious
and it was baggy, and a decent chunk of the criticism at the time was about
pacing — too much city, not enough reason to be in most of it.

The sequel throws that structure out. You're Calvin Rafferty, an occult
adventurer, not a detective, and the city — a supernaturally drowned 1920s
Arkham — is no longer an open world to wander. Eurogamer's hands-on piece
called it a shift toward "classic survival horror," and the review consensus
agrees: tighter spaces, resource pressure, a much higher ratio of things
trying to kill you to things asking you to solve a puzzle. GamesRadar+ pitched
it as Resident Evil crossed with Silent Hill, which is doing a lot of work as
shorthand but isn't wrong about the register.

## The part nobody's reviewing: what a flood actually saves you

Here's where I want to stop reporting the consensus and talk about what I
think is really going on underneath it, because "we made it scarier" isn't
the whole explanation for why this plays so much better with the same-sized
team and the same troubled studio behind it.

**An open, walkable flooded city is one of the most expensive environment
art briefs there is.** Every street the player can freely approach needs to
read at full resolution from every angle, at every time of day the game
supports, with a navmesh that accounts for waterlines, boat traffic, climbable
debris, and a dozen entry points per building. You're not modelling a level.
You're modelling a city that has to survive being looked at from anywhere,
which means you can't cheat depth, you can't fake a skyline with a matte
painting past the third row of buildings, and you can't decide a street
doesn't need texture work because "the player probably won't go there" — in
an open world, they will.

**A corridor-based survival horror sequence is the opposite problem, and it's
a cheaper one to solve well.** The camera and the critical path are
authored together. You know exactly which fifteen feet of drowned hallway the
player is going to be standing in when the tension needs to peak, so you can
put your best asset, your best lighting rig, and your only working light
source exactly there — and let everything outside that beam go dark, or
underwater, or simply not exist past the geometry you bothered to build. The
first game had to build a city. The second only had to build the fifteen
percent of the city the player is ever actually going to see up close, and
stage it in the order that makes it land.

This is the same trick I wrote about with Mortal Shell II two days ago —
withhold light, spend the saved budget on the few things the player's eye is
guaranteed to land on — but The Sinking City 2 gets there through structure
rather than art direction alone. A linear critical path is itself a budget
tool. It's the environment-art equivalent of writing a shorter book instead
of a longer one and hoping nobody notices the difference: the trick only
works if what's left is genuinely better made, and by the score jump here,
Frogwares spent the savings well rather than just pocketing them.

## Where critics differ

The spread runs from Cubed3 and PC Gamer at 7 up to DualShockers and Console
Creatures at 9, with most of the field clustered at 8. That's a narrower,
higher band than the original game managed, and it's consistent with what a
tighter, better-controlled scope should produce — fewer places for a smaller
team to visibly run out of time.

The lower scores point at pacing in the back third and some combat encounters
that lean on enemy-spam over the atmosphere the opening hours build carefully.
Nobody in the seven reviews I averaged called the production values cheap,
which given the studio's history and the genre they walked away from is
probably the actual headline.

## Worth your time?

If you wanted the original's slow-burn detective work, this isn't that game
anymore, and it isn't trying to be. If you wanted Frogwares to prove the
Lovecraftian-Arkham setting could support a tighter, better-paced horror game
without the budget of a AAA survival horror sequel, the consensus says they
did it — and if you build environments for a living, it's worth a look
specifically for how much less city it lets itself show you.
