The interesting thing about *Eri* is where its shadows come from. Honami Yano
draws on cel, which plenty of people still do. She also paints her
**backgrounds** on cel — on transparent sheets rather than on paper or board —
and stacks them so the characters' shadows fall onto the background lying
beneath them. The dark patch under a cow is light that a sheet of acetate
physically interrupted.

That film won Best Animated Short at the Ottawa International Animation
Festival this weekend, at the festival's fiftieth edition. *We Are Aliens* took
the Grand Prize for animated feature and *Candy Caries* won in the young
audiences category. Yano has been here before: her earlier short *A Bite of
Bone* took Ottawa's Grand Prix, along with a New Face Award at the Japan Media
Arts Festival.

*Eri* premiered at Cannes this year, in the Directors' Fortnight selection. It
is a story about a Holstein cow who is permitted to go on living only while she
keeps producing calves, and who falls in love with another cow called Sawa. A
France–Japan co-production, between Au Praxinoscope and Miyu Productions.

## The stack used to be a tax

For most of the century in which cel animation was the industry, the cel was
the thing you worked around. Every sheet of acetate you laid over a background
swallowed a little light and pushed the art underneath it slightly green. Two
sheets, more green. Four, and the background painter's careful colour had
drifted somewhere else entirely.

So studios kept the stacks shallow. Three levels, four at a push, and colour
stylists painted the lower layers deliberately brighter to survive being looked
at through the layers above. Whole craft traditions exist because of that
absorption — it is part of why cel-era film has the palette it has, and part of
why restoring it is delicate work. Scan an original negative at 4K and you are
resolving the acetate along with the paint, which is roughly [where a cel scan
hits its own ceiling](/blog/ninja-scroll-4k-restoration-cel-scan/).

Yano takes the artefact and uses it as a light. Put the background on cel,
raise it a few millimetres under the camera, and the sheet above casts onto the
sheet below because that is what happens when you interrupt light with an object.
The tax becomes the tool.

## Why anyone lighting things for a living should care

Contact shadow is the cheapest honest trick in rendering and the one we spend
the most on. The darkening in the crevice where two surfaces meet, the small
gradient under a foot — that is what convinces the eye an object is *in* a scene
rather than pasted on top of one. Ambient occlusion passes exist for it. Ray
budgets get spent on it. Shadow map resolution gets argued about in meetings
because of it.

Yano gets it by stacking paint under a lens.

And the comparison that keeps nagging at me runs the other way. CG anime spends
real money travelling in the opposite direction: dropping frames on purpose,
bending models per-camera, having faces redrawn by hand, giving up most of the
asset reuse that 3D is supposed to pay you back with. [Everything Studio Orange
does to make its CG read as drawn](/blog/studio-orange-3d-anime-2d-look/) is
work aimed at a look that Yano's stack of sheets produces as a side effect of
being made of matter.

These are answers to different questions —
one has to fill twelve episodes a season, the other has twelve minutes and a
festival deadline, and the economics of those two facts decide almost
everything else.

## The part that would frighten a production manager

The catch in Yano's method is that the lighting is baked at the moment of
exposure. A digital pipeline keeps shadow separable from drawing until the last
possible minute, which is in practice an insurance policy: it means a note from a producer in week thirty costs a slider rather
than a reshoot. Yano has no slider. Change the shadow and you repaint the cel
and photograph it again.

Twelve minutes is survivable that way. A series is not, which is most of the
reason the industry stopped.

What I would like to see is the shooting setup — the spacing between the levels,
whether she lit for the shadow or let it happen, how she kept the density
consistent across a thousand-odd frames when every sheet of acetate is very
slightly different from the last. None of that is in a press release. It rarely
is, and it is usually the only part worth knowing.
