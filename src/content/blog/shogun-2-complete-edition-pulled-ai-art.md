Sega and Creative Assembly took Total War: Shogun 2 – Complete Edition off sale
two days after it launched. It arrived 13 October 2026; by the 15th the Steam
rating was Overwhelmingly Negative and the publisher was working with platform
holders to pull it and put the 2011 original back up. Anyone who already bought
it keeps it. No date for a return.

The statement said the game had fallen short of both player expectations and
Sega's own standards. It did not mention AI once, which is the thing most of
the reviews were about.

## What people actually photographed

The unit cards. Shogun 2 shipped in 2011 with a few hundred of them — small
painted portraits, a samurai or an ashigaru per card, the kind of asset you
read in half a second while hovering over a stack. The Complete Edition's
versions came back soft and shiny, and then people started finding worse
things in them: faces that distort, a helmet melting into the face under it,
a hand with an extra finger.

Those two complaints are not the same complaint. A card that has gone soupy
and plastic is what an enlarger does to a small painting — it has no new
information to add, so it invents smooth gradients and calls it detail. The
brushwork goes first. That is ugly and cheap and entirely explicable.

Fingers are a different failure. An upscaler working at 4x does not grow a
digit; it does not know the thing is a hand. Something that adds anatomy is
generating, not enlarging, and at least one outlet covering this noted the
same split — that parts of what circulated looked more like bad upscaling than
generated artwork. **Both can be true across a few hundred cards**, and nobody
outside the studio can say which tool touched which asset.

Sega has confirmed nothing. There is also no generative-AI disclosure on the
store page, which Valve requires when generative content ships in a game. That
is either an accurate absence or a missed box, and the statement declines to
say.

## Why a team reaches for the enlarger

Here is the part worth sitting with. The source art for a 2011 game is often
gone. Not deleted maliciously — migrated off a server, left on a contractor's
drive, saved in a Photoshop version nobody has, or never layered in the first
place because the card was painted flat and exported once. Fifteen years is a
long time in a studio.

So a remaster team opens the bin and finds the final 256-pixel PNGs. The
options from there are three, and only one of them is cheap. Repaint several
hundred cards, which is a real commission with a real schedule. Ship the
originals untouched at their original size, which looks dated on a 1440p
monitor and wins no marketing bullet. Or run them through something.

EA hit the same wall and went the other way. [The Wing Commander III remaster
exists because a producer found the camera masters in the archive](/blog/wing-commander-3-fmv-source-tapes/),
and the Command & Conquer collection before it, working from compressed copies
with no masters to be found, paid Lemon Sky to redraw the unit art by hand
rather than enlarging it. That cost money. It is also the reason nobody is
arguing about Command & Conquer's unit art fifteen years on.

Shipping the 2011 cards as they were would have passed. That is the option
that keeps getting skipped, and it is free.

## The decision that turned it into a scandal

Sega delisted the original ahead of the Complete Edition's launch. For two
days, the remaster was the only way to buy Shogun 2 — so the audience could
not route around it, and the review page became the only place to put the
complaint. Thousands did.

There is an audience now that reads art for machine tells the way it used to
read textures for tiling, and it is fast and it is organised. A yuri art
exhibition in Tokyo [cancelled its anniversary event last week after one
promotional image](/blog/yuriten-anniversary-event-cancelled-ai-promo-art/)
drew the same scrutiny. Whatever a studio's position on these tools, the
detection layer is already deployed and it does not need the studio's
cooperation.

The 2011 build is back at $29.99 and sitting at Very Positive, roughly 90% of
some 22,500 English reviews. The Complete Edition also moved modding from Steam
Workshop to Mod.io, and the announcement confirmed existing mods would not
carry across — fifteen years of community work, left behind by a version that
is now itself delisted. Both editions are currently in limbo for different
reasons, which is a strange place for a strategy game from 2011 to have ended
up this week.
