The early access page for 1666: Amsterdam sells you the first piece of the
campaign. Roughly fifteen hours, thirty dollars, PC only for now, live since
**25 August**. Everything after that first piece is promised rather than
present.

That is a production shape before it is a marketing one, and it is the most
honest thing on the page.

## Fifty people, once

Patrice Désilets joined THQ Montreal as creative director in June 2011 and spent
about two years on 1666 with a team that got close to fifty. THQ went bankrupt
in December 2012. The Montreal studio was auctioned to Ubisoft the following
January, Ubisoft let him go, and Ubisoft kept the game. He fought for it and won
it back in a 2016 settlement, by which point he had already founded Panache
Digital Games and was building Ancestors: The Humankind Odyssey, which shipped
in 2019.

Thirteen years between the cancellation and the early access build. I could not
confirm how many people are at Panache today, and the studio does not publish
the number, so take the fifty as a fact about the version that died and not as a
comparison.

## What Ancestors told you about the pipeline

Ancestors is the useful document here, because a studio's previous game is a
fairly reliable description of what its pipeline can afford.

That game gave you an enormous stretch of Miocene Africa and almost no authored
content inside it. No quest markers, no tutorial worth the name, barely any
words. Reviewers found this maddening. From a production seat it reads as a set
of decisions about cost: traversal, climbing, perception and the whole learning
loop are **systems**, and systems are the thing a small team can build once and
run across ten square kilometres. Bespoke content does not work that way. Every
scripted beat, every unique interior, every line of performed dialogue is paid
for a second time each time you want another one.

1666: Amsterdam is a narrative action-adventure set in a city. It wants the
expensive kind of content.

## Cities are the worst thing you can ask a small team to build

Historic Amsterdam is, on paper, kind to a modular kit. Canal houses are narrow,
tall, repetitive, and sit on a grid of water — you can get a long way with a
handful of façade widths, a brick set and some gable variants. Any environment
artist would take that brief over a modern city.

The trouble starts one step in. Gables are what people look at, so the moment
your gable set repeats, the street reads as tiled. Water needs to interact with
the geometry rather than sit under it. Crowds want somewhere to walk to.
Interiors are the killer: a canal house you can enter is a bespoke set, lit
separately, populated separately, and the fifty-person version of this game was
almost certainly designed assuming you could enter a lot of them.

Nobody has published what survived from the THQ build, and I would not assume
much did — engines and toolchains moved on twice over in that gap. What survives
across a rebuild like that is design intent, and design intent is exactly the
thing that does not shrink to fit a smaller team.

## "Buggy and bewildering, but real ambition"

Eurogamer's early access piece, up on 7 September, lands on that phrasing, and
it describes the trade precisely. Ambition in an early access review usually
means a reviewer noticing a bigger game's architecture inside a smaller game's
budget. Bugs mean the QA surface of a systemic city being larger than the number
of hours available to walk it.

Selling the campaign in slices is the sane answer to both. Ship one chunk,
fund the next from it, keep the studio alive between milestones. That is
a financing structure as much as a release plan, and the alternative is what
happened to [Bit Reactor, who furloughed most of their
staff](/blog/bit-reactor-zero-company-furlough/) with the game not out.

It is also the mirror of what large publishers do when the arithmetic stops
working. Sega spent years on an internal plan worth roughly ¥100bn and
[wrote the whole thing off](/blog/sega-super-game-cancelled-100bn-plan/) rather
than ship a slice of it. Désilets had that decision made for him in 2013, and
the second time round he owned the thing outright, which is the only reason
there is a build to buy at all.

## And then, on Sunday, he said he'd make Ancestors 2

The same week the early access reviews went up, Désilets told the press he
intends to make a sequel to Ancestors: The Humankind Odyssey.

I have watched enough studios say that to know it is usually a statement about
morale rather than a schedule. But it sits oddly next to a campaign with one
slice on the shelf and no published length for the rest, and the honest question
for anyone considering the thirty dollars is whether that rest is funded or
hoped for.

The early access agreement does not answer that. They rarely do.
