**Beast of Reincarnation** came out on **4 August** for PS5, Xbox Series X/S and
PC, and it took the review cycle a few weeks to settle into what it actually
is: the first game **Game Freak** has shipped that is not a Pokémon game, in a
company history that goes back to 1989. Metacritic has it at **73** from over
fifty critics, OpenCritic a little higher at **76**. Averaging the eight
outlet scores I could verify on a 1-10 scale gives **7.0** — the full working
is at the foot of this piece.

That number undersells how split the coverage actually is. This is not a
game everyone shrugged at. It's a game a third of critics loved and a third
found actively disappointing, with not much in between.

## A studio that has never had to do this before

Set the score aside for a second, because the more interesting fact is what
Game Freak was attempting. Every mainline Pokémon game, for its entire
history, has been built around turn-based combat, static overworld
navigation and comparatively contained 3D spaces — even **Legends Z-A**,
their most ambitious recent open-zone experiment, still leans on menu-driven
battles once combat starts. Beast of Reincarnation drops all of that. It's a
real-time, one-character-one-dog action RPG set in a ruined, post-apocalyptic
Japan, with Emma and her blighted dog Koo fighting through boss encounters
that are staged and lit like the game wants you to actually look at them,
not just select an attack from a list.

That's a real production gap to close. A studio that has spent three decades
building turn order UI and route-corridor overworlds is, with this game,
building hit-stop, camera-lock, environmental readability and traversal for
what appears to be the first time at this scale. GamesRadar+ and PSU — the
two highest scores in my set, at 9 and 8.5 — both frame the game as exactly
that: a legacy studio taking a real swing at something outside its comfort
zone and mostly landing it. Push Square and Screen Rant, both at 7, are more
measured but still call it a credible first attempt.

## Where the low scores agree

The lower cluster — IGN, GameSpot and Eurogamer all at a converted 6, GameRant
at 5 — is where the "first time doing this" reading gets less charitable.
Push Square's own review, despite its 7, flags "some visual and technical
issues" alongside a story it calls unenthralling. That phrase shows up in
different words across most of the lower-scoring reviews: combat that
doesn't fully justify its own systems, boss encounters that read as
impressive set pieces the first time and repetitive the fifth, and a game
that looks like it spent its production budget on the spectacle moments
without the same care going into the traversal in between.

That's a familiar shape to anyone who has worked on an environment team
moving from a corridor-based game to an open one for the first time. Hero
moments are easy to sell in a trailer and easy to greenlight — a boss arena
gets concept art, gets a lighting pass, gets iteration time. The connective
tissue between hero moments is where a first attempt usually shows its
inexperience, because nobody budgets for the boring square footage until
the game is already in production and it's too late to go back and dress it
properly. Several of the lower reviews are, read closely, describing that
exact gap: strong bosses, thinner world in between.

## Worth your time?

If you go in expecting Game Freak to have solved real-time action design on
the first attempt, the 5s and 6s in this set are telling you something real.
If you go in curious to see a studio that has made turn-based games for
thirty years try to build hit-stop and boss choreography from close to
scratch, the 7-to-9 cluster is telling you something real too — just a
different thing.

Either way, a firm 7 with this much spread between the outlets is a more
useful number than it looks. It's not a consensus that the game is
"pretty good." It's a consensus that critics disagree about exactly what
kind of first attempt this is.
