Damon Lindelof and Chris Mundy's **Lanterns** premiered on HBO Sunday night,
**16 August**, after a review embargo that lifted the Friday before, and the
critical reception is strong: I count six named outlets — ScreenRant,
IndieWire, IGN, Empire, AV Club and Slant Magazine — publishing a numeric or
letter score, and the aggregate sites back them up, sitting at roughly **91%
on Rotten Tomatoes** across more than thirty reviews and **72 on Metacritic**
as of this writing. That is a real consensus, not a handful of early takes,
and Kyle Chandler as Hal Jordan and Aaron Pierre as John Stewart are getting
singled out across the board.

But the thing every review keeps circling, praise or complaint, is not the
performances. It's a production decision: the ring costume is a real,
physically built suit, not a digital effect.

Six scores, arithmetic mean of 8.04, which rounds to an **8**. Worth
flagging: I could only confirm a numeric or letter grade for these six.
Variety, The Hollywood Reporter, Vulture, RogerEbert.com, Collider and TVLine
all reviewed it too, but I could not pin a published grade to any of them
through search, so they're left out of the average rather than guessed at —
same logic Metacritic itself uses when an outlet doesn't publish a score.

## A suit built on an actor, not rendered in a GPU

Here's the production detail that's actually worth an artist's time. Costume
designer Cynthia Summers built Hal Jordan's Green Lantern suit as a physical
costume, constructed directly on Kyle Chandler, reportedly taking around
**45 minutes** to put on and going through three full design iterations
before Mundy and director James Hawes signed off on the final version. Per
Chris Mundy, that wasn't a budget call — it's a stated philosophy. The show
was shot practically, on real LA sets and locations (Warner Bros. Burbank,
plus location work in Piru, California, between February and July last
year), with CG and volume work reserved for whatever genuinely couldn't be
built. And the ring constructs — the show's version of Green Lantern's
signature power, solid light shaped into whatever the wielder imagines —
were built to carry visible physical weight when they do appear, rather than
read as weightless CG the way they famously did in the 2011 Ryan Reynolds
film.

I get why that's the pitch. A CG suit is a lighting and shading problem that
never fully resolves — the material has to survive every angle, every set,
every DP's key light, and a suit an actor can shrug their shoulders in tends
to read as *there* in a way a rendered one rarely does, no matter how good
the shader. Practical costuming also buys you something a VFX pipeline can't
easily replicate: an actor who can feel the weight of the thing and perform
against it, instead of acting opposite a tennis ball on a stick.

But a physical suit is also a constraint you cannot software-patch your way
out of once the season is locked. If the ring's signature trick is
"anything the wielder can imagine," a practical build caps how far that can
stretch before you're back to a digital extension anyway — and that's
exactly where a chunk of the more mixed reviews are landing. Slant's
critique in particular is that the visual palette, described elsewhere in
the roundup as muted grays and dust, keeps the fantastical elements looking
"oddly restrained rather than awe-inspiring" — the show stays so committed
to looking like *True Detective* with a ring in it that when the aliens and
constructs do show up, the CGI deliberately doesn't bloom into the
Technicolor spectacle the premise could support. Several critics used a
version of the same line: this is a superhero show for people who don't
like superhero shows.

That's a legitimate creative choice, not a mistake — restraint is a real
visual strategy and I don't think the critics who dislike it are wrong to
notice it either. What I find genuinely interesting is that it's the same
trade-off environment artists hit constantly at a smaller scale: how much of
the fantastical do you ground in something physically plausible before the
grounding starts working against the sense of wonder the fantastical
element was supposed to deliver in the first place. Lanterns picked a side
of that line hard, on purpose, across an entire season — and the reviews are
essentially a referendum on where that line should sit.

The credited VFX houses — Digital Domain, Image Engine, Spin VFX, Ghost VFX,
El Ranchito, Distillery VFX, Fin Design + Effects, Twin Pines and Refuge VFX
among them, per Art of VFX — are not small shops, which tells me the
restraint in the finished frame is a directed choice by VFX supervisor Greg
Steele's team, not a budget shortfall dressed up as an aesthetic. It's a
grounded show that spent real digital-effects money to *look* like it barely
used any.
