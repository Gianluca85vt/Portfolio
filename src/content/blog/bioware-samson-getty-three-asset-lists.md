Three companies came apart in the space of eleven days, and I could tell you
exactly what each of them owns. *Mass Effect*. A city called Tyndalston. A stock
library old enough to count as production infrastructure. What I cannot tell you,
from any document any of the three has filed, is who made those things, because
nobody files that. An inventory has a column for the asset and no column for the
hands.

We have got very good at the first column.

## Inventory

Last Wednesday EA closed BioWare and ended early work on the next *Mass Effect*.
Four hundred jobs went across the company; around fifty of them were at the
studio, which is the number it took to switch off thirty years. The *Mass Effect*
trademark did not go anywhere. It is still an asset, still carried, still worth
whatever the next slide says it is worth.

On 12 October the Stockholm District Court declared Liquid Swords bankrupt, on a
petition filed by its own staff, who had not been paid since August. The trustee
is taking bids on *Samson: A Tyndalston Story* until the end of this month, which
means this week. Somebody will buy a city.

On 21 October Getty Images filed a prepackaged Chapter 11 in Delaware, clearing
more than half a billion of debt and handing ownership to the Getty family and
Koch Equity Development for up to $533 million of new equity. Public shareholders
get nothing. The library keeps selling, and contributors keep getting paid.

Different instruments, different countries, the same shape. In every case the
catalogue survived the organism.

## Bids close Saturday

I wrote about *Samson* a week ago and I have not stopped thinking about one
detail. Twenty-eight critics reviewed it, most of them did not like it, and the
praise they did find kept landing in the same place: graffiti layered onto
facades, dressed storefronts, reflections in wet road. The environment art
shipped. The missions it was built to carry did not.

So what the trustee is now selling is the finished part of an unfinished game.
A city with its set dressing done, its driving tuned, and no one left in the
building who knows why any of it is where it is.

That is the part that should bother you if you do this for a living. A city is a
few thousand decisions, most of them unrecorded — the street that bends because
the sightline needed closing, the window left blank because the interior got cut
in week nine, the kerb height somebody raised by six centimetres so the car
stopped scraping. None of that is in the files. It was in thirty people, and
thirty people have gone to find other work while a court decides what the folder
is worth.

## Twenty billion, borrowed

Read the BioWare closure as a repayment schedule and it stops being mysterious.

PIF, Silver Lake and Affinity Partners took EA private for $55 billion in
August. Reporting at the time put roughly $20 billion of that as borrowed money,
and EA subsequently told investors it would find $700 million a year in annual
costs. Seven hundred million a year has to come from somewhere, and it does not
come out of a trademark. Trademarks do not have salaries. People do.

Fifty jobs at BioWare is a rounding error against $700 million. That is exactly
why it happened: the studio was small enough by then that closing it was cheap,
tidy, and visible — the kind of decision that reads well on a cost line and costs
nothing on an asset line. Everything EA named as the destination for the money is
a thing it already owns and can keep extracting from. Sports. *Apex*. *Battlefield*.
The next Jedi game.

Buy a company with debt, then pay the debt with the people who made the company
worth buying. It works, in the sense that the arithmetic closes.

What I find harder to take is the petition. Over 187,000 signatures now, asking
EA to keep *Mass Effect* going — and explicitly offering, as the alternative,
that EA license the IP to some other studio instead. The petition does not ask
for BioWare back. It has already conceded the premise. Keep the asset alive; the
team is a sunk cost.

I understand why. I would probably sign it too. But read it as a document and
it is 187,000 people agreeing that the file is the thing worth saving.

## The library stays open

Getty is the honest version of all this, and the one I am least angry about.

A stock library is the closest thing our industry has to a pure asset. The value
is the catalogue and the rights attached to it. Nobody pretends otherwise. When
Getty restructures, the images keep selling and the photographers keep being paid,
because the photographers are suppliers rather than staff — which, as a working
arrangement, is both more precarious and more durable than being an employee of a
studio with a parent company that has borrowed twenty billion dollars.

Still, every single frame in there was taken by somebody who stood somewhere and
decided. That decision is the product. It is just that the paperwork has had a
century to learn how to separate the two, and games are only now catching up.

An archive at least admits what it is. A game studio's asset list pretends the
city is the work, when the city is a record of the work, the way a photograph is
a record of a photographer.

## A hundred and eighty-eight thousand frames

Which brings me to the one production this week that cannot be liquidated into a
list, and which is for the same reason the one nobody can replicate by buying
something.

*Wildwood* opened on Friday. 131 minutes, the longest stop-motion feature anybody
has released, around 188,000 exposures at 24 frames a second. Laika's own stated
target is roughly 4.3 seconds of finished animation per animator per week; on
*Kubo* the reality came in nearer three. Run the sums and *Wildwood* sits somewhere
between 1,830 and 2,620 animator-weeks of shooting. Those are my numbers, not
Laika's. They are also person-weeks on a stage, one frame at a time, by people
each finishing about three seconds of usable footage before the weekend.

It took third place at the box office against a budget reported near $100 million,
which in the language of the other three stories this week makes it a bad asset.

And there is nothing in it to sell off. No trustee can take bids on the thing that
makes a Laika film a Laika film, because the thing is 1,800 weeks of hands and the
institutional memory of how to point a camera at a puppet twenty-four times for
every second. You cannot buy it. You cannot download it. If Laika went, the
puppets would be lots in an auction and the capability would simply stop existing,
visibly, in a way that a games liquidation manages to hide.

## What the folder is worth

Three collapses, three inventories, and underneath all of them the same quiet
claim: that the work is the file.

The file is a photograph of a decision. You can own the photograph, license it,
auction it, carry it at book value and clear it through a Delaware court, and none
of that gets you the next decision, because the next decision was in thirty people
in Stockholm and fifty people in Edmonton and they are updating their portfolios
this morning.

An industry that keeps buying the street and firing the person who knew why it
bends is going to end up with a great many streets and nobody left who can bend
one.
