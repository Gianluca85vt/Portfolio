Nintendo shipped The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time on Switch 2 on
5 November and said the game was built in house, by Entertainment Planning &
Development Production Group No. 3. Four days later two other companies
published pages on their own sites saying what they had made for it.

Bandai Namco Studios describes partial production of artwork and visual assets
for the remake: characters, terrain, animations, cutscenes and UI. Monolith
Soft lists partial contract development covering game design, programming and
graphics — its fifth mainline Zelda, going back to Skyward Sword in 2011. In
the game's own credits, which you reach by finishing the story, Monolith Soft
appears under Special Thanks.

Read that asset list again, slowly. Characters, terrain, animations,
cutscenes, UI. Those are five departments in any studio I have worked the kind
of hours in, each with its own pipeline, its own naming conventions, its own
argument about where the rig ends and the animation begins. Handing all five
out is commissioning the art.

## Specifying a look is the expensive part

The assets are the easy half of outsourced art. What Nintendo
had to produce first was a document precise enough that a team in another
building, on another engine branch, could make Hyrule Field and have it come
back looking like Hyrule Field — the fog falloff, the grass density, how far
Link's silhouette reads against a hill at dusk, which of the 1998 decisions
were taste and which were the N64 talking.

My earlier piece on this remake took Nintendo's line at face value and treated
the rebuild as the work of [the team that missed 1998](/blog/ocarina-of-time-remake-devs-never-played-it/)
— EPD Group No. 3 deciding what Hyrule looks like rather than a port house. I
still think that is where the decisions were made. The disclosure moves where
the hands were, which is a different question, and a more interesting one.

Because EPD Group No. 3 is the Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom
team, and that team is not idle. Whatever they are building next, they were
building it through 2026. A full asset rebuild of a 1998 game needs a hundred
people for a year who do not exist on that org chart, and the capacity came
from outside. That is the honest reason a remake of this size arrived on this
schedule, and it is the same arithmetic behind [what a Switch 2 port costs the art team](/blog/diablo-4-switch-2-age-of-hatred-what-porting-costs-art/)
when the deadline is fixed and the hardware is not the one the assets were
authored for.

## Special Thanks

Here is what bothers me. Bandai Namco Studios and Monolith Soft both told the
world about this work themselves, on their own corporate sites, days after
launch. They are not in a position to put a logo on the title screen. So the
credit that reaches the person scrolling past at two in the morning is a line
under Special Thanks, next to the catering.

This is normal in Japanese co-development and it has been normal for twenty
years. Normal does not make it fine. An artist's credit is their portfolio;
"Special Thanks, Monolith Soft" does not tell a hiring lead that you modelled
the Gorons. The studios disclose it on their own websites precisely because
the credits do not, which is a workaround, and workarounds leave the junior
people out. Nintendo's own Ask the Developer interviews name its internal
staff generously. The asymmetry is the point.

I cannot tell you who did which part. Monolith Soft says graphics, Bandai Namco
says artwork and visual assets, and those two phrases could describe the same
work or completely different halves of it. Nothing I have read names Grezzo,
the studio that made the 3DS version of this game and the Link's Awakening
remake, so whether they touched this one at all is unconfirmed. The sources
also disagree on billing: some coverage calls Monolith Soft a co-developer,
the credits say Special Thanks, and both are accurate depending on which
document you trust.

What is settled is the shape. Nintendo specified it, and a lot of the pixels
were made somewhere else.
