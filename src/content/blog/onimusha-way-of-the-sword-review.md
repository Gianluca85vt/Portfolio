Capcom's first new Onimusha in nearly two decades landed to a consensus most
games never get. Across the twelve scored verdicts I could verify, the average
is **8.5**, and the range runs from 7 to 9.2 — a two-point spread on a
ten-point scale. OpenCritic has it at 85 from sixty-three critics with 92
percent recommending.

A spread that tight usually means one thing: nobody is arguing about what the
game was trying to do.

## The combat is the reason for the number

Every review lands on the same system. Parry a fraction of a second before an
enemy connects and Musashi answers with an Issen — a single cut that ends the
fight. Around it sits a loop of deflects that drain enemy stamina, parries that
build a burning meter on the blade, and a critical hit at the end of the chain.

That is a design with one demand: the player has to read the wind-up. Which
means every enemy attack needs a silhouette that resolves at speed, an anticipation
frame that is legible at whatever distance the camera sits, and a follow-through
that tells you whether the punish window is open. Animation carries the entire
combat system, and the scores suggest it holds.

## Where the complaint is, and why it is interesting

The criticism that recurs is the look of Kyoto. Perpetually overcast, muddy,
short on the glamour a game set in the old capital promises. None of the famous
temples are standing.

Here is the part worth sitting with as an artist: that is written into the
premise. The Genma have wrecked the city, so the destruction is the reason the
palette is narrow and the reason the landmarks are rubble.

I have no way of knowing which came first — the story that justified the
restricted palette, or the palette that needed a story. Both happen, and from
the outside they look identical. What is observable is that the constraint got
noticed by reviewers anyway. A narrative reason for a limited environment is
still a limited environment, and players feel the second one whatever the first
one says.

Capcom bought something real with it. Fewer standing structures means fewer
hero assets, fewer unique interiors, and a fog-and-overcast lighting setup that
covers a multitude of transitions. Set against that, the same reviewers call
this the best-looking RE Engine game Capcom has made on facial animation, art
direction and cutscenes. That is where the money went instead.

## The number, and what it is not

**8.5**, from twelve scores between 7 and 9.2.

That is an arithmetic mean of what was published, rounded to the nearest half
point. It is not a verdict on whether you will like it, and it is worth saying
that a couple of the outlets here reviewed the Switch 2 version, which is a
different technical proposition to the PS5 one even when the design is
identical.

If you want the argument for the game in one line, it is the one Creative Bloq
made: it gets the important thing right. The important thing being the sword.
