Everything I watched on Monday night was built to be looked at.

Not played. Looked at. Two hours of footage cut to survive a stage, an audience,
and a clip on someone's timeline — and I say that as someone who has built
exactly that kind of thing, and who will explain further down what it cost.

Opening Night Live is not the problem. It is a symptom, and this year it was
unusually honest about what it is.

## Gilded cages

Look at what we can do now.

Nanite eats the polygon budget that governed environment art for twenty years.
Lumen means you can move a light source at runtime and the room answers. A
phone in your pocket produces a gaussian splat of a real place in an afternoon.
MetaHuman hands you a face that would have taken a small team a month in 2012.

We have never been this equipped. Not close.

And the headline output of the biggest showcase of the year was: a remaster of a
game from 2015, a remake of a strategy game from 1999, and the third instalment
of a remake of a game from 1997.

I want to be careful here, because the easy version of this argument is stupid.
Remasters are not a moral failing. Old games deserve to keep running. The
Witcher 3 remaster is free, which is more than most publishers manage, and
Heroes 3 has earned every ounce of the affection people have for it.

But line the tools up next to the output and something does not add up. We built
machinery capable of describing anything, and we mostly point it backwards.

The cage is gorgeous. It is still a cage.

## All of it fake

The word the industry uses for the thing I built is "vertical slice", and it is
a good name, because a slice is exactly what it is: a cut through a cake that
does not exist yet.

You take the two minutes somebody is going to see. You light it for one camera
angle. You put the good asset where the eye lands and a box with a texture on it
where it does not. You do not build the systems, you build the appearance of the
systems working. Everyone does this. It is not fraud, it is how you get money to
make the rest.

The trouble starts when the slice is the only thing that ever exists.

## A slice, and a gate

Some years ago I worked on a vertical slice for N31, a stylised open-world
driving game. Low-poly cars, saturated palette, a city built to be driven
through fast. The kind of art direction I like, because it earns its look
through decisions rather than through budget.

We built the slice. We sent it to Epic MegaGrants for approval.

We never moved to the next phase.

The Steam page is still up, as far as I can tell. It still says coming soon. The
screenshots on it have not changed since January 2023.

I am not telling you this as a grievance. Projects stall for a hundred reasons
and most of them are nobody's villainy. I am telling you because of what it
taught me about the shape of the thing: we had poured everything into the two
minutes that would be looked at, and the gate we were building for was a gate
that decides based on looking.

That is the machine. Build a thing to be looked at, show it to people whose job
is to look, and hope the looking converts into permission to build the part that
matters.

Sometimes it does not convert, and what you are left with is a very good-looking
two minutes.

## Eleven years

Here is what broke my own argument.

In 2015 The Witcher 3 was the ceiling. Graphics, interaction, density — it moved
what people thought a game could be, and it moved it for me personally. I
remember the impact it had.

It is eleven years old now. Everything technical about it has been surpassed,
some of it twice over. The lighting is baked. The foliage is cards. The LODs pop
if you look sideways while riding.

And it still stops me. Not out of nostalgia — I go back and there are vistas
that make me stop the horse, and an action-RPG rhythm that holds up against
things built a decade later with an order of magnitude more compute.

Go back to a scene you made a long time ago and it is like watching a very old
film after a run of new ones. You find every flaw in the world. But if the thing
has a real soul, that part stays, and it stays beautiful.

That is not a technical property. You cannot buy it with Nanite.

## The part nobody notices

So what is it, in production terms? Because "soul" is a word people use when
they have stopped trying to explain something.

I think it is this: somebody sweated a detail that nobody was ever going to
notice.

There is one in almost everything I make — 2D, 3D, animation — because I am
enough of a perfectionist that I cannot leave it. Days lost on a seam, a
transition, a piece of timing that not one person will consciously see. It never
shows up in a review. It never gets pointed at in a trailer, because it is
invisible by definition.

But it is the difference. A world where a thousand invisible decisions went the
right way feels different to stand in, and every one of us can tell, and none of
us can say why.

A vertical slice cannot contain that. There is no room in two minutes for a
thousand invisible decisions, and no reason to make them — the camera is not
going there. You make the decisions that will be seen. That is the brief.

Which is why a slice can be flawless and still feel like nothing.

## Where the void really sits

The emptiness in "architectures of the void" is not a shortage of technology. We
are drowning in technology. It is a shortage of reasons.

We can build anything, so we build the thing with the clearest reason attached:
a name people already love, a corridor they already walked, in 8K. The reason
comes pre-approved. Nobody has to be convinced. Nobody has to gamble on whether
a thousand invisible decisions will add up to something.

And the industry keeps being surprised when the pre-approved thing feels hollow,
as though fidelity were the missing ingredient.

An eleven-year-old game with baked lighting and cardboard trees is still out
there stopping people mid-ride. Not because of what it was built with. Because
of how many times somebody decided to do the harder thing in a place where it
would never be seen.

We do not have a technology problem. We have a nerve problem.
