Reviews for STAR WARS: Galactic Racer arrived four days ahead of its 6 October
2026 release, which is early even by the standards of a publisher confident in
what it has. Across the eight scored verdicts I could verify on 2 October, it
averages **9.0**, from a range of 8 to 10. OpenCritic had it at 87 from 49
critics the same day, with 96 per cent of them recommending it. Metacritic,
working from 72 reviews, split the number by platform: 88 on PS5, 86 on Xbox
Series X, 82 on PC.

That makes it the highest-rated Star Wars game in more than two decades. The
last one to score higher was Knights of the Old Republic, in 2003.

## What Fuse Games actually built

Not a podracing revival, though podracing is in it. The game pulls from three
separate racing ideas scattered across forty years of the licence: the canyon
podracing of Episode I, the Riot Racing of The Bad Batch's second season, and
the sky circuits from Star Wars Resistance. Fuse Games developed it. Secret Mode
publishes.

Stitching three visual traditions into one game is the sort of brief that
usually ends with a title that looks like three games wearing a shared logo.
Reviewers describe the opposite. The thing they return to is the campaign
structure — a run-based, risk-and-reward loop that Game Rant singled out and
that GameSpot's Jordan Ramée credited with making it his favourite racing game
in years. Push Square's 90 called it a racer in a league of its own on PS5.

## Eight outlets, two points apart

Two points, from 8 to 10, across eight outlets. That is tight. Compare it to the review week
this one shares: [Gears of War: E-Day drew verdicts from 6 to 9.5](/blog/gears-of-war-e-day-review/),
a three-and-a-half point range, and the disagreement there was about level
structure — whether the open sections earned their place. Galactic Racer's
critics are not arguing about structure. They agree on it, and the complaints
that do appear are about scope rather than design.

IGN's 8 is the low end here and it is not a dissent. A two-point range on
eight verdicts means the game does one thing and does it the same way for
everybody, which is rarer in an arcade racer than the aggregate suggests.

## The PC number

Six points is a wide gap to leave unexamined. It is also not proof of anything
on its own — platform Metascores draw on whichever outlets reviewed which
build, and the PS5 cohort and the PC cohort are not the same set of people. A
racer reviewed on console by console sites will score differently from one
reviewed on PC by sites that weigh frame pacing heavily, before you touch the
code.

Still, it is a pattern worth naming, because this blog saw it two days ago.
[Ace Combat 8 averaged 9.0 and still sat Mixed on Steam](/blog/ace-combat-8-wings-of-theve-review/),
with a memory leak and a 150GB install doing the damage while the flight model
itself went uncriticised. Flight and racing games share the problem: at speed,
a dropped frame is not a cosmetic event. It is a missed apex. Hold the PC
verdict until the launch build and the first patch are both out. Four days.

## Why a racer is an environment-art problem

Handling gets the credit when a racer works. The art department does the
labour.

At the speeds this game runs, a player is not reading geometry. They are
reading silhouette, contrast and the rhythm of landmarks, and deciding where to
commit two seconds before they arrive. Get the spacing wrong and the track
feels unfair — which the player will describe as bad handling, every time.
Canyon walls have to separate from the sky at a glance. A hazard has to differ
in value from the surface it sits on, not merely in hue. Then it has to survive
motion blur, which eats precisely the high-frequency detail an artist is
tempted to add. Detail is the enemy here. Shape wins.

Three source traditions makes that four times harder, because the readability
language has to hold across a slot canyon, a city street and open sky. Those
are three completely different problems in contrast and depth cueing. Reviewers
agreeing that the game is legible at speed, across all three, is the loudest
thing in the coverage, and almost none of them will have filed it as a
compliment to the environment team.
