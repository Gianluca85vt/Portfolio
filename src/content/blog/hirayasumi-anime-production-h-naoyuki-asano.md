Anime News Network ran a feature yesterday arguing that Keigo Shinzō's *Hirayasumi*
will survive being animated — that the soft line work, the rounded character designs
and the watercolour backgrounds of its cover art "translate perfectly to animation."
The same day, five more voice actors were added to the cast, with Daisuke Namikawa
as Hideki Noguchi and Yōko Hikasa as Yomogi Tachibana among them. The series goes
out on NHK General in **January 2027**.

I want to take the translation claim seriously, because it is the thing I would be
nervous about if the work landed on my desk, and because the people hired to do it
look like they picked the job rather than drew it out of a hat.

## What the drawing actually asks for

Shinzō's line is not a clean line. It thickens and thins, it goes a bit soft at the
corners, and quite often a shape does not fully close. That is most of the charm. It
is also, in order, every problem a television animation pipeline exists to eliminate.

A TV design has to be drawable by a lot of people. Key animators, in-between artists,
second-tier studios taking a cut of the episode count — and the usual way you make a
design survive that many hands is to flatten it. Uniform line weight. Fewer features.
Closed shapes, because the digital paint step fills regions with a bucket, and a line
with a gap in it leaks colour across the whole cel. Every one of those fixes is a
direct subtraction from what makes a Shinzō page look like a Shinzō page.

So there are two outcomes. You keep the wobble, and you pay for it in correction
passes and inconsistency between episodes animated by different teams. Or you clean it
up, and three episodes in you have made a perfectly nice slice-of-life show that looks
like eleven other perfectly nice slice-of-life shows.

## Watercolour is the harder half

The backgrounds worry me more than the characters.

Painterly texture behaves badly in motion. Hold the plate still and it is gorgeous —
anime has known this for fifty years, which is why the painterly shot is so often the
one where the camera does nothing and a character moves across it. Start moving the
camera and paper grain becomes a problem of frequency. Fine texture is high-frequency
detail. High-frequency detail aliases first under motion, crawls first under scaling,
and is the first thing a streaming encoder throws away when it runs short of bits. If
the grain is baked into the plate it slides around like a sticker on glass during a
parallax move. If it is generated per frame it boils.

None of that is unsolvable. It costs money and it costs decisions, and the decision
usually gets made in favour of the schedule.

There is also a quieter gap in the ANN argument: the watercolour being praised is
*cover art*. One image, painted once, taking however long it took. A season needs
something in the region of three hundred background plates on a fixed calendar, and
whatever arrives will be a digital approximation of watercolour — textured overlays,
soft gradients, grain layers in a paint package. The approximation is where this look
lives or dies. It is a job that deserves a specialist, and this one has
[a named art director in Mika Nishimura](/blog/ghost-in-the-shell-exhibition-backgrounds),
which is the same signal as a studio treating background art as its own discipline
rather than the thing behind the characters.

## The staff list is the argument

**Naoyuki Asano** has the character designs. He designed *Keep Your Hands Off
Eizouken!* at Science SARU in 2020, plus *Mr. Osomatsu* and *Saint Young Men* before
it. Eizouken is a show whose entire register depended on characters being allowed to
stretch, squash and go briefly wrong on purpose. Somebody who has shipped that knows,
in episodes rather than in theory, how much looseness a broadcast schedule can carry
before it stops reading as style and starts reading as an error.

The studio is **Production +h.**, whose previous work includes *The Orbital Children*
and *Dead Dead Demons Dededede Destruction*. That second one matters here. Adapting
Inio Asano means dealing with an author whose pages are dense, heavily
photo-referenced and drawn with a very fine character line — the same category of
problem as Shinzō, arriving from a different direction. They have done this before and
have a house answer for it.

Kei Suezawa directs, and also handles series composition and the scripts. Three
jobs, one person. On a show with a car chase that would be a scheduling hazard; on a
slice-of-life about a 29-year-old who inherits a house from an elderly neighbour, tone
*is* the product, and you cannot hold tone by committee. Kaori Sawada scores it.

What I keep noticing is that nobody in that list is a generalist hire. Which is the
same move, at a different scale, as Kore-eda's live-action *Look Back*
[handing one rotoscoped sequence to Yōko Kuno](/blog/look-back-live-action-rotoscope-sequence)
rather than to whoever was free: when the drawing is the point, you staff the drawing
first.

## Worth saying out loud

*Hirayasumi* has run in Shogakukan's *Weekly Big Comic Spirits* since April 2021, and
it already got a live-action television drama, which aired in Japan across November and
December 2025. The drama never had to answer any of this. You point a camera at a house
and the house is textured for free.

The anime is the adaptation that has to earn the look, and the staffing suggests
everybody involved knows it. I would still like to know what is under those
backgrounds before I agree that the translation is perfect. Ask me again in January.
