A cel is a sheet of acetate about a tenth of a millimetre thick. The line work
goes on the front. The paint goes on the back, so the ink edge stays clean from
the camera's side. Then somebody stacks three or four of those sheets over a
painted background, presses the lot flat under a glass platen, and photographs
it. That was the job in 1993, and **Ninja Scroll** was made that way from the
first frame to the last.

HIDIVE and Iconic Events are putting it back in roughly 600 North American
cinemas on 4, 5 and 7 October — subtitles on the Sunday, the English dub on the
Monday, an encore on the Wednesday in whichever format the theatre picks. Sentai
produced the restoration and Yoshiaki Kawajiri, who wrote and directed the
original, supervised it. The new master had its first outing at the 76th
Berlinale back in February.

The distributor describes the work in three steps: a 4K scan of the original
35mm negative at 3840 × 2160, frame-by-frame damage repair, then colour
correction to an archival-quality digital master.

The middle step is where these things live or die.

## 4K is the ceiling on a cel picture

A 35mm negative holds more than 3840 pixels across. People who scan live action
argue about 6K and 8K for exactly that reason, and they are not wrong about the
film stock.

Cel animation moves the bottleneck off the film and onto the drawing
photographed through it — an ink line laid down by a person at a fixed physical
size, on a sheet framed to a fixed field size under the camera. Scan far enough
to resolve the edge of that line and the useful information runs out. Everything
past it is grain and dust.

So 4K on a 1993 cel picture lands about where the artwork stops having more to
give.

## What the scan picks up that nobody drew

Photograph a stack of acetate and you photograph the acetate too.

Each sheet swallows a little light. A character painted on the third level sits
fractionally duller and warmer than one on the first, and the grader on a 1993
production either matched the paint to compensate or let the shift ride. At
video resolution the difference disappeared into the noise floor. At 4K it is
visible, and it is a record of how the shot was assembled — you can sometimes
read the layer order off a frame.

The platen leaves its own evidence. Dust that settled between takes, a hair,
the faint bloom where the glass was not quite flat. Cel animation is full of
this, and a good scan hands you all of it at once.

The second step goes after something else. All of that is the film keeping a
record of having been shot on a table by people, and it should survive. The
blog's piece on the
[hand-painted backgrounds from the same era, seen at exhibition
scale](/blog/ghost-in-the-shell-exhibition-backgrounds/) is the closest thing to
looking at the source art with your own eyes, and the texture there is the
texture a 4K scan starts resolving.

## The thing an automated filter cannot tell apart

Dust removal works by comparing a frame against its neighbours. A speck that
appears for one frame and vanishes is dirt. Flag it, patch it, move on.

Now run that over anime.

Japanese television and film animation is built on held drawings — the same cel
photographed twice or three times before the next one arrives. A temporal filter
looking for change across neighbouring frames sees long stretches where nothing
moves at all, then a hard jump. And the effects work is worse. Impact flashes,
sword glints, blood spray, a single white frame at the moment of a cut: these
are deliberate one-frame elements that look, to the software, exactly like a
piece of grit on the platen.

Kawajiri's film is made of those. It is a picture about people being cut open in
the dark by things that move faster than the camera can follow, and its grammar
depends on marks that exist for a twenty-fourth of a second.

Push noise reduction too hard on top of that and you get the other classic
failure, which anyone who has bought a badly remastered disc will recognise on
sight. The grain goes. The colour fields flatten into plastic. And the ink line
— the one element carrying all the drawing — turns soft and waxy at the edges,
because the filter has been averaging it against the paint on either side. A
line that a person put down in one confident pass ends up looking like it was
traced by a machine. It is the same quality the Imageworks crowd spent years
learning to protect when they moved [ink lines onto a moving CG
character](/blog/spider-verse-inklines-grease-pencil-geometry-nodes/): the mark
has to keep the hand in it or the whole style collapses.

## Which is why the supervision line matters

Every one of the decisions above is a judgement call rather than a repair, and
the person who stood over the original photography is the one who can say which
speck was dirt and which was a highlight somebody painted on purpose. That is
what the supervision credit buys.

Colour is the same problem in a different coat. This film has been graded for a
1993 theatrical print, then for laserdisc, then for DVD, then for whatever
Blu-ray master has been in circulation since — four different targets with four
different ideas of what black is. *Ninja Scroll* is mostly night. Torches,
moonlight, forest interiors, a lot of frames where the composition is a shape
against something almost unlit. Lift those blacks to "reveal detail" and you
have not restored anything, you have thrown away the staging.

Sentai's description stops at "colour correction", which tells you nothing about
where they pointed it. That is the part I would want to see on a big screen
before saying the restoration is good.

Two things I could not confirm and am not going to assert: whether the scan was
done wet-gate, and whether the master runs at the original 24 or has been
retimed. Neither has been published.
