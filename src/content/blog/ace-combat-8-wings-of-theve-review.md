Bandai Namco let reviews of Ace Combat 8: Wings of Theve out on 25 September.
The game launches globally on 2 October. A publisher that lifts an embargo a
full week ahead of release has read the coverage in advance and decided it
helps, and that timing is worth as much as any single score in it.

Across the seven scored verdicts I could verify, the average is **9**, on a
range from 8 to 10. OpenCritic had it at 88 from 36 critics on the day reviews
landed, rising as more filed; Metacritic sat at 87 for the PS5 version.

That is a two-point spread, wider than the headline number suggests, and the
disagreement concentrates in a single department.

## Everybody agrees about the clouds

PC Gamer's review — a 92 — spends its space on a dynamic weather system that
runs through nearly every mission. Cloud banks stand over the battlefields like
buildings. Flying into one buys you a few seconds out of a missile lock, and
costs you your own sight lines while you are in there.

Read that as a rendering decision and it is the most interesting thing about
the game. Clouds that occlude, that you pass through, that change what you can
see from inside them, are volumes. They have to be marched through per pixel,
and they are one of the few effects in real-time rendering that scales with how
much of the screen they cover rather than how many objects are on it. In a
flight game they routinely cover all of it.

It stays affordable because of what a flight game never has to draw. No streets. No crowds. No foliage a metre from the camera, no
interiors, no character models in close-up for most of the runtime. Terrain
sits kilometres below the player and can be served at a fraction of the detail a
third-person game needs. The frame is almost empty, and an almost empty frame is
a budget looking for somewhere to go.

This is the same trade [4A Games made in the other direction on Metro 2039,
where corridors are what make ray tracing at 60fps affordable](/blog/metro-2039-ray-tracing-60fps-base-ps5/).
Constrain the geometry, spend the savings on light. Project Aces has the most
unconstrained space in games — open sky — and almost nothing in it, so the sky
itself becomes the thing worth spending on. Same accounting, opposite shape.

The detail I would want confirmed, and cannot from here, is how the weather is
simulated versus authored. A system that genuinely evolves cloud cover over a
mission is a different engineering problem from one that plays back a designed
weather state on a timeline. Reviewers describe the effect, not the
implementation, and I am not going to guess at it.

## And the cutscenes are where the spread comes from

GameSpot's 8 and TechRadar's review land on the same objection from different
angles: the cutscenes run long, the payoff rarely arrives, and the antagonist
the game spends hours constructing folds in a few minutes of dogfighting.
Metacritic's only two mixed verdicts at the time of writing were both 7s.

I find this structurally predictable rather than disappointing. Look at what a
flight team is staffed to make. Airframes — hard-surface modelling, panel lines,
weathering on metal, ordnance that has to read at distance. Skies and terrain at
altitude. A HUD dense enough to fly by. That is a very specific set of
disciplines, and none of them is facial animation, cinematic staging, or
performance capture direction.

A studio that ships a flight game every few years does not carry a standing
cinematics department the way a narrative studio does. So the cutscenes get
made, competently, by people whose best work is elsewhere in the same build.
The gap reviewers keep describing between the flying and the talking is an org
chart made visible.

## A note on the platform numbers

At the time of writing the Xbox Series version showed the highest Metascore of
the three, around 90, from roughly four reviews. The PS5 version sat at 87 from
more than fifty. Four verdicts is not a sample, and anyone citing the Xbox
number as evidence the game runs better there is reading noise.

## What the 9 is saying

An average of 9 across a two-point spread describes a game that is excellent at
one thing and ordinary at another, where the excellent thing
happens to be what the genre is for, so most reviewers weight it accordingly
and a few do not.

The blog has run into this shape recently: [Well Dweller averaged a 9 across a
2.5-point spread with one firm dissent](/blog/well-dweller-review/), and the
dissent was the most useful review in the set. Here the dissent belongs to a
department rather than an outlet, and every outlet found it.

Deluxe edition holders get in from 29 September in European time, four days
before everyone else. The scores are early by a week and honest about which half
of the game earned them.
