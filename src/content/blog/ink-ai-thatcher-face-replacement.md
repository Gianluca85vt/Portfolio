Thirty seconds. That is the whole budget of generated imagery in *Ink*, Danny
Boyle's film about Rupert Murdoch buying *The Sun* and handing it to a young
editor named Larry Lamb. Boyle has been asked about those thirty seconds at
every stop since the film screened at Toronto in September, and he keeps
answering, which is more than most directors manage. Some of it went on
Margaret Thatcher's face. The rest went on mice.

Start with the mice, because they show where this tooling currently sits in a
pipeline. Boyle wanted vermin running through the Fleet Street newsroom — set
dressing, half a second of movement at the edge of frame, the thing that says
"1969 print works" faster than any prop. Shot conventionally that is a creature
job: a groom, a sim for the fur, four feet making contact with a floor, lighting
matched to whatever the set was lit with that day. Days of work from somebody
good, for a shot no audience will ever recall. Boyle's description of the tool
was that it is "very good with animals."

He is right, and the reason says something about where the technology is
strong. Nobody in an audience carries a precise internal model of how a mouse
crosses a particular floor. Everybody carries one for a human face. Face
recognition is the most over-trained visual system we have, running constantly
and below conscious attention, which is why the uncanny valley is very nearly a
face-only phenomenon. The mice are the safe seconds. Thatcher is the exposed
ones.

## Two accounts of the Thatcher shot

Here the reporting splits, and the split is worth naming, because the two
versions describe different pipelines.

Speaking to Variety and *The Hollywood Reporter* this week, Boyle described
casting a lookalike: "we got an impersonator in to play the whole scene," and
then put Thatcher's face on her. What he wanted was the documented detail —
Thatcher turning up at Lamb's office late, shoes off, curled on the couch with
a whiskey. "I didn't want an imitation Thatcher. I wanted Thatcher herself."

Around the Toronto screening, IndieWire and *Vanity Fair* described the thirty
seconds differently: generated motion applied to still photographs, of Thatcher
and of the Kray brothers. Both accounts can hold across different shots, and
none of the outlets settles it.

For anyone who works in post, that distinction is the entire question. A face
replacement over a performer has a performance underneath it — the body, the
timing, the weight shift, the shoes coming off. Somebody acted, and the comp
carried a likeness on top. An animated photograph has nothing underneath. The
movement is invented by the model, and whatever the frame does, no person chose
it.

The difference also decides whether Boyle's defence holds. His line is that he
used the tool sparingly and was "very careful to make sure it didn't replace
anyone," with the qualifier that you pay actors "if you're going to replace them
with some kind of AI embodiment." An impersonator who played the scene and got
paid is that bargain, honoured. A photograph animated by a model replaced nobody,
because nobody was ever going to be hired for it. The second case is cheaper and
lonelier. It is also the one a production under pressure reaches for first.

## Where the cost floor moved

Putting a dead public figure's face on a hired body is not new work. *Rogue One*
rebuilt Peter Cushing in 2016 with a performer under the likeness and a vendor
team on top. *The Irishman* de-aged three leads across a whole feature in 2019.
Both were enormous line items — a facility, a bid, months of iteration, a
supervisor whose entire job was that one problem.

*Ink* is a mid-budget British period drama from StudioCanal, Media Res and House
Productions, built on a James Graham screenplay adapted from his own stage play.
It does not have a Cushing budget. It got a Thatcher anyway.

That is the development worth watching, and it has little to do with Boyle
personally. A class of shot that used to require a vendor relationship now sits
within reach of a production counting its money. The question shifts from "can
we afford this" to "do we want it," which is a far less protective question.
Cost has quietly done a great deal of ethical work in this industry for
decades, simply by making certain shots impossible.

Hold thirty seconds next to the other AI number in circulation this autumn. The
BBC's director general watched [a fifty-minute AI-generated *Doctor Who* episode
and called it "pretty good"](/blog/bbc-ai-doctor-who-fifty-minutes). Fifty
minutes is where generated video comes apart — rooms change dimension between
cuts, continuity errors compound instead of cancelling. Thirty seconds, split
across a face with a real performance beneath it and some animals at the edge of
frame, is the length at which the output survives being looked at. Boyle picked
a duration his tool can actually service. That is a craft decision, whatever you
make of the rest.

## The estate he did not call

Boyle did not seek permission from Thatcher's estate, and his reasoning was
that the estates of such figures should not hold control over artistic ideas
about their influence on public life. As a claim about a three-term prime
minister it is defensible. As a general principle it thins out fast, because it
scales to anybody a producer decides was historically significant.

The consent question keeps arriving in this shape. The material gets used, the
argument for using it gets assembled afterwards, and the people with standing to
object are either dead or [were never in the room when the model was
trained](/blog/capcom-ai-engine-work-artists-already-made). Boyle drew his line
around actors, who have a union and a rate card. He did not draw one around
subjects.

The mice, for what it is worth, sound like the correct use: a shot nobody will
study, in a register where the model is genuinely strong, standing in for a job
nobody was going to enjoy. Thirty seconds is a small number. It is also a
precedent with a receipt attached, which is why Boyle keeps getting asked.
