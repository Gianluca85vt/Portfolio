About 1,500 prompts went into one scene.

That is the figure Kakao's own production team put forward for *Still, Ryan*, a
short animated film made with OpenAI's image tooling and released on **11
November 2026**. Fifteen hundred instructions, on a single scene, to land the
staging and the emotional register the director was after.

Nobody has said how many scenes the film has.

## What they made

Ryan is a Kakao Friends character — a lion, heir to the throne of Doongdoong
Island, and bald where a lion is supposed to have a mane, which the webtoon
treats as the source of his insecurity. The short marks ten years since he
turned up. Kakao Entertainment launched the story-driven webtoon behind it in
March 2025, and Kakao announced the film on **10 November 2026**, a day before
putting it out on Ryan's Instagram, Kakao Friends' YouTube, and OpenAI's own
Instagram and YouTube channels.

Two things the coverage cannot agree on. Anime News Network calls it roughly
seven minutes; Digital Today and JKN say about five. And the English title
moves depending on who is writing — *Still, Ryan* in the Korean trade press,
*Ryan Begins* in ANN's headline after the webtoon, *Nevertheless, Ryan* in
Digital Today's own Chinese edition. Take the runtime as five to seven minutes
and the title as unsettled.

The credits are the interesting part. Han Ji-won directs, off *Lost in
Starlight*, the animated feature Netflix put out. Kim Young-jun is credited as
AI supervisor. Both named, in that order.

## The pipeline, as Kakao describes it

Scene sketches and prompts in. Generated images out. Artists then select from
what came back and edit it by hand to fix the direction. Kim's account is that
when a generated image missed the intended emotion, they ran it again — so the
ceiling was set at the director's desk.

The tool, per one report, is ChatGPT's image feature. Not a video model.

Which leaves the question the coverage walks past. A still-image generator
gives you pictures. Animation is what happens between pictures, and nothing in
the published account says where the motion came from — whether frames were
generated individually, whether there is inbetweening at all, whether the
"scene" that took 1,500 prompts resolved into one held shot or twenty-four
frames a second of them. That gap is where the entire cost of animating
normally sits.

Most of the AI showing up in anime and webtoon production so far has stayed
upstream of the drawings. On *The Painted Soul* earlier this autumn,
[the AI was in pre-production](/blog/the-painted-soul-viki-ai-pre-production/)
and out of the frames entirely. Kakao has put it in the frames.

## Why a maneless lion is the hard case

Ryan's design is an absence. The character reads as a lion and has no mane, and
that negative space is the whole joke, the whole character arc, and the reason
the webtoon exists. Now hand that to a generator whose prior for "lion" is
built from a few million photographs and illustrations of lions with manes.

Every roll, the mane tries to come back. A wisp at the jawline. A slightly
fuller crown. A rim light that reads as fur. None of it wrong enough to be
obviously broken, all of it wrong enough to break the design, and all of it
different from the last frame because a still-image generator holds no memory
of the frame before. Character consistency is the standing problem with this
class of tool, and it bites hardest on designs whose identity is a specific
simple shape rather than a pile of detail.

So 1,500 attempts on one scene is believable. I would expect it.

## What 1,500 prompts is, as a day's work

A prompt is a roll of the dice with a note attached. You write the note, you
roll, you look at what lands, you adjust the note. The adjustment is real
craft — knowing that "warmer" will get you the wrong thing and "late afternoon,
window left" will get you closer is a skill — but the loop between asking and
knowing runs through a machine you cannot direct, only persuade.

Fifteen hundred of those on one scene is somebody at a desk all day, doing
quality control on a slot machine.

Set that beside a key animator drawing the same scene. The animator knows after
the first drawing whether the design is holding, because they drew it, and the
second drawing inherits the decision from the first. The generated version
relitigates the character from scratch on every roll. Drawing and rolling are
both work, in shapes different enough that nobody has a rate card covering the
second one. Which is why Kakao can publish the number as a boast and nobody
can say whether it is one.

## The numbers Kakao did not give

Prompt count, yes. Scene count, no. Shot count, no. How many artists, how many
weeks, how much of the five-to-seven minutes was hand-edited past recognition —
none of it.

Those are the figures that would turn this from an announcement into evidence.
Without them, "1,500 prompts for one scene" is a quantity floating free of a
denominator, which is a strange thing for a technology pitch built on
efficiency to leave out.

Worth watching all the same, because the direction of travel is clear enough.
Kakao owns the character, the webtoon platform and the distribution, and it has
now put a generation step in the middle of its own IP with a feature director's
name on top. That is a more durable arrangement than an AI company
[buying a webtoon platform outright](/blog/toptoon-owner-ankey-ax-ai-company/)
and hoping the catalogue cooperates.
