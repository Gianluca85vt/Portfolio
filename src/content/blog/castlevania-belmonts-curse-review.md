Reviews for Castlevania: Belmont's Curse went up on 13 October, two days
before the game's 15 October release — some storefront listings show the
14th, while Konami's own materials and the game's Steam news post say the
15th. Konami did not hold reviews to launch day,
which for a dormant franchise handed to an outside studio is a confident
decision.

Across the twelve scored verdicts I could verify and convert, the average is
**8.5**. The range runs from 6 to 10 — four points wide on a ten-point scale.
OpenCritic has it at 85 from fifty critics with 98 percent recommending;
Metacritic sat at 86 from forty-four at the time of writing. TheGamer worked
out that an 86 makes this the fifth highest-rated traditional Castlevania ever,
behind Circle of the Moon and Aria of Sorrow at 91, and Symphony of the Night
and Dawn of Sorrow at 89.

Four points is a lot of disagreement for a game nobody dislikes.

## Everyone agrees about the pictures

Read the twelve and the praise is interchangeable. The colour. The whip. The
music. GamesRadar+ calls it gothic brilliance. Polygon talks about bold colour
making the gothic visuals pop. Even VGC, at the bottom of the range, led its
headline with the game looking incredible.

Then the complaints, which are all about shape. Rock Paper Shotgun's verdict is
"a fantastic action game, a less than stellar Metroidvania". IGN's argument is
that it works better as a Metroidvania generally than as a Castlevania
specifically. Push Square, at 7, calls it perfectly fine and says longtime
genre players will find little new. RPGSite describes a frustrating start and a
late game that goes extremely easy. TheSixthAxis, at 8, picks on fast travel.

The arguments are all about the map and the pacing laid over it.

## Which is the one thing Evil Empire had never built

Evil Empire is the studio that spent years maintaining and expanding Dead
Cells, and Motion Twin advised on this. That lineage is not a footnote. It
describes a specific production pipeline, and the pipeline predicts which half
of this review spread you get.

A roguelike's levels are assembled at run time. The artists and designers build
a library of rooms and transitions that have to work in any order, because the
game will put them in any order. Every room gets judged on whether it reads
instantly, whether its silhouette is clear at speed, whether the player can
tell where the floor is in the first sixth of a second. That discipline makes
you extremely good at local craft: readability, animation weight, colour
separation, the frame-by-frame feel of a hit landing.

What it never asks you to do is author one map.

A Metroidvania map is a single object. It is one artefact where position carries
meaning — this door matters because of what is three rooms behind it, this
shortcut matters because of how long you spent walking the long way, this boss
gates that ability because the ability reframes a corridor you already hate. You
cannot iterate on that in pieces. You build the whole thing, walk it end to end,
find that the middle sags, and rebuild the whole thing. It is slow, it is
unglamorous, and getting it right is the entire difference between a
Metroidvania people replay for fifteen years and one they finish once.

So a studio with Dead Cells behind it and a Castlevania in front of it starts
with a surplus of exactly the skills that make rooms sing, and a deficit of the
one skill that makes a map sing. The reviews describe that distribution
precisely. Nobody questions the rooms. The people at 6 and 7 are describing
fast travel, backtracking and a difficulty curve — all of them properties of
the whole map rather than any part of it.

I should be careful here, because this is a reading from the outside. I have not
walked the castle. What I can say is that when twelve reviewers agree about
every local quality and split four points on structure, the production history
behind the split is usually visible, and in this case it is.

## The colour decision is a real one

One thing worth sitting with, because it is the part reviewers noticed first
without quite saying why.

Castlevania's 2D canon was built under hardware limits. Symphony of the Night
and the GBA and DS games ran on fixed palettes and tight sprite budgets, and a
lot of what people remember as masterful art direction was restraint that the
hardware enforced. Modern 2D has no such limit. You can put every colour on
screen, and the genre's instinct when you can is to desaturate everything into
brown and grey, because murk reads as gothic and murk hides inconsistency
between artists.

Choosing saturation in a vampire castle is a readability decision disguised as a
style decision. Bright colour means every silhouette has to hold up on its own,
because nothing is hiding in the dark. Reviewers describing the visuals as
popping are describing a team that backed itself.

## Three in a row, the same shape

This is now the third review on this blog in three weeks where critics agree on
the surface and split on the systems. [Silent Hill: Townfall averaged 8.0 across
a four-point spread, with reviewers calling the atmosphere franchise-best and
then arguing about the stealth](/blog/silent-hill-townfall-review/) — also a
dormant Konami property, also handed to an outside studio. A fortnight later,
[Hellraiser Revival's six verdicts ran from 4.5 to 8.5 while every one of them
liked how the Labyrinth looked](/blog/hellraiser-revival-review/).

I do not think that is a coincidence of the calendar. Look and feel are now
reliably achievable: the tools are mature, reference is abundant, and a good
art team with a clear brief lands them. Structure is not, because structure is
one person's judgement about an artefact too large to hold in your head, and no
tool helps. The craft floor has risen and the design ceiling has not moved, so
the variance in reviews migrated to where the hard problem still lives.

## The number

**8.5**, from twelve converted verdicts between 6 and 10. Famitsu's four
reviewers filed 8/8/8/8 for 32 out of 40, which is as close to a consensus
vote as that panel ever gives. The mean tracks both aggregators, so the figure
is not an artefact of which twelve I happened to find.

The four-point range matters more to a buyer than the mean does. Somebody who wants a
beautiful 2D action game with a whip is being told by everybody that this is
one. A buyer who wants the map that made Symphony of the Night worth
twenty-nine years of imitation is reading twelve reviews that do not agree, and
should read the ones at the bottom before the ones at the top.
