Sega put *Sonic the Hedgehog: Memories and Beyond* on YouTube on 12 November
2026, free, to mark the franchise's thirty-fifth year. Around thirteen minutes
of hand-drawn animation. Eggman gets hold of the Chaos Emeralds and sets about
upgrading Metal Sonic with Sonic's own life data; Tails works out a counter;
Knuckles, Shadow, Amy and Rouge are all in there.

Try to find out who drew it.

The director is named everywhere: Kevin Molina-Ortiz. Ian Flynn wrote the
script, which will surprise nobody who has read a Sonic comic in the last
fifteen years, and the story is credited to Sonic Team's Takashi Iizuka. All
three turn up in every write-up, consistently spelled, consistently credited.

The animation studio does not. One account has the short produced by Studio
GIGGEX and animated by SIMAGE Animations. Others print GXS Productions as the
animation house and stop there. When the short was announced at Anime Expo in
July, The Sonic Stadium read the panel and concluded it was safe to assume
Giggex, which is the sound of a reporter working from inference because the
press material did not say.

I have not sat down with the short frame by frame — this is written from the
coverage and from Sega's own material, a day after it went up.

## Why the name matters more than it looks

Thirteen minutes of 2D is not a weekend. Strip the romance off it and you have
layout, key animation, in-betweens, clean-up, colour, backgrounds, compositing
and a mix, and every one of those is a department with people in it. Even on a
brisk schedule that is months of work by dozens of artists, most of them on a
contract that ends when the short does.

A commissioning brand gets the title card. A director gets the press release.
The studio that put pencils on the thing gets a line in the credit roll that
nobody transcribes, and if two outlets disagree about which studio that even
was, nobody is going to settle it.

It matters because that credit is the only portable thing most of those artists
take with them. A studio name on a well-received short is how a 2D house wins
the next commission, and how the individual in-betweener points at something
and says that was us. Blur it and the work still exists; the career evidence
does not.

The flipside is worth saying plainly: brand shorts of this kind are good jobs.
A thirteen-minute piece on a famous property with no runtime pressure from a
broadcaster is the sort of brief 2D crews rarely get. Which is the argument for
naming the people who took it.

## The director is the real signal

Molina-Ortiz came out of *Rise of the Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles*, a show with
a very particular grammar — loose, aggressive drawing, characters stretched
hard through a movement and snapped back, poses that break proportion in service
of the beat. Toon Boom lists him among its ambassadors, which tells you Harmony
and tells you a rigged-and-deformed 2D pipeline rather than a paper one.

Now put that against Sonic. The character has one of the most tightly policed
style guides in games. Quill count, eye geometry, the exact arc of the shoes —
all of it is locked down across three decades of licensing, because a brand that
appears on lunchboxes cannot afford drift. Sonic's on-model sheet is a legal
document as much as an artistic one.

So the question to ask of the short is how far the drawing is allowed off model
when the action gets going, and whether Sega's licensing people let the smears
through. A director hired for a loose hand, working inside a style guide built
to prevent exactly that, is a genuine tension, and it is the one thing in this
production that could not have been predicted from the press release.

Sonic's animated output has swung between hand-drawn and CG for years, and the
choice is rarely about which looks better. It is about what the material wants
and what the schedule can carry — the same calculation that decides whether
[a cast is made of something a renderer is already good at](/blog/cosmic-mimimi-ichikawa-after-the-gems/).
Quills, speed lines and impact frames sit on the 2D side of that line without
much argument.

## One number drifted

When the short was unveiled at Anime Expo in July it was billed at around ten
minutes. It landed at about thirteen.

Three minutes sounds like nothing. On a hand-drawn short it is close to a third
more footage, and a third more footage is a third more key animation, more
in-betweens, more clean-up, more comp. Nobody adds that by accident at the back
end of a schedule. Either the cut earned its length in edit and somebody signed
off the extra, or the ten-minute figure was a rough estimate given to a panel
audience months before picture lock. Both happen. Neither is reported.

Both voice casts came back, which is the other quiet sign that somebody cared
about this one. Roger Craig Smith, Colleen O'Shaughnessey and Dave B. Mitchell
on the English side; Jun-ichi Kanemaru, who has voiced Sonic in Japanese since
the late nineties, and Kotaro Nakamura as Eggman. Anniversary pieces often make
do with whoever is cheap and available.

A short film is the cheapest honest test there is of whether an idea and a crew
hold up — it drags layout, colour, comp and mix into daylight in a way a sizzle
reel cannot fake, which is exactly why
[ABEMA is buying four-minute pilots rather than series commitments](/blog/prismation-open-call-three-anime-pilots/).
Sega has just run that test at thirteen minutes, in public, for free, and the
result is sitting on YouTube.

It would be useful to know whose test it was.
