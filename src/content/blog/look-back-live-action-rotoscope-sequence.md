Small staff announcement in the feeds today, easy to scroll past. The official site for the
live-action **Look Back** — Hirokazu Kore-eda's adaptation of Tatsuki Fujimoto's 2021 one-shot,
opening in Japan on **11 September 2026** — named three guest staff.

Kenshi Yonezu plays guitar on the film, credited as *"Shark Kick Guitar"* after the manga Fujino
serialises inside the story. Tōko Miura sings an insert song. And **Yōko Kuno directs a
rotoscoped animation sequence built on that performance**, with Shin-Ei Animation producing it.

The first two are casting-adjacent fun. The third is a production decision, and it's the one I
want to talk about, because it's Kore-eda telling you in advance how he plans to shoot the
unshootable part of this story.

## The thing a camera can't point at

Look Back is about drawing. Not about two girls who happen to draw — about the physical,
compulsive act of putting line on paper, and what it costs and what it gives back. Fujimoto's
whole method in the one-shot is to let the panel construction *be* the feeling: the pages where
Fujino draws for years in a single hunched pose, the ones where a body just moves through a frame
with no dialogue at all.

A live-action camera can film a hand, a pen, a page, a face. It cannot film the interior surge
that the manga is actually made of. That's the adaptation problem, and it's not a small one.

The 2024 anime never had to solve it, because it cheated in the best possible way — it *was*
drawing. Kiyotaka Oshiyama and Studio Durian deliberately shipped the film with the production
noise still in frame: eraser ghosts, coloured layout lines, the wobble of a pencil that hadn't
been cleaned up. The medium and the subject were the same substance, so the film could argue its
point without ever stating it.

Kore-eda gives all of that up the moment he points a camera at Natsuki Deguchi and Aju Makita in
a farmhouse in Nikaho. So he's buying a piece of it back, for one sequence, from a specialist.

## Why rotoscope specifically, and not "an animated bit"

There are a dozen ways to put animation inside a live-action film. Almost all of them cut.

Rotoscope is the exception, and it's the exception for a mechanical reason: **it's the only
animation technique whose input is a plate.** You shoot the performance for real, then draw on top
of it, frame by frame. Which means the timing, the weight, the parallax, the specific micro-hesitation
of a real person shifting their grip — none of that gets re-authored. It survives into the drawn
version untouched. Only the surface changes.

That distinction matters more than people expect, and I've run into it repeatedly from the
technical-art side. When you drop a heavily stylised shader over a photoreal base, or push a
non-photoreal pass on top of scanned geometry, the result holds together far better than it has
any right to — as long as the motion and the parallax underneath are unchanged. Swap the surface
and the eye goes along with it. Re-key the motion and the whole thing reads as a different scene
grafted in, no matter how carefully you match the palette.

So: a rotoscoped sequence isn't a cutaway to animation. It's the same take, restated. The audience
is looking at the identical performance in a different material, which is a much stranger and more
useful effect than a cut to a drawn insert, and it's almost exactly the register Look Back needs —
the world seen the way the person drawing it sees it.

Kuno is the obvious hire for this and not a fashionable one. She co-directed **Ghost Cat Anzu**
(2024), which was shot live-action first and rotoscoped in full, and she worked on Shunji Iwai's
*The Case of Hana & Alice* back in 2015, also rotoscoped. Shin-Ei Animation — the Doraemon and
Crayon Shin-chan house — produced Anzu with her too, and is producing this. That's a team
reassembling, not a name being borrowed for a poster.

## The part nobody puts in the press release

Rotoscoping does not interpolate.

That's the whole economic story of the technique. In conventional animation you can key a pose,
key another, and let inbetweens or a curve carry the middle. Rotoscope has no middle to give away.
Every single frame is a drawing, and every single drawing is *constrained* — it has to sit on the
plate, match a real silhouette, and still be a deliberate stylistic choice rather than a trace.
Trace it too faithfully and you get the uncanny stiffness rotoscope is infamous for. Depart too
far and you lose the motion fidelity that was the entire reason to shoot it in the first place.

Kuno has been candid that this was hard on Anzu, partly because Shin-Ei wasn't a rotoscope house
and had to learn it on the job. Doing it again for one sequence inside someone else's live-action
feature is a narrower brief but not an easier one, because now the sequence has to survive being
adjacent to photographed reality on both sides.

## What I'll actually be looking at

The seam. Every time.

There are only really three ways in and out of something like this, and they read completely
differently:

- **Hard cut.** Cheapest, and it announces itself as a device. Fine if the device is the point.
- **Dissolve or a graded ramp.** The surface migrates while the motion continues. Hardest to
  execute, and by far the most likely to sell the idea that you never left the shot.
- **A diegetic hand-off** — a page, a window, a reflection carrying you across. Kore-eda is not a
  showy director, and Look Back hands you an obvious one, since the story is full of paper.

Whichever it is, the tell will be whether the drawn section keeps the camera's own imperfections:
the breathing of a handheld frame, a rack focus, a lens flare that has no business existing in a
drawing. Keeping those is the expensive, correct choice, and it's the difference between an
animated interlude and a shot that simply changed material halfway through.

Anyway — file this as the most interesting thing announced this week that was framed as a music
credit.
