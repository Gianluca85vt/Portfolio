A production cel is a sheet of acetate with paint on one side and ink on the
other. The paint goes on in flat fields by hand, which means it has thickness,
and thickness means the edge of a colour field sits very slightly above the
sheet. Stack three or four of those under a camera, light them, and you are
photographing a small piece of physical sculpture. Dust lands on them. The
registration pegs hold each layer in roughly, not exactly, the same place.

All of that is in the negative. For thirty-three years almost nobody has seen
it, because every format the film reached — VHS, laserdisc, DVD, a 1080p
transfer — threw away more detail than the cels contained.

*Ninja Scroll* goes back into cinemas this week with that ceiling lifted.

## What is actually shipping

HIDIVE and Iconic Events Releasing are putting a new 4K restoration of Yoshiaki
Kawajiri's 1993 film into roughly **600 screens** across the United States and
Canada, on three dates: 4 October subtitled, 5 October in the English dub, and
a 7 October encore running whichever format the individual theatre picked. A 4K
UHD SteelBook follows in early 2027. The restoration had its world premiere at
the Berlinale back in February.

The spec, as announced: a 4K scan of the original 35mm negative at 3840x2160,
then frame-by-frame damage repair and colour correction, producing a new
archival master. Sentai produced it. Kawajiri supervised.

## Scanning the negative is the whole decision

Film duplication is generational. Camera negative to interpositive to internegative
to release print, and each hop costs resolution and adds contrast. A lot of
nineties anime reissues were pulled off whatever element was nearest to hand,
usually an interpositive, because the negative was in a vault in Tokyo and
getting at it is a legal and logistical problem rather than a technical one.

You can see the difference without any equipment. A scan from a print has
blacks that have already been crushed once and highlights that have already
clipped once, and no amount of grading recovers what the duplication stage
removed. A scan from the negative starts with the full latitude the
cinematographer exposed for.

So "original 35mm negative" in a press release is the line worth reading twice.
It means somebody did the unglamorous half of the job.

## The pass that ruins these

Here is where cel-animation restorations go wrong, and they have gone wrong a
lot. A 4K scan of a 1993 negative arrives full of things that look like faults:
film grain, dust that was sitting on the cel in 1993, faint seams where one
acetate layer overlaps another, a shimmer along painted edges as registration
drifts a fraction of a millimetre between frames.

The temptation is to clean all of it. Digital noise reduction will take the
grain out, and it will also take out the finest variation in an inked line —
the place where the brush ran dry, the slight swell at the end of a stroke.
Push it and faces go waxy, linework thins toward nothing, and flat paint
flattens further into something that looks like vector fill. The film stops
looking photographed and starts looking generated.

Which is the same argument this blog had about [a generative pass over finished
art](/blog/dlss-5-leak-generative-pass-finished-art/) from the other direction.
Inventing plausible detail and recovering real detail are different operations
that produce superficially similar pictures, and the second one is harder,
slower and worth more. A restoration's grain is evidence. Scrub it and you have
a guess.

Dust is the judgement call nobody agrees on. Some of it is genuinely a fault —
a hair in the gate, a scratch from a projector. Some of it was on the cel when
the camera fired, which makes it part of the photographed image, and a frame
with no dust in it never existed. I have sat through enough before-and-after
reels to know that the aggressive clean always looks better in a ten-second
comparison and worse over ninety minutes.

## Why Kawajiri's name on it matters

Supervision by the original director is not a courtesy credit on a job like
this. Three or four passes in a restoration are pure taste, and the only person
who can settle them is the one who signed off the original answer print.

Kawajiri's look is specific and fragile. He builds shots out of hard-edged
shadow shapes — a face half in black, a figure read entirely as silhouette
against a lit wall — with a limited palette and an enormous amount of the frame
given over to near-black. That is a style that lives or dies on where the
shadow detail sits. Lift the blacks in a grade and the shapes go soft and
grey. Crush them to look punchy on a modern display and the shapes weld
together into one mass, which reads as cheap rather than dramatic.

A colourist working alone optimises for a waveform monitor. A director who
remembers which blacks were meant to hold a little detail and which were meant
to be a hole optimises for the film.

If you want the physical version of this argument, the Ghost in the Shell
retrospective made the case better than any transfer can: the piece on [what
those hand-painted backgrounds look like up
close](/blog/ghost-in-the-shell-exhibition-backgrounds/) is about standing in
front of the artwork and seeing the brush decisions. A good 4K scan is the
nearest a cinema gets to that.

## One number to be slightly suspicious of

3840x2160 is UHD. It is the resolution a television wants. A 35mm frame scanned
properly supports more than that — 4096 wide is the standard digital cinema
figure, and scanner operators will go to 6K or 8K on an archival pass precisely
so that the master outlives the current display generation.

Announcing 3840x2160 suggests the delivery target was the consumer release as
much as the theatrical run. That is not a scandal and it is a long way above
anything this film has had before. It does mean the SteelBook in early 2027 is
probably the full resolution of the project rather than a reduction from it.

What to watch for, if you go: the blacks in the interior scenes, and whether the
line on a face holds its weight when the face is small in frame. Those two tell
you how heavy the clean-up hand was. Everything else about this film has been
settled for three decades.
