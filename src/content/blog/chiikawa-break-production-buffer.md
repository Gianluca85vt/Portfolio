Three hundred and eighty-one episodes since April 2022. That is the number to
sit with before anything else. Roughly two hundred and thirty-four weeks have
passed since the Chiikawa anime started, which puts it at a little over one and
a half episodes a week, every week, for four and a half years, with no cour
structure to hide behind.

On Friday the official site said the show is stopping. Episode 381 went out on
25 September; from 2 October the slot carries a "revival" rebroadcast of 25
older episodes instead, running through 25 December, with new episodes back in
January 2027. The 25 are curated rather than sequential — early instalments, and
ones connected to *Eiga Chiikawa: Ningyo no Shima no Himitsu*, the film Toho put
in Japanese cinemas on 24 July.

Thirteen weeks of no new animation.

## It did this last year too

March 2025: the same announcement, reruns in the slot, new episodes back in
July. Call it fifteen weeks. So this is the second time in eighteen months that
Chiikawa has taken roughly a quarter off, on a published schedule, with the
return date attached to the announcement.

That repetition is what makes it worth writing about. A one-off break reads as
trouble — a director off sick, a sponsor pulling out, a studio underwater. Two
of them, the same length, the same shape, announced the same way, reads as a
production that has measured its own throughput and built the recovery into the
calendar.

## Why a minute is not cheap

This is the part that gets lost by people who see the runtime and assume the
work scales with it.

I spend my days on shots, and the thing you learn early is that effort does not
divide neatly by duration. A ten-second shot and a sixty-second shot are not six
times apart. The fixed costs sit at the front and the back and refuse to shrink:
you still storyboard it, still do layout, still cut a timing sheet, still book
the voice session and the studio time around it, still comp it, still master and
deliver it to a broadcaster with its own specification and its own deadline. The
bit in the middle that genuinely scales with length is the drawing, and on a
show this stylised the drawing is not where most of the hours go either.

Per second of finished animation, short-form is the expensive end of the
business. It looks like the cheap end because the invoice is small.

Now multiply. Three hundred and eighty-one of those, each with its own board,
its own timing, its own session, its own delivery. Six and a bit hours of
finished animation in total, which sounds modest until you count it as 381
separate deliveries rather than one long one.

## What a buffer actually is

Throughput and capacity are different numbers, and a production can run above
the second one for a surprisingly long time by drawing down a stock of finished
work. Every schedule like this has a bank of completed episodes sitting ahead of
broadcast. Some weeks you put more in than you take out. Most weeks, on a
cadence like this, you do not.

When the bank empties there are two options. Stop, or ship anyway.

Anime's usual answer is the second one, and everybody watching knows what it
looks like — the episode where the faces go off-model, the cut that was clearly
finished by a studio that had never seen the show, the held frame that sits two
seconds longer than the timing wants because nothing was ready to cut to. The
work gets worse in public and the people who made it know exactly why.

Chiikawa is choosing to stop, and saying so in September for a break that starts
in October. Thirteen weeks is not a gesture. That is a quarter of a year of slot
time handed back to keep the show at the level it has been at, and somebody had
to sign off on the revenue side of that.

## Where the film went

One detail worth pulling out: *Ningyo no Shima no Himitsu* was not made by the
television team. The series is Doga Kobo. The film went to Cygames Pictures,
directed by Kei Oikawa.

That is normal enough — features and weekly television are different disciplines
with different staffing — but it also means the movie did not consume the TV
buffer directly. The break is not the film's bill coming due. It is the ordinary
cadence needing its ordinary recovery, arriving where it was always going to
arrive.

And the rerun selection has a second job. Twenty-five episodes weighted towards
the film's material, running from October to Christmas, is a promotional window
wearing a hiatus as a coat. The slot keeps working. The audience keeps the habit.
Nobody has to fill thirteen weeks with something new.

## The shape of the answer

There are two other ways studios respond to this pressure, and the blog has
looked at both recently.

One is to change the tooling so the same people produce more. That is the
argument behind [Level-5 compressing five-year projects into
two](/blog/level-5-ai-apology-five-to-two-plan/) — raise output per head and the
ceiling moves. The other is to change the medium: [Orange bending 3D models
until they read as hand-drawn](/blog/studio-orange-3d-anime-2d-look/), where the
promise is asset reuse and the catch is how much of that reuse the style gives
back.

Chiikawa is doing the third thing, which nobody writes admiring pieces about.
Accepting the ceiling, and stopping underneath it on purpose, twice now, with
the dates printed.

Whether it holds is the thing to watch in January. A break that keeps its return
date is a buffer. A break that slips becomes the other kind, and nobody announces
that one in advance.
