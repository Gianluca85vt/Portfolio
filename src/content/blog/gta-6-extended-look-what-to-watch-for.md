Rockstar is putting the next look at Grand Theft Auto VI on **Netflix** before
anywhere else. Here are the times, and then the part I actually care about.

## When and where

| | Netflix | YouTube and rockstargames.com |
|---|---|---|
| Los Angeles | 12pm PT | 6pm PT |
| New York | **3pm ET** | 9pm ET |
| London | **8pm BST** | 2am BST (28th) |
| Rome, Berlin, Paris | **9pm CEST** | 3am CEST (28th) |
| Tokyo | 4am JST (28th) | 10am JST (28th) |

About thirty minutes. Netflix holds it exclusively for six hours, then it
goes up on Rockstar's own channel and the Grand Theft Auto VI site.

Creators may livestream it from the Netflix premiere onward, but only with
their own commentary over it — a straight rebroadcast of the video is not
allowed. So the usual channels will be running it live tonight with someone
talking over the top, which for once is the format Rockstar wants.

The game itself is dated 19 November 2026, on PS5 and Xbox Series X|S.

## The phrase in the fine print

Rockstar's own description says the footage is **captured entirely in-game on
PlayStation 5**.

That is a stronger claim than the one most publishers make, and it is worth
knowing why. "In-engine" only means the renderer that ships the game produced
the pixels — it can still have taken four seconds a frame on a workstation with
nothing else in memory. "Captured in-game on PS5" names the hardware and says
the machine you can buy produced it.

It still leaves room. Captured in-game does not mean captured in one take, at a
locked frame rate, by someone holding a controller. Thirty minutes of footage
cut for a premiere has been chosen, ordered and paced. The claim is about where
the pixels came from, not about what a Tuesday evening with the game will feel
like.

## Four things worth looking at

If you want more out of tonight than the plot, these are the places where a
curated thirty minutes usually gives itself away.

Watch the camera when it stops. Real capture has a person on the stick: a
slight overshoot when a pan halts, a correction, a hesitation before the next
move. Footage assembled on splines moves too cleanly, and once you have noticed
it you cannot stop noticing it.

Watch what happens at the horizon. Rockstar's open worlds live or die on
streaming — how the far half of the map is loaded and swapped while you move
through it. The tell is not pop-in, which they will have edited around. It is
whether the camera ever travels fast in a straight line toward something
distant. That is the manoeuvre that exposes streaming, and the one a trailer
editor avoids.

Watch the crowds and the traffic. A dense street is expensive in a way a
pretty vista is not, because every pedestrian is a decision being made every
frame. If the busy shots are short and the long shots are quiet, that is the
budget talking.

Watch the weather change on screen. Leonida is Florida, so rain and storm
are the setting's signature. A weather transition that happens *during* a shot,
rather than between two shots, is much harder to fake and much more expensive
to run.

## Why it is on Netflix at all

The business half of this is more interesting than it looks.

A trailer is marketing, and marketing wants the widest possible reach at the
lowest possible friction. Putting it behind a subscription for six hours does
the opposite on purpose: it converts the most anticipated thirty minutes in
games into something Netflix can advertise as programming.

Which tells you what Rockstar thinks it has. You do not lease your marketing to
a streaming service unless you are confident the audience will follow it there
— and confident enough that the six-hour delay costs you nothing, because
everyone who missed it will still be waiting at 9pm Eastern.

I will be watching the horizon.
