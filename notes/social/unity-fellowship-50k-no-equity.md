## LinkedIn

Unity announced the Unity Fellowship on 16 November: ten indie developers, $50,000 each, no equity, no repayment, no revenue share, no claim on the IP. Half a million dollars, with the only hard conditions being that you build in Unity and plan to launch inside twelve months.

That twelve-month window is why the programme is interesting to me as an artist rather than as a headline.

Ask anyone who has worked the back half of a project where the money ran out and you get the same answer in different words: somewhere after the vertical slice looked good, somewhere before the game actually worked. That stretch is where the art debt comes due. Placeholder materials that read fine in the trailer angle have to read fine from every other angle. Lightmaps get rebaked once someone finally stops moving the geometry. Somebody goes through and makes the LODs stop popping. Somebody fixes the eighty assets whose pivots are in the wrong place. The UI gets rebuilt because it was laid out for one language and the game ships in nine.

None of that is in a pitch deck. All of it is visible in the first review.

It is also the part nobody funds, because a committee wants to see the thing that proves the game is special, and the thing that proves the game is special was finished eighteen months ago. What is left to show is a task list.

Will $50,000 cover it? On most teams it buys a few months of one contractor: an environment artist to finish the levels nobody got to, a tech artist to go through the shaders, an audio pass, a localisation run. Months of runway, then — and months of runway at that point are often the whole difference between shipping the version you meant and the version you had.

Cash with no recoup is rare. Most money offered to an indie at this stage is an advance, which is a loan with a nicer name. Ten teams is a small number against the size of the problem. The aim is still pointed at the right place.

Unity Fellowship, announced 16 Nov: ten indie teams, $50,000 each. No equity, no repayment, no revenue share, no IP claim. Build in Unity, plan to launch inside twelve months. Half a million dollars total.

The twelve-month condition is the part worth reading twice. It aims the money at the last stretch before launch, which is the stretch nobody funds, because by then there is nothing left to demo. Only a task list.

That stretch is where art debt comes due. Placeholder materials that read fine in the trailer angle have to read from every angle. Lightmaps rebaked once geometry finally stops moving. LOD pops. Eighty assets with pivots in the wrong place. UI rebuilt for nine languages.

What $50k actually buys on most teams: a few months of one contractor. An environment artist to finish the levels nobody got to, a tech artist through the shaders, an audio pass, a localisation run. Months of runway rather than a year of payroll.

Worth saying what makes it unusual: no recoup. Most money offered to an indie at this stage is an advance, a platform deal, or a publishing cut that lasts forever. A grant that asks for nothing back is a marketing budget, and Unity has reasons to want one.

Ten teams against the size of the problem is small. Valve's build review queue hit twelve business days this month, and Early Access moved the art deadline to day one rather than removing it. The final leg keeps getting more expensive.
