Nintendo shipped Ocarina of Time on Switch 2 on 5 November and said it was built in house, by EPD Production Group No. 3. Four days later, two other companies published pages on their own corporate sites describing what they had made for it.

Bandai Namco Studios lists partial production of artwork and visual assets: characters, terrain, animations, cutscenes and UI. Monolith Soft lists partial contract development across game design, programming and graphics — its fifth mainline Zelda, going back to Skyward Sword in 2011.

Read that first list again. Characters, terrain, animations, cutscenes, UI. That is five departments, each with its own pipeline, its own naming conventions, its own long-running argument about where the rig ends and the animation begins. Handing all five out is commissioning the art.

Which tells you where the hard part of outsourcing art sits. Nintendo had to write a specification precise enough that a team in another building could build Hyrule Field and have it come back looking like Hyrule Field — fog falloff, grass density, how far Link's silhouette reads against a hill at dusk, and which of the 1998 decisions were taste rather than the N64 talking. That document is harder to produce than the meshes.

It also explains the schedule. EPD Group No. 3 is the Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom team, and they were busy through 2026. A full asset rebuild needs a hundred people for a year who are not on that org chart.

The part I keep coming back to: in the game's credits, Monolith Soft appears under Special Thanks. Both studios disclosed this work themselves, on their own websites, because the credits did not. That is standard in Japanese co-development and has been for twenty years. It still means the people who modelled the Gorons cannot point at a line that says so, and a credit is a portfolio.
