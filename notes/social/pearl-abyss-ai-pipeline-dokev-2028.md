## LinkedIn

Pearl Abyss has told investors it is expanding generative AI across its entire development pipeline. In March it apologised for generative AI props shipping inside Crimson Desert.

Both halves of that are about the same shelf of assets, and it is the shelf nobody protects: 2D props. The painting on a tavern wall, the poster, the book cover on a shelf, the pattern in a rug. Thousands of them in a world that size. Scheduled last, cut first, never praised in a review.

The March explanation was that those props were early-stage iteration that never got replaced. I believe it, and it is worth saying that this is an entirely ordinary failure. Placeholder art has been shipping in games for thirty years.

But the gate that normally catches it is one person's eye passing over an asset in a review, and that gate only works because placeholder art is loud. A grey box announces itself. So does a checkerboard, or a texture with PLACEHOLDER across it in 72pt.

A generated painting is quiet. It has composition, a palette, brush texture, a frame. It reads as finished at a glance, from three metres, in engine, under the lighting it will ship under — which is the only distance and the only lighting a decorative prop ever gets looked at. The giveaways sit where nobody inspects a prop: the hands on a background figure, the text on a spine, a perspective that does not resolve.

Then a player walks up to the wall, because that is what an open world invites you to do.

So "strengthening internal processes" has a concrete meaning here. A flag that travels with the asset, from whatever generated it through to the build, loud enough to fail a review a human eye would otherwise pass. That is a tooling problem, and it is solvable.

The item on Pearl Abyss's list I would argue with is a different one: using AI to validate design concepts before implementation, on DokeV. Generated concepts average toward the middle. DokeV's whole proposition is a look that resembles nothing else.
