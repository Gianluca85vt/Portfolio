## LinkedIn

Every account of Godzilla Minus One's visual effects Oscar leads with the same number: thirty-five people, in one room at Shirogumi in Tokyo, somewhere between 610 and 620 shots.

Godzilla Minus Zero opens in Japan on 3 November and in North America on 6 November, and it is being sold as the first Japanese production filmed for IMAX. Takashi Yamazaki is back as director, writer and VFX supervisor. Shirogumi is back on the effects. What changed is the container, and for anyone who works on simulation it is the most interesting thing about the film before anybody has seen a frame of it.

IMAX full frame is 1.43:1. Against the 2.39:1 these films usually finish in, the difference is not width. It is height. A wide crop is quietly generous: ceilings, upper floors, the ground under a creature's feet — a great deal of an environment never has to exist, and artists have budgeted around that crop for as long as it has existed. A near-square frame puts sky and pavement in the same shot as the monster. Set extensions grow upward. Digital buildings need roofs. The creature is lit by a sky that is now visible rather than implied.

Then the arithmetic. Raise delivery resolution and a render slows roughly in proportion to the pixels — annoying, predictable, solvable with more machines. A simulation is solved on a grid, and holding detail at that frame size means raising the grid. Cost climbs with the cube of the increase, and so does the cache on disk.

Which explains why Yamazaki's two statements about the sequel point the same way: every effects shot is more complex than Minus One's, and the extra money went on more people. Compute buys render farm. It does not buy iterations on a sim that solves overnight, and iterations are what a destruction shot is made of.

No shot count and no crew size have been published for Minus Zero. Worth remembering next week, when everyone wants to compare it to thirty-five.
