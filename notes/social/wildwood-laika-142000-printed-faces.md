## LinkedIn

On Coraline in 2009, LAIKA printed around 20,000 replacement faces and then had to paint every single one by hand. One of Coraline's faces reportedly took over a week to finish, because the printer only laid down one colour of resin and the freckles were a brush job. That painting cost was the ceiling on how expressive a character could be.

Wildwood has more than 142,000 faces.

What changed is not really the sculpting. It is the colour pipeline. The faces come off Stratasys J750 and J850 PolyJet machines that jet photopolymer in full colour, driven by software called Cuttlefish out of Fraunhofer IGD. Cuttlefish takes a coloured 3D model and works out how to stack translucent resin so the printed object matches the albedo an artist signed off on a monitor. Resin is not ink and it is not a display — it scatters light under its own surface. Anyone who has pushed a subsurface material through a render and then through a print knows exactly how many afternoons that eats.

So the handmade film has a substantial software stage in the middle of it. Every one of those expressions was modelled, textured and colour-checked in a DCC package before it existed as an object. And 142,000 unique parts, each tied to a frame of a specific shot, is an asset-management problem before it is an art problem: naming conventions, version control, and a shelving system a human can work at speed. Nobody hands out awards for that, and if it fails the animation stops.

Around it the old crafts held. Armatures hold the poses, magnets hold the faces on, and eyes still get nudged by hand frame by frame. Over 2,700 shots. More than 220 puppets. Roughly 231 weeks of animation. Sixteen years from book to screen.

Then the arithmetic. $12.2 million domestic opening, an A CinemaScore, the best reviews the studio has had — against a reported budget near $100 million. The pipeline worked. The sums are still tight.
