## LinkedIn

Film Heritage Foundation's 4K restoration of Pakeezah premiered at Festival Lumière this month. The thing I keep thinking about is that none of it came from the film's camera negative, because when archivists opened the cans at Ramnord Lab in 2024 the negative had almost melted.

So the restoration was rebuilt from duplicates: a 35mm interpositive, a CinemaScope release print, and a colour reversal internegative held by the NFDC-National Film Archive of India. Two years of work at L'Immagine Ritrovata in Bologna, with Lionheart Cinema.

Every one of those elements is a photographic copy of a copy, and each dupe stage adds grain, softens every edge, compresses the contrast at both ends and shifts the colour. None of that comes back. Which means the 4K number describes the scanner, not the information. A 4K scan of a dupe is a very precise record of a generation of loss.

The condition report also lists vinegar syndrome, and that one is a geometry problem more than a chemistry problem. Acetate base hydrolyses, the acid it releases catalyses more hydrolysis, and the base shrinks and warps. The film stops sitting flat at the gate and the perforation pitch stops being constant, so every frame needs its own stabilisation and its own warp correction. That is the part of a restoration timeline nobody sees.

The hardest call was colour. The internegative had faded, and Eastmancolor fades unevenly — cyan dies first, and the shift varies along the roll and with density, so no single curve puts it back. Nobody shot a reference chart on a Bombay set in the sixties. The grade was reconstructed from period stills and from what Kamal Amrohi's children remember the film looking like.

That is the nearest thing to a control sample that exists. It is also a reading rather than a match, and it becomes the canonical Pakeezah the moment it ships. Worth labelling honestly.
