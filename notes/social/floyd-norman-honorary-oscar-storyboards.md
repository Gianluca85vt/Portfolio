Floyd Norman did clean-up and inbetween drawings on Sleeping Beauty, released in 1959. Forty-two years later his name is on Monsters, Inc. as an additional story artist. On Sunday he collected an honorary Oscar at the Academy's Governors Awards.

Think about what happened to animation in those four decades.

Inbetweening was the biggest body of headcount in a cel studio — a room of people drawing the frames between an animator's keys. In a 3D package that is a curve in the graph editor, recomputed every time you scrub. Ink and paint was its own department with its own hierarchy, and it ended with CAPS, the system Disney built with Pixar: one shot at the end of The Little Mermaid in 1989, then a whole feature with The Rescuers Down Under. The multiplane camera was a physical tower of painted glass somebody cranked by hand. Now it is a transform on a null.

Storyboarding came through all of that with the work basically unchanged, and I think the reason is structural rather than sentimental.

Every other department hands an asset downstream. Modelling hands over geometry. Rigging hands over a control hierarchy. Layout hands over cameras with numbers attached. The moment something becomes an asset, software starts absorbing the manual parts of making it. That has been the direction of travel for forty years.

A board hands over a decision. Where the camera is, what is out of frame, which beat lands on the cut. The drawing is just the cheapest notation for that decision, and you throw boards away by the hundred. Nothing downstream opens the board file. So it never became a technical problem, and nobody got to automate it.

One honest caveat: on a CG feature, 3D layout can and does restage a boarded shot, because what reads on paper does not always read at 35mm with the walls where they really are. Boarding kept its craft and gave up some of its authority.

Norman turned 91 in June. The statuette arrives seventy years after he joined Disney.
