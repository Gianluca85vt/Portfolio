## LinkedIn

Epic is removing the clause in the Unreal Engine EULA that blocks AI training on the engine's output. Tim Sweeney confirmed it on 27 October — in a reply on X, with no published text and no effective date.

Worth being precise about what "output" means, because the word sounds like exhaust.

The output of Unreal Engine is a Sequencer render of a level somebody lit. It is a lightmap bake that took four hours and three attempts. It is a MetaHuman animation curve set, which is a recording of a face — usually a real one, captured on a stage and retargeted onto a rig. Beauty pass, depth pass, velocity buffers: exactly the set of images a diffusion model would most like to be fed.

An artist's output and the engine's output are the same file. There is no version of "training on engine output" that does not mean training on somebody's lookdev, somebody's animation, somebody's art direction. The engine contributes the maths. What makes one UE5 frame worth learning from and another worthless is a person. That is why the clause was drafted in the first place.

Sweeney's caveat is that each game stays under the terms its developer chooses. True, and narrower than it reads. The person who sets a game's terms is whoever signs the publishing agreement — not the environment artist who spent nine months on the city, not the contractor who did the facial capture on a two-week booking. Studio-level consent has never been artist-level consent.

One thing I would not overstate: a EULA restriction binds licensees, so striking it lifts the bar in every direction, not just Epic's. Copyright on the assets themselves does not move. Whether anything trained on this material stays inside Epic's models or gets licensed onward is unknown until the text is published.

Read your own contract. Specifically the derived-works and machine-learning language, because the engine's terms are no longer the backstop they were three weeks ago.
