## LinkedIn

MAPPA is fifteen this year, and the anniversary exhibition at the Yurakucho Museum in Tokyo is a good illustration of a problem the whole industry has: the most important part of a modern animation pipeline cannot be put behind glass.

What the show hangs is paper. Original drawings from Chainsaw Man – The Movie: Reze Arc, from Jujutsu Kaisen, from Attack on Titan: The Final Season. Cels, storyboards, twenty-three productions' worth. And in the Final Season room, a hand-painted watercolour of the hill from the ending.

That watercolour is the only exhibit that is close to a finished frame, and the reason is structural. A background is authored complete — somebody paints it, and what they painted is nearly what reaches the screen. A key animation drawing is not like that. It is pencil, timing notes and corrections, handed downstream to be cleaned up, scanned, digitally painted, re-weighted, filtered and graded. A brilliant object, and an instruction rather than a result.

Ask anyone to describe what MAPPA's work looks like and you get answers about surface: the heat shimmer, the grain, the way a light source blooms, fights that move the camera through space instead of panning across a held cel. Almost none of that is decided on paper. It is decided in satsuei — the photography department, which in a digital studio means compositing — and in layout, where the camera gets placed before any drawing exists.

So the showpieces got built as sets instead: a life-size Bomb lit from inside, a recreated train interior, a large installation of the Rumbling. Those are exactly the sequences where the work is a camera problem rather than a drawing problem, and a room you can walk into communicates a camera move better than a stack of genga does.

An honest exhibit would be a full layer breakdown — line art, paint, shadow, diffusion, grade, stepped out. I would stand in front of that for twenty minutes. I also understand why nobody builds it.

MAPPA EXPO runs to 7 December, then tours.
