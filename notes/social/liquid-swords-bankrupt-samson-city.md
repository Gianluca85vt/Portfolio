Twenty-eight critics reviewed Samson in April and most of them disliked it. Read the reviews anyway and the praise keeps landing in the same place: the city.

Liquid Swords — Christofer Sundberg's studio, founded in 2020 after he co-founded Avalanche Studios — was declared bankrupt by the Stockholm District Court on 12 October. The petition came from the staff, not a creditor. Around thirty people had gone unpaid since August. Sweden's wage guarantee should cover what they are owed, which is worth saying out loud, because an artist on a contract in most other places has nothing like it underneath them.

The part that interests me is why the reception had the shape it did. NetEase put roughly $20 million into the studio in November 2022 for a large action RPG. In February 2025 the studio confirmed layoffs and later reporting puts the cut at about half the team. What shipped on 8 April 2026, self-published at $24.99, was described in its own press release as a "focused hardboiled action game".

An open city is the most labour-expensive thing we build and also the most front-loaded. Blocks, facades, props, road meshes, a lighting pass — once that content is authored it is in the bank, and it does not rot when the team shrinks. It photographs well. It reviews well in the first hour.

Everything that turns a city into a game needs people in the room at the end. Mission triggers. Encounter pacing. Economy balance. The twelfth playtest where somebody says out loud that losing a day's in-game money to a scripting failure makes people quit. That work is concentrated in the final year and it does not survive halving a team.

So Samson's reviews have the shape of its layoff. The environment art held up. The systems around it are what critics found broken. Cirio, the appointed trustee, is taking bids on the remaining assets until the end of October.
