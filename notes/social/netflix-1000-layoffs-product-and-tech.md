## LinkedIn

Netflix cut around 1,000 roles today, roughly 6% of the company, and the majority of them sit in product, technology, legal and HR rather than in content. If you deliver work to streamers, that distribution is the part to read twice.

The content organisation came through nearly intact. Commissioning continues, the shows keep their release dates, and anyone whose studio depends on Netflix buying pictures can breathe out. I would not confuse that with the cut being cheap for the people who make the work.

Netflix's technology group is where a surprising amount of this industry's shared infrastructure came from. Their delivery specifications are the most prescriptive in the business and they are published rather than negotiated — IMF packaging, approved camera lists, colour and HDR requirements written down to the metadata field. Photon, the IMF validation tool, is open source, so post houses all over the world are checking their own packages with Netflix's code before shipping them anywhere at all. VMAF was co-invented there to replace PSNR in encoding decisions, then open-sourced, and it now lives in FFmpeg and most quality-aware encoding toolchains. Per-title encoding came out of the same group, per-shot after it.

What nobody has disclosed is which teams inside "product and technology" actually took the hit. That bucket also holds recommendations, ads, games, the player on every device ever made, and live infrastructure. A thousand people weighted toward product and tech tells you nothing about whether the encoding group lost two people or forty, and I would not trust a report claiming otherwise on day one.

So this is a watch item rather than a verdict. The symptoms would be slow and specific: how long partner engineering takes to answer a delivery question, whether the open-source repositories keep getting commits, whether the spec documents get their usual revisions.

A content team has a title and a release date. An encoding team's best year is one where nobody noticed anything.
