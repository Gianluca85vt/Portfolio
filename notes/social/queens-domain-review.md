## LinkedIn

Queen's Domain gives you no map, and that decision is an environment art budget before it is a design philosophy.

The game shipped on 30 October from Freshly Baked Games, a first-person crawler in the King's Field lineage: slow melee, save obelisks, no autosave, no minimap. Six scored reviews I could verify average 8.5, running from 7 to 9.5. OpenCritic's larger sample sits around 90.

The spread is the interesting part, because nobody in it disagrees about what the game is. They disagree about whether it is navigable.

Here is why that lands on the art team. Take the map away from a first-person game and the navigation work does not disappear, it relocates. Where am I, have I been here, which way is forward — every one of those questions moves from the UI layer into geometry, materials and light placement. That is expensive in a way no trailer shows. A corridor that only has to look good can be instanced. A corridor that has to be distinguishable from the other eleven corridors needs unique dressing, a different stone, a broken thing on the floor you will remember a half hour later.

The low fidelity helps, which surprised me. Chunky geometry and small textures mean a landmark resolves as one shape instead of as detail that dissolves at distance. Fog closes the sightline so you are never choosing between twenty similar doorways at once. The same restriction that makes it look like 1996 is what makes a mental map possible.

The sevens and the nine-point-fives are measuring the same thing from different chairs: the length of the window in which the game is simply confusing. Reviewers who came out the other side wrote about a world they knew.

Worth studying if you build levels.
