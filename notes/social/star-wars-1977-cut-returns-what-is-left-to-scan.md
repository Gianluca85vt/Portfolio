Disney has dated the restored 1977 theatrical cut of Star Wars for 19 February 2027, as the centrepiece of a year of 50th anniversary releases. Lucasfilm has not said which film elements it is scanning, and for anyone who works with image pipelines that is the only detail that matters.

A restoration is a chain of decisions and the first one cannot be undone: you choose a physical element, you scan it, and everything downstream is bounded by that choice. The camera negative is one generation and the best information there is. Separation masters sit a generation down, soft by comparison, and do not fade the way colour dye layers do, because they are black-and-white silver records of each colour channel. Interpositives and internegatives fade, and fade unevenly. A release print is furthest out, and is also the only element anyone ever graded for an audience. 4K describes the scanner. The element describes the information.

Star Wars is a strange candidate for this, for reasons that have nothing to do with 1997. Every effects shot in it is an optical composite, re-photographed element by element on an optical printer, so those shots sit several generations below the dialogue shot that cuts next to them. ILM knew what that would cost, which is why the effects plates were shot on VistaVision — eight perforations, film running horizontally, roughly twice the negative area. You spend that at the start because the pipeline eats it later. Render at 2x and downsample, forty years early.

So the film has two textures and the cut moves between them constantly. That unevenness is not damage.

Which is where a 2026 restoration gets interesting. Denoise, grain management and learned upscaling are now routine, because distributors ask for clean masters. Point that at five generations of optical printer grain and it will even it out, and the result will be more consistent than anything a 1977 audience saw.

Whatever ships in February becomes the original Star Wars for everyone who watches it after February.
