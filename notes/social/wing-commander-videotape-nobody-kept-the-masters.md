## LinkedIn

EA restored seven of its classic PC games this week by pulling the cutscenes out of its own videotape archive. A physical tape library, in 2026, because that was the surviving copy at a quality anybody would ship.

The next day SNEG released Pool of Radiance: Ruins of Myth Drannor with no source code, no tools and no documentation. They worked backwards out of the retail discs. They also had to clear rights with three separate companies to do it.

Then on Sunday one of BioWare's co-founders put the cost of buying Mass Effect off EA and finishing it at over two hundred million dollars.

Three games came back, three different routes, and not one of them ran through a project file.

Everything that makes a game reopenable fifteen years later is a technical artist's job. Version-pinned tools. Bakes that reproduce on a machine you have never seen. Source files a stranger can open. Naming nobody has to decode. All of it is the first budget line struck when a schedule compresses, because none of it shows up in a build review. You cannot screenshot a working pipeline.

Now put that next to Capcom's half-year. 36.86 million games sold, 32.18 million of them catalogue. Eighty-seven percent. Seven units in eight the company shipped in six months were games it had already finished.

So the old work is where the money is, and the condition the old work is kept in belongs to nobody.

Most of that two hundred million buys re-establishing tools, documenting what was undocumented, rebuilding a content chain, and relearning by excavation what fifty people knew by Tuesday and took with them when the badges went off. Dispersal is the expensive part. Dispersal gets decided on a spreadsheet in an afternoon.

Film already ran this experiment. It burned its own negatives and then spent fifty years paying to undo it.

Archive the project. The build is what you sold once. The project is the only thing that lets you sell it again.
