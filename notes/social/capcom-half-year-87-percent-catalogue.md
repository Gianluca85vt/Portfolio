## LinkedIn

Capcom sold 36.86 million games in the six months to 30 September. 32.18
million of them were titles from previous years.

That is 87 percent of the volume coming out of the back catalogue, and about
one unit in eight going to something the company launched in the period.
Pragmata is at 2.73 million since April, Onimusha: Way of the Sword at 1.78
million after two months, Resident Evil Requiem at 8.51 million lifetime.
Good numbers, all three. The half was paid for by the other 32.18 million.

I keep coming back to what that ratio asks of an art department. An asset that still sells in year five has to
survive a platform it was never authored for, a storefront re-release, a
resolution tier that did not exist at ship, and an engine version written by
people who have since left the company. In practice: source files a stranger
can open, shader graphs with fallbacks that degrade instead of failing,
texture density that holds when someone doubles the output resolution, naming
conventions nobody has to reverse-engineer.

None of that work appears in marketing. All of it decides whether a title can
be re-sold without a rebuild.

It also explains the engine strategy better than the release slate does.
Capcom put three structurally unalike games out of one fiscal year off RE
Engine — a sci-fi shooter in a single station, a sword game in a wrecked
Kyoto, a survival horror. One pipeline, re-dressed three times. And the REX
Project rebuild reads differently against a half where 87 percent of volume is
catalogue: an engine that can carry fifteen years of shipped games forward is
worth more than one that makes the next game prettier.

One detail I liked: sales of older Monster Hunter games rose after Capcom gave
Monster Hunter Wilds: Ascendance a 2027 window. Announcing a game that does
not exist yet sold copies of games from 2018. A catalogue in good shape is a
marketing asset rather than a shelf.
