## LinkedIn

A first-time studio chose to rotoscope a survival horror game. I keep turning that decision over, because it is the least scalable animation technique there is.

Silver Pines, from Wych Elm and published by Team17, is out on 8 October. Hand-painted 2.5D, animated by drawing over recorded footage frame by frame, with a mostly fixed camera. Reviews landed two days early and the five verdicts I could verify and attribute average 9, running from 8 to 10.

Rotoscope does not scale. You cannot retime it cheaply. You cannot lift a cycle into a new context without it reading wrong. Every additional action is another shoot and another drawing pass. For a debut team with a debut budget, that is a strange place to put the money.

What it buys is weight. A rotoscoped figure sits on the ground properly, because the timing came off a body that was obeying gravity. In a genre where the horror is mostly about how slowly and how heavily you move down a corridor, that is load-bearing, not decoration.

The locked painted camera pays twice over. Detail per frame goes up, and a whole category of work disappears: no off-angle geometry to clean, no lighting rig that has to survive a player spinning inside it, no LODs. A fixed shot is a promise the player will never look behind them, and a small studio can spend that promise.

Where I would watch carefully is the structure. Survival horror wants you lost and rationed. A Metroidvania wants you confidently re-reading a map you have already beaten. Holding both means the map stays legible while the world stays hostile, and that seam is where a horror game went wrong a week ago.

The eights and the tens here disagree about combat feel and pacing. Those are the parts of a first game that get rebuilt last.
