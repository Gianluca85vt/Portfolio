You cannot spot a generated illustration on a promo banner at the size a promo banner gets reviewed. You can ask for the layered source file, and that catches it every time.

Yuriten, the yuri manga exhibition run by Lilium, has cancelled the free kickoff event for its tenth anniversary. On 10 October the company admitted that generative AI had made the announcement visual — and, going further than most of the coverage noted, the event's physical decorations as well. The free event planned for 17 and 18 October outside Hareza Ikebukuro is off. The exhibition itself, and the Atelier Lilium café, are still going ahead.

The organisers pointed at an outside contractor. I believe that, and I do not think it absolves anyone, because it is the most ordinary way generated art enters a pipeline nobody wanted it in. A banner goes out to a design shop with a brief, a pixel size and a deadline. Nobody at the client types a prompt. The shop is asked for a flat JPEG by Friday, and a flat JPEG by Friday is precisely what comes back.

The fix is not vigilance. Vigilance loses to 11pm. The fix is a line in the delivery spec: ship the layered source alongside the export. A drawn illustration arrives as forty layers with a sketch somebody forgot to hide and two experiments nobody deleted — it has a history because the hours left marks. A generated image has a prompt and a raster. Ask for the working file and there is nothing to send.

Most of us already demand the PSD for completely unrelated reasons, because we want to move the logo later. Turning that into an acceptance condition costs one sentence. Games largely got here first, and only because licensors wrote it into contracts rather than trusting good intentions.

The decorations are the detail I keep returning to. Artists were being asked to hang original drawings inside a room dressed with generated imagery.

The teaser is what everyone saw. The admission went further: generative AI was also used in the event's decorations. Artists were to hang original drawings inside a room dressed with generated imagery.

Nio Nakatani, who drew Bloom Into You and exhibited at past Yuriten shows, called it confounding and disappointing. Yukiko and Yayoi Osawa objected too. These are the people the exhibition exists to showcase.

The organisers pointed at an outside contractor. That is the ordinary route. A banner goes out with a brief, a pixel size and a deadline, nobody at the client types a prompt, and a flat JPEG by Friday is exactly what a brief for a flat JPEG by Friday returns.

You cannot catch it by eye. Nobody zooms in on the hands of a key visual at 11pm. You catch it by asking for the layered source: a drawn file has a hidden sketch layer and forty names like hair_2_FIX. A generated one has a prompt and a raster.
