Alessandro Carloni gave Cartoon Brew the line that explains the entire art department on The Cat in the Hat: the team didn't want to use CG to just "simulate" an illustration. That is close to the hardest brief you can hand a CG pipeline, and the reviews suggest they met it.

Six scored verdicts average 5.8 out of 10, from 5 to 7. Inside that range the critics are not arguing about the animation at all. Next Best Picture calls it excellent and the story overstuffed. Roger Ebert's review says the visuals are stunning and the film overstimulating. Silver Screen Riot rates the style a boon, then says the film does nothing with it beyond overpopulating the frame.

What Warner Bros. Pictures Animation actually attempted on its first feature is worth looking at properly. The look was split three ways: the Cat's Seuss World, bold and graphic and minimal; the human world, pitched as an elevated version of classic 3D; and the box worlds, marrying a graphic 2D treatment to real 3D depth. Read that as a schedule and it means three shader libraries, three lighting setups with three sets of rules for how light behaves, and a decision for every asset that crosses a boundary about which version of itself it is wearing.

Then there is the translation itself. Production designer Aidan Sugano drew the film by hand and those designs became Maya geometry, hatch marks and negative space and arches intact. A hatch mark is a mark made at a fixed size on a fixed page. Put it on a surface in a 3D scene and somebody has to choose: world space, where it scales with the object and dissolves into noise at distance, or screen space, where it holds its weight and slides across the geometry whenever the camera moves. Neither is free.

Seuss architecture has no load path anywhere in it. Fine on paper. Awkward the instant you add a camera that can orbit.

Four territories, two years, Autodesk Flow tracking every version from boards to final comp — to arrive at a 6, with the blame landing on the script. The pipeline did its job.
