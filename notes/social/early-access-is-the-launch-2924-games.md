## LinkedIn

Four games in five make their money on the way into Early Access, not on the way out.

That is GameDiscoverCo's number, and it reframes a planning assumption most art teams are still working to. Only 21% of games leaving Early Access earned more in their first thirty days after 1.0 than they did in their first thirty days of Early Access. The 1.0 badge is a milestone for the team. It is rarely a commercial event.

The volume is up alongside it. SteamDB counted 2,924 Early Access launches on Steam in 2026 as of mid-October, up 18% on 2025 and 60% on 2020, with two months still to run. As a share of everything Valve ships, Early Access went 10.4% in 2024, 11.3% in 2025, 13.7% this year. Roughly one in seven releases.

Here is what that does to a schedule. The old arrangement let you ship rough to a forgiving audience and spend two years making it look like the thing in your head, with the real marketing beat waiting at 1.0. Invert the earnings and you invert the schedule: the store capsule, the first trailer and the first ten hours have to carry a finished-looking game before most of the game exists.

The part that gets underestimated is the quality bar. Day-one assets define what every later asset has to match, and they get authored when the team's understanding of its own game is at its thinnest. You can see the join in a lot of these builds — a first biome with real material work in it, then a second and third built to a budget somebody calculated in year one.

Iron Gate's Richard Svensson puts player tolerance for an Early Access build at about 20% below a full release. Twenty per cent is a rounding error, not a different category of expectation.

So the teams getting this right tend to look cheap on purpose: a small, repeatable asset vocabulary that three years of content can actually match. That is arithmetic, not compromise.
