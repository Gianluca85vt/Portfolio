Hands are where 2D animation budgets go to die, and CompTown has just announced a show that cannot hide them.

The TV anime of Kuzushiro's Amayo no Tsuki — The Moon on a Rainy Night, serialised at Kodansha — put out its first promotional video this week, with a 2027 premiere window. The lead, Kanon Oikawa, is hard of hearing, which turns the usual hand problem into a far more specific one.

A wave is a gesture: draw it loosely, hold a cel, smear the in-between, nobody is harmed. A sign is not a gesture. Four things carry meaning: the handshape, its orientation, where it sits relative to the body, and the path it travels. Get the shape wrong and you have drawn a different word. Get the path wrong and you have drawn a grammatical error, because in Japanese Sign Language the motion is doing work that spoken Japanese does with particles.

So every economy an animation director normally reaches for is off the table. No held cels, because the movement is the sentence. No smeared in-betweens, because the in-between is a phoneme. No cutting to a reaction shot, because cutting away from the middle of a sign is the animated equivalent of muting a line and leaving the subtitle up.

The thing I am watching for is a credit. Neither the December 2025 staff announcement nor this week's addition lists 手話監修 — sign-language supervision. It may just be unannounced. But it sits upstream of every drawing: an animator who does not sign cannot invent a correct sign, and reference has to be shot before anyone keys a scene. Naoko Yamada has described having that guidance on A Silent Voice from pre-production through post, which is why the signing there reads as accurate rather than decorative.

Both escape routes are expensive. Rotoscope guarantees correct handshapes by refusing to invent them, and looks like rotoscope. 3D hands on a rig promise reuse, and Studio Orange's record says the corrections that make CG read as anime claw it straight back, worst of all where the detail is finest.
