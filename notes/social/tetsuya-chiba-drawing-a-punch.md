## LinkedIn

Tetsuya Chiba died on 27 October, aged 87. He drew Ashita no Joe, and he solved
a problem that every animator and every technical artist still works on.

A punch in a still image has almost nothing in it. The instant of contact is
two bodies touching with no displacement — nothing has moved yet. Film covers
that with motion blur, a shaking camera, a sound cut. A manga page has ink that
does not move and a reader whose eye travels at a speed you only partly set.

Chiba's answer was to stop drawing the contact. Open one of his fights and the
largest panel is usually the frame before — heel screwing into the canvas, hips
rotated ahead of the shoulder, fist still loaded — or the frame after, with the
head already gone and the arm past its target. Anticipation and follow-through,
reached from a drawing desk rather than from an animation textbook, because
those are the two poses that survive being still.

He used layout as timing, too. A full-width panel is a long beat, because your
eye has further to travel across it. Four narrow panels stacked are fast.
Nobody gives a mangaka a frame rate, so he authored the reading speed into the
geometry of the page — the job a dope sheet does for a scene, and much harder
to revise.

The part I keep thinking about is that he let the figure deteriorate. Our whole
discipline runs the other way: approve the model sheet or the mesh, then
protect it, because every shot has to match. Damage arrives as a decal pass or
a swapped texture. Chiba moved the proportions instead. Joe's skull thickens
over five years of weekly serialisation, weight-cutting hollows him out, and
volume one and volume twenty are not drawings of the same person. No model
sheet could have reconciled them. He paid that bill on purpose.

In 2024 the Japanese government gave him the Order of Culture, reported as the
first to go to a manga artist.
