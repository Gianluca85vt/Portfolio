## LinkedIn

Danny Boyle's Ink contains thirty seconds of generative AI, and the way those
seconds were spent is a better guide to where this tooling sits in a pipeline
than any vendor demo I have seen this year.

Some of it went on Margaret Thatcher's face. The rest went on mice running
through the Fleet Street newsroom.

The mice are the interesting half. Shot conventionally, vermin crossing a floor
is a creature job: a groom, a fur sim, four feet making contact with a surface,
lighting matched to whatever the set was lit with that day. Days of work from
somebody good, for half a second at the edge of frame that no audience will ever
recall. Boyle says the tool is "very good with animals". He is right, and the
reason matters: nobody carries a precise internal model of how a mouse crosses a
particular floor. Everybody carries one for a human face. Face recognition is
the most over-trained visual system we have, which is why the uncanny valley is
very nearly a face-only phenomenon.

So the mice are the safe seconds and Thatcher is the exposed ones.

On the Thatcher sequence the reporting splits, and the split is the whole
question for anyone who works in post. Variety and The Hollywood Reporter
describe an impersonator who played the entire scene, with the likeness carried
on top — which means there is a performance underneath the comp: the body, the
timing, the weight shift. IndieWire and Vanity Fair describe generated motion
applied to still photographs instead, which has no performance underneath it at
all. The movement is invented, and nobody in the room chose it.

That difference also decides whether Boyle's own defence holds. He says he was
careful nobody was replaced, and that you pay actors if you replace them. A paid
impersonator is that bargain honoured. An animated photograph replaced nobody
because nobody was ever going to be hired.

Worth noting what this costs now. Rogue One rebuilt Peter Cushing in 2016 with a
facility, a bid and months of iteration. Ink is a mid-budget StudioCanal period
drama and it got a Thatcher anyway. The question moves from "can we afford this"
to "do we want it", which is a far less protective question. Budget has done a
lot of quiet ethical work in this industry simply by making some shots
impossible.
