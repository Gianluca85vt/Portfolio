## LinkedIn

Unity has bought Synty Studios, which means Unity now owns the POLYGON packs — somewhere between 60,000 and 70,000 low-poly assets, depending on which outlet you read. Terms undisclosed. Most of the artists are joining Unity full time and carrying on making packs.

The destination is Unity Spark, the browser-based prompt-driven editor Unity announced with Google on 7 October. It goes into closed beta later this year, and Synty's library goes in with it.

Ask a machine for a street and the street is the easy half. The building on the corner has to sit next to the car parked outside it at the same scale, under the same shading model, with the same idea of how bright the world is. Generated meshes do not arrive with that agreement. Neither does an asset store full of brilliant individual work from four hundred vendors at four hundred different texel densities.

Synty's range is close to unique at that size because the packs were drawn to be mixed. A metre is a metre across the whole library. Pivots sit where a modeller would put them. One shared palette atlas per pack keeps the draw calls down and turns recolouring a set into a texture swap. Pull a dungeon kit and a 1960s city kit into one scene and the join does not show. For a tool that places art rather than inventing it, that consistency is the product.

Unity has said the reassuring things: existing packs, licences and workflows unchanged, pricing unchanged "at this time", free assets and the Synty Pass carrying on, packs still available for Unreal and Godot. Epic said comparable things when it sold ArtStation and Sketchfab to KitBash in August.

The question nobody has answered: what a licence means once those assets live inside a generative editor. If Spark drops a POLYGON building into a scene because a prompt asked for a street, whose licence is doing the work? Unity says the terms have not changed. Those terms were written for a person dragging a prefab into a viewport.
