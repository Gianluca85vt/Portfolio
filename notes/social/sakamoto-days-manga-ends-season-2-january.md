Sakamoto Days finished on Monday. Chapter 284, Weekly Shonen Jump issue 52, six
years after Yuto Suzuki started it in November 2020.

The timing is the part I keep turning over. Netflix has season 2 of the TMS
Entertainment adaptation arriving in January 2027. Volume 30 of the manga is
dated 4 January 2027 and volume 31, the last one, 30 April. So the anime comes
back after the story has ended and before the story exists as a finished set of
books.

That gap matters more than it sounds to anyone who has built assets from
reference. Collected editions are where an author goes back and fixes the pages
that got away from them on deadline — a hand, a background that was three lines
and a prayer, lettering sitting over something it shouldn't. If you are drawing
model sheets and layouts, the tankōbon is the cleaner document and the one the
author approved twice.

The deeper problem with this title is staging. Suzuki's fights are built on
continuous space. A brawl in a convenience store uses the convenience store: the
shelf you can see over, the aisle that dead-ends, the freezer door that opens
into somebody's face. He describes the room, then lets people wreck it, and the
choreography reads because the geography never cheats.

In animation that puts the cost in the least protected department. Character
animation is the line item everyone defends. Backgrounds and layout are where a
television schedule makes its saving — and here the background *is* the
choreography. A fight in a fully described room needs that room consistent
across forty cuts, with the knocked-over shelf still knocked over. Set-dressing
continuity, except nobody built the set.

The cheap exit is a speed-line field or a colour wash with the bodies carrying
it. It works, and it throws away what made the page good. Season 1 did both at
different moments, and you can watch the schedule decide which.
