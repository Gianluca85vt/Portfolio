## LinkedIn

Laika's Wildwood opens on Friday and runs 131 minutes, which makes it the
longest stop-motion feature ever released. That number is a production
schedule before it is a creative choice, and it is the only spec on the sheet
I really care about.

Laika's stated throughput target is about 4.3 seconds of finished animation
per animator per week. On Kubo and the Two Strings the reported reality was
nearer three, with 27 animators shooting their own scenes in parallel. Run
those rates against 7,860 seconds of film — my arithmetic, not the studio's —
and Wildwood sits somewhere between 1,830 and 2,620 animator-weeks of
shooting. Kubo, at 101 minutes, works out between 1,400 and 2,000. The extra
half hour of runtime cost four to six hundred animator-weeks that the earlier
film never had to find.

Here is the part that matters to anyone who works in a pipeline. Everything we
have built for twenty years, in games and in VFX, exists to make a late change
cheap. Non-destructive modifiers, caches you can swap, fracture setups that
inherit motion a director already approved. The whole value proposition is that
somebody can change their mind in week 90.

Stop-motion has none of that. A shot is a physical event that already happened,
on a stage that has since been struck. Trim two minutes in the edit and you
delete roughly 40 animator-weeks of a named person's work. So all the story
risk moves upstream: the boards get frozen before anything moves, and pacing
has to be solved on paper, years out, by people looking at panels instead of
footage.

Which is exactly where the reviews have landed. Craft praise is unanimous —
Empire calls the animation astonishing, Variety calls it a spectacular formal
achievement. The split is over pacing and familiarity, with IndieWire and the
Associated Press both arguing the technology outruns the story. A CG feature
screens its reels, decides act two drags, and re-boards it. Twice, if needed.
Laika got one go at that call, in pre-production, and then spent two years
committing to it.
