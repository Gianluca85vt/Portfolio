## LinkedIn

Twelve reviews of Castlevania: Belmont's Curse, and the spread is four points
wide — 6 to 10, averaging 8.5. What I find interesting is not the number. It is
that every single reviewer praises the same things and criticises the same
thing.

The praise is interchangeable: the colour, the whip, the music, the
frame-by-frame feel of movement. The complaints are all structural. Fast travel.
A frustrating opening. A late game that goes soft. Rock Paper Shotgun's verdict
is "a fantastic action game, a less than stellar Metroidvania".

There is a production reason for that distribution, and it is worth
understanding if you build levels for a living.

Evil Empire spent years on Dead Cells, with Motion Twin. A roguelike assembles
its levels at run time from a library of hand-made rooms. That pipeline makes a
team exceptional at local craft — readability, silhouette clarity at speed,
colour separation, animation weight — because every room has to work in any
order it gets dealt.

What it never asks anyone to do is author one map.

A Metroidvania map is a single artefact. Position carries meaning: this door
matters because of what sits three rooms behind it, this ability gates that
corridor because it reframes a space you already walked. You cannot iterate on
that in pieces. You build the whole thing, walk it end to end, find the middle
sags, rebuild the whole thing. Slow, unglamorous, and it is the difference
between a Metroidvania people replay for fifteen years and one they finish once.

So the studio arrived with a surplus of exactly the skills that make rooms sing
and a deficit of the one that makes a map sing. The reviews describe that
distribution almost perfectly.

The wider pattern: look and feel are now reliably achievable. Mature tools,
abundant reference, a good art team with a clear brief. Structure is not,
because structure is one person's judgement about an artefact too large to hold
in your head, and no tool helps with that. The craft floor rose. The design
ceiling did not move.
