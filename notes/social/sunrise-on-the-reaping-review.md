Six outlets gave The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping a number I could read. The mean is 5.5. The range is 2 to 8.

A six-point spread on a ten-point scale means the critics answered different questions. Peter Bradshaw's one star in the Guardian says the film has no reason to exist beyond its fanbase. The eights are answering a different question, about whether a competently told story is worth two and a half hours. Those arguments never touch.

What interests me is the thing both sides half-agree on. Reviewers call this the best-looking entry in the series and, a paragraph later, visually flat. Both are accurate, and the reason is something anyone who has worked on a long-running property will recognise: a design language is an asset that depreciates.

Panem was fixed in 2012. Brutalist Capitol, soot-and-timber District 12, pastel grotesques in the broadcast circus. Every film since has built inside that grammar, so by the sixth picture a new set is judged against five earlier versions of itself before the gaffer has made a single decision. Jo Willems is on his fourth Panem film and shot this one in IMAX digital. Uli Hanisch, of Babylon Berlin and Dark, did the production design, and one review places the tribute training centre inside Berlin's International Congress Centre. That is a strong crew getting diminishing credit for work that is genuinely better than the last time.

IMAX cuts both ways here. The taller frame gives the arena vertical room the 2012 crop never had. It also resolves every seam in the dressing, across a 158-minute cut that several reviewers call slow out of the gate. A large-format frame does not flatter an unhurried scene.

The departments that build things came out of this better than the department that decides what gets built.
