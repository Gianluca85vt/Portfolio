Nine reviews of the Ocarina of Time remake, converted to a ten-point scale, average 9.5 — and the whole spread is one point. Nobody under 9. That kind of agreement is rare enough to be worth asking about.

Reviews lifted 4 November, a day before the Switch 2 release. Metacritic settled at 96 across seventy-odd critics, OpenCritic at 95 across 69, and the recurring reservation is the same one everywhere: it is a very safe remake.

Here is the production reading I keep coming back to. Bandai Namco Studios says it produced characters, terrain, animations, cutscenes and UI for this game. Monolith Soft lists game design, programming and graphics. That is an entire art department commissioned outside the building.

Now think about what you have to write down to commission that. A spec precise enough that another company can build Hyrule Field and have it come back looking like Hyrule Field is, by necessity, a description of a game that already exists. Fog falloff, grass density, how far Link's silhouette reads against a hill at dusk. Every element you might want reconsidered is an element you cannot specify, because nobody knows yet what it should be.

So the brief describes 1998, closely. The conservatism the reviews flag is in the procurement before an artist opens a file.

What they did change is more work than it sounds. Forbes reports a free camera and a real jump button. The 1998 jump was contextual because the N64 pad had one stick and four C buttons, and every ledge in the game was authored knowing the player could not miss it. Add a jump button and somebody walks the entire geometry again checking what is reachable now. Same for the camera: Z-targeting existed partly because there was no second stick, and dungeon rooms were composed as single-angle stages.

Faces got scoped rather than solved. Voice acting in key cutscenes, not throughout. Two dozen shots rigged properly beats six hundred done cheaply, and on characters whose 1998 faces were a texture on a flat plane, that is the right call.

The changes they did make are real work. A free camera and a jump button on a game designed around their absence. The 1998 jump was contextual because the N64 pad had one stick — every ledge was authored knowing you could not miss.

Z-targeting existed partly because there was no second stick to look around with. Dungeon rooms were composed as single-angle stages. Hand the player free look and you are re-checking sightlines in every one of them.

Faces were scoped, not solved: voiced key cutscenes rather than the whole script. On characters whose 1998 faces were a texture on a flat plane, twenty-odd shots rigged properly beats six hundred done cheaply.
