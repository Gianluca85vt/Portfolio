## LinkedIn

Unity announced Spark yesterday, and the interesting part is not the prompt box. It is the asset layer.

Spark builds playable 3D games in the browser from typed prompts, and it assembles them out of packages from the Unity Asset Store. Real meshes, real materials, real prefabs, made by people who are credited for them. In Unity's demo, a prompt asking for a cape returned a shelf of capes somebody had modelled rather than a diffusion guess at one.

After two years of generative game tooling trained on whatever could be scraped, that is the first version of this idea a working artist can argue with on the merits. The output is inspectable. It is fixable. It came from a shop with terms and names attached.

Then you look for the rate, and it is not there.

Unity has not finalised the commercial model. The Asset Store has paid publishers 70% of a sale for fifteen years, and a sale is a legible event — one person, one package, one transaction. A prompt that pulls eleven packages from four publishers and takes two meshes from each is a usage event, and usage events need a rate, a counting method, and somebody auditing the count. None of those three is public.

The launch restrictions tell you Unity knows it. No selling what you make, and no export to the desktop editor. Both keep the assets inside Unity's walls while the terms get written, which is the only version of this product that could ship this year.

Three things an Asset Store publisher should watch: whether the rate is per generation, per session, per published game or a subscription share; whether you can opt out without delisting entirely; and whether Spark usage counts toward the store rankings that decide whether a pack gets seen.

Closed beta later this year. The rate sheet will say more about the next five years of selling 3D work than the demo does.
