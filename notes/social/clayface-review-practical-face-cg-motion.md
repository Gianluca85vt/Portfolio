## LinkedIn

Clayface is out, and the reviews have accidentally written a very good lesson in
effects budgeting.

Nine scored verdicts average 6, across a range from 4 to 8. But read the bodies
rather than the numbers and a pattern appears in nearly every one: the
prosthetic makeup is excellent, and the digital work layered on top of it is a
step down. James Watkins set the production rule himself before release — Matt
Hagen's face is a practical appliance whenever it is not moving.

That rule is sound economics before it is sound taste. A prosthetic sitting
still is the cheapest realistic thing you can put in front of a lens. It is a
real object in the real key light, picking up the set's bounce for free, with
the grain landing on it at the same time as everything else. Nothing to
match-move. Nothing to roto. No shader approximating sub-surface scatter,
because the scatter is physical.

Motion is where it gets expensive. Clay has mass. A melting face needs
viscosity, surface tension, self-collision so the sheet running down a jaw does
not pass through the one beneath it, and contact with collars, floors and other
actors' hands. Each of those is a simulation with a slow iteration loop, and a
note from the director on Tuesday is a new cache on Friday. Then it has to be
dropped into a plate that already contains a lit rubber appliance, where the
lighting is baked in and cannot be adjusted — so the digital half comes all the
way to the practical half rather than meeting it halfway.

And the hardest shots in the film are the handoffs. The dozen frames where the
appliance becomes the simulation. Get those wrong and you have shown the
audience where the seam is, and they will look for it for the rest of the
runtime.

Reported budget sits somewhere between $30m and $45m depending on the outlet.
For a creature film built on transformation, the transformation is the line
item you cannot underfund.
